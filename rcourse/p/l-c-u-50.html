Lorazepam dependence: agreeing a safe reduction

Wednesday 09:30, outpatient clinic · Community mental health clinic room

The task

Mrs Natalie Osei, aged 38, has just moved to the area and joined a new GP practice. The GP was worried to find she has been prescribed lorazepam 4 mg a day for anxiety for about four years, and she gets shaky if a dose is delayed. She is anxious about any change and does not want to come off it. The GP has referred her to the local outpatient clinic for advice on managing the medicine. Please agree a management plan with her, including how to reduce and stop the benzodiazepine safely.

Your job: In about ten minutes, take a brief, focused history of her lorazepam use and dependence, screen mood, risk, alcohol, other drugs and physical health, explain why reducing helps her, and agree a written, gradual community taper she feels in control of.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Promise no sudden stop and nothing without her agreement
  2. Record why it started, current dose and timing, and 2+ dependence features
  3. Screen mood, suicidal thoughts, alcohol, other sedatives or opioids, fits
  4. Name dependence without blame; give 2+ reasons to reduce that fit her life
  5. Agree a written taper: diazepam 40 mg, step size, interval, never stop suddenly
  6. Community, not hospital; she controls the pace
  7. Single prescriber, weekly scripts, CBT referral, review in 2 weeks, urgent-help advice
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Natalie Osei?
NatalieYes. Is this about my tablets?
YouIt is, Natalie. Your new GP asked us for advice. Nothing will change today without us agreeing it together.
NatalieOkay. I'm already nervous.
YouThat's understandable. Could you tell me how the lorazepam started and how it's going now? We've got about ten minutes.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, no sudden stop, joint decision)
  • 2. Lorazepam story (work crisis, 4 years, 1 mg four times a day, other treatments tried)
  • 3. Signs of dependence (shakes when late, clock-watching, tolerance, extra doses, side effects)
  • 4. Mood, risk and substances (anxiety now, suicidal thoughts, alcohol, other sedatives, fits, driving)
  • 5. Why reduce (no blame, tiredness, memory, accidents, tolerance)
  • 6. How the taper works (switch to diazepam 40 mg, small cuts every 2 to 4 weeks, months)
  • 7. Her fears (anxiety returning, busy life, no hospital, she sets the pace)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, safety points, GP letter, weekly scripts, CBT, review)

Reasons that fit her life

Does less for anxiety; tiredness, poor focus; accident risk with driving or wine

It has helped, but your body has got used to it, so it does less for anxiety while making you tired and foggy.
It also raises the risk of accidents, especially with driving or alcohol.
Why can't I just stay on it, then? It works.

The switch to diazepam

Over about a week, one dose at a time; 1 mg lorazepam = 10 mg diazepam; 40 mg total

First we'd switch you to diazepam, a longer-acting cousin, over about a week. One lorazepam dose at a time.
It's the same strength, just steadier, so fewer ups and downs between doses.
How would it even work?
Isn't that more tablets?

The cuts, at her pace

Cut about a tenth every 2 to 4 weeks; smaller steps near the end; pause if needed; months

Then we cut by small amounts every two to four weeks, at your pace. It usually takes several months.
You choose when each step happens. If a step feels too much, we pause.

Law and safety net

Prescribing and driving rules

Schedule 4 CDs; single prescriber; weekly scripts; don't drive if drowsy, especially switch week; consent

Never stop suddenly, avoid alcohol while we reduce, and don't drive if you feel drowsy, especially in the switch week.
Your GP will be the only prescriber, with weekly scripts.
Okay.
Watch out
