Explaining to a 10-year-old why her mum is on a psychiatric ward

Lesson 12 of 14 · about 15 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

Saturday 14:30 · Family room off the adult acute ward

The task

You are the CT1 covering the ward at the weekend. Mrs Sarah Bennett, 38, was admitted on Thursday under Section 2 with her first manic episode with psychotic features. Her daughter Lily, 10, saw the police and paramedics take her mum from home after an argument about bedtime. Her father, Mr Tom Bennett, 40, asks you to explain to Lily 'what's wrong with Mum', because Lily thinks it is her fault. Yesterday Mrs Bennett agreed that Lily should be told 'gently and honestly'. Explain Mum's illness to Lily in words a 10-year-old understands, with her father present; tell her clearly it is not her fault; answer her questions honestly; and agree visiting, contact and Young Carers support.

Your job: Explain Mum's illness to Lily in words a 10-year-old understands, with her father present; tell her clearly it is not her fault; answer her questions honestly; and agree visiting, contact and Young Carers support.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Mum's consent and what Dad has told Lily before you start
  2. Ask Lily what she thinks happened before you explain
  3. Explain the illness in 3 simple messages; Lily repeats it back
  4. Say 'It is not your fault' clearly, at least twice, naming the row
  5. Answer die / police / prison / catch it honestly; give no return date
  6. Agree a phone call, a planned family-room visit and a card
  7. Young Carers referral, school (her say), carer's assessment for Dad; Think Family recorded
The exact words

Your opening

YouHi Lily. I'm one of the doctors looking after your mum. Hello, Mr Bennett. Is it OK if I sit down here with you both?
LilyOK.
YouI like your drawing. Is that your cat?
LilyIt's Biscuit. He's ginger.
YouLily, Dad told me you've got some questions about Mum. Before I explain, what do you think has happened?
LilyMum went mad and the police took her. Because I shouted at her.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Prepare (Mum's consent, Dad's view, family room, paper and pens)
  • 2. Start with her (name, Biscuit, 'what do you think has happened?')
  • 3. Simple explanation (illness in her brain, fast thoughts, the TV, medicine and rest)
  • 4. Not her fault (say it, name the row, repeat it, Mum asked about her)
  • 5. Her questions (die, police, prison, catching it)
  • 6. Coming home (honest 'I don't know yet', no date)
  • 7. Contact (phone call, family-room visit, card, what Mum may be like)
  • 8. Support and law (Young Carers, school, carer's assessment, consent, visits, Think Family)
  • 9. Teach-back and close (what she'd tell Biscuit, next contact, notes)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Prepare before you sit down

Mum's consent in the notes; what Dad has told Lily; family room booked; paper and pens; nurse in charge aware

Mr Bennett, before we go in, what has Lily been told so far?
What words do you use at home for Mum being unwell?
Is there anything you'd rather I didn't say?
Dad: Just that Mum's poorly. She keeps asking if it's because of the row.
Dad: She's been helping her mum a lot since the summer.
B
Get it done

Start with her story

Something she likes, then her version first; name the fear

Before I explain, what do you think has happened to Mum?
What did you see that night?
That sounds like a really scary night.
She was talking really fast and she wouldn't stop. And she said the TV was talking to her.
Mum went mad and the police took her.
C
Get it done

Three simple messages

Illness in her brain; thoughts too fast and believing things that aren't true; medicine and rest help

Mum has an illness in her brain. You can't see it, like a cold or a cut, but it changes how she thinks and feels.
Right now her thoughts are going super fast, like a car without brakes, and she hasn't been able to sleep.
Sometimes the illness makes her believe things that aren't true, like the TV talking to her. The doctors and nurses are giving her medicine to help her sleep and slow her thoughts down.
Like what?
Is she going to die?
D
Get it done

Lift the guilt, then say it again

'Not your fault' plainly; name the row; repeat; Mum asked about her; all feelings allowed

Lily, I want you to hear this really clearly. It is not your fault.
Nothing you said or did made Mum ill. Not the shouting, not the fight about bedtime. This illness happens by itself.
Lots of children say that when they're cross. Mum knows you love her. She asked about you this morning.
But it's my fault. We had a fight about bedtime and I said I hated her.
Dad: She did, Lily. She asked if you'd fed Biscuit.
(cries) Really?
E
Get it done

Short, true answers to her questions

Will she die? Why the police? Is it prison? Can I catch it?

No. This illness doesn't make people die. Doctors know a lot about it, and most people get better with medicine and rest.
She's not in trouble. When someone is very poorly and doesn't realise it, the police sometimes help get them to hospital safely.
This is a hospital, not a prison. She has her own bedroom, and there are nurses here all day and all night.
You can't catch it, like you can't catch a broken leg. Hugging Mum is completely safe.
Why did the police come? Is she in trouble?
Is she in prison here?
Can I catch it?
F
Get it done

Coming home: honest about not knowing

'I don't know yet'; usually a few weeks; what has to happen first; no promised date

I don't know yet, and I don't want to make a promise I can't keep.
Usually it's a few weeks. When she's sleeping and her thoughts slow down, we'll start talking about home.
I know that feels like forever. It's OK to be sad about that.
When's she coming home?
Weeks?
G
Get it done

Contact: call, visit, card

Nurse-supported phone call tomorrow; planned ~30-min family-room visit early next week; a card; prepare her for how Mum may seem

If Mum's calm tomorrow, you could speak to her on the phone, with a nurse helping.
There's a family room just outside the ward. The nurses will plan a visit with Dad, maybe early next week. It might be about half an hour.
She might still talk fast, or seem sleepy from her medicine. That's the illness, not Mum changing. If it feels too much, you can stop the visit, and that's fine.
Can I see her?
Will she be weird?
Can I bring her a card?
H
Get it done

Young Carers, school and Dad

Young Carers referral with Dad's consent; Lily chooses who at school is told; ask Dad how he is; carer's assessment

Mr Bennett, there's a young carers service locally, with clubs and someone to talk to. Would you like me to refer Lily?
It might help to tell Lily's teacher on Monday. Lily, how would you feel about that?
Mr Bennett, how are you doing with all this?
Dad: She does a lot for her mum. Yes, please.
Only Mrs Shah. Not the whole class.
Dad: Honestly, I'm shattered. Work, Lily, the hospital.
I
Law and safety net

Consent: your licence and your limit

GMC Confidentiality (2017): share what Mum agreed to; explain illness and plan, not every detail

Mum told us yesterday she wants you to know what's happening, gently and honestly.
Mum asked about you, and wants you to know that too.
Dad: Is it OK for you to tell her all this?
J
Law and safety net

Child visits, planned and safe

MHA Code of Practice (2015), ch 11: family room away from the ward, planned in advance, in the child's best interests

The nurses will plan the visit with Dad, in the family room, not on the ward.
A nurse will be close by, so if anything feels too much, someone can help straight away.
Dad: Can't she just come onto the ward?
K
Law and safety net

Carers have rights, not favours

Children and Families Act 2014: young carer's needs assessment; Care Act 2014: carer's assessment for Dad

Lily can have her own young carer's assessment from the council.
You can ask the council for a carer's assessment for yourself too.
Dad: I didn't know I could ask for anything.
L
Law and safety net

Think Family, and who to call

Working Together (2023): record children in the household and their needs; safeguarding lead only if concerns; ring the ward any time

If more questions pop up later, Dad can ask the nurses, or you can ask me when you visit.
You can ring the ward any time, day or night. The nurses will call you tonight about tomorrow's phone call.
Dad: Who do I call if she gets upset tonight?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Euphemisms ('Mum's tired')
Name it simply and match what she saw: fast thoughts, the TV
Promising a date for home
'I don't know yet'; usual range; what has to happen first
Talking to Dad over Lily's head
Speak to Lily; check in with Dad separately
Skipping the guilt
Address it directly, name the row, say it twice
'You won't get it'
'You can't catch it, like a broken leg'
Finish

Close it well

YouLily, can you tell me what you'd say to Biscuit about Mum?
LilyMum's brain is poorly and her thoughts are too fast. It's not my fault. I can't catch it. She's in hospital, not prison. I can phone her tomorrow and make her a card.
YouThat's brilliant. You've got it all. If more questions pop up, Dad can ask the nurses, or you can ask me when you visit.
YouMr Bennett, the nurses will ring you tonight about tomorrow's call, and I'll ask our carers' lead to contact you. Any questions for me?
YouThank you both. Bye, Lily. Say hi to Biscuit.
