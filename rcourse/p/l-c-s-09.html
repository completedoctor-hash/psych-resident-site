Adult Psychiatry · Psychosis

Eliciting first-rank symptoms in early psychosis

Lesson 2 of 90 · about 15 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

Your place is saved.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Thursday 10:00 · Early intervention in psychosis (EIP) team, community clinic

The task

Kieran Abara is a 25-year-old man seen by the Early Intervention in Psychosis team. Over the past year he has become increasingly withdrawn, left his university course and begun saying strange things to his mother, who prompted the referral. Please speak to Kieran with the aim of eliciting Schneider's first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia, asking sensitively about each one. You do not need to complete a risk assessment or explore other diagnoses.

Your job: Elicit each first-rank symptom in turn with precise questions, separating true symptoms from look-alikes, and summarise what you found.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Let him describe his experiences in his own words first
  2. Ask precisely about thought echo, voices discussing him in the third person, and running commentary
  3. Test insertion, withdrawal and broadcast for outside origin, not just a 'yes'
  4. Ask about made feelings, impulses and acts, and somatic passivity
  5. Ask how it all became clear: delusional perception
  6. Summarise present and absent symptoms, check accuracy, explain next steps
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Kieran Abara?
KieranYeah.
YouThanks for coming, Kieran. Your mum was worried you've been having a hard time this year.
KieranShe worries too much.
YouMaybe. I'd like to hear about it from you. Has anything unusual been happening?
KieranMy mind's being taken over. By the network. They know everything about me.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, rapport, open question about the past year)
  • 2. Permission (warn that questions may sound unusual; no arguing)
  • 3. Auditory hallucinations (voices about him, running commentary, thought echo)
  • 4. Thought alienation (insertion, withdrawal, broadcast; test outside origin)
  • 5. Passivity experiences (made feelings, impulses, acts; somatic passivity)
  • 6. Delusional perception (normal perception, then sudden new meaning)
  • 7. Summary and next steps (reflect back, physical screen, team plan)
  • 8. Consent and close (mum with consent, safety net, contact numbers)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Open wide and listen for bridges

His own words first; note 'talk', 'get into my head', 'taken over'

Can you tell me what's been happening, in your words?
That sounds really frightening. Tell me more about how the network does that.
Take your time.
My mind's being taken over. By the network. They know everything about me.
They talk. They get into my head. I can't explain it properly.
B
Get it done

Ask permission for unusual questions

Normalise the questions; no arguing, no collusion

Some of my questions may sound unusual, but I ask everyone them. Is that okay?
I don't think that. These experiences are real and frightening for you, and I want to understand them.
Why are you asking such strange questions?
You think I'm mad, don't you? Everyone does.
C
Get it done

Voices discussing him

Third person, talking or arguing about him; not second-person voices

You said they talk. Do you ever hear voices when nobody's there?
Do they talk to you directly, or about you to each other?
Do they call you he or him, or do they say you?
Yeah. From the vent in my room. Two men.
About me. 'He's useless.' 'He'll never finish anything.' They argue about me.
He. Always he.
D
Get it done

Running commentary

Voices describe his actions as he does them

Do they ever describe what you're doing, while you're doing it?
Can you give me an example of what they say?
All the time. 'He's getting up.' 'He's going to the kitchen now.'
E
Get it done

Thought echo

Own thoughts heard aloud, just after or as he thinks them

Do you ever hear your own thoughts spoken aloud, just after you think them?
Is it out loud, like a voice, or more in your mind?
Sometimes. I think something and a second later they say it. Like an echo.
F
Get it done

Thought insertion

Alien thoughts placed in his mind; not his own unwanted thoughts

Do thoughts come into your head that don't feel like yours, as if someone else put them there?
How do you know they aren't your own thoughts, ones you just don't like?
Can you give me an example of a thought like that?
Yeah. The network's thoughts.
Because they're not how I think. They're put there. I can feel them arrive.
G
Get it done

Thought withdrawal

Thoughts removed by an outside force; not ordinary losing track

Do your thoughts ever stop suddenly, as if something took them out of your head?
Is that like losing your train of thought, or does something take them?
Mid-sentence. They pull them out. Then there's nothing.
Something takes them. The network.
H
Get it done

Thought broadcast

Thoughts leave his mind so others know them; not face-reading or guessing

Do you feel your thoughts leave your head, so other people know them without you saying anything?
Could they be guessing from your face or from what you've said?
They leak through the walls. The neighbours know what I'm thinking.
No. They know the thoughts themselves. I don't say anything.
I
Get it done

Passivity and somatic passivity

Made feelings, impulses, acts; bodily sensations imposed from outside

Do you ever feel your emotions are put there by someone else?
Do you get urges to do things that feel put into you?
Does anything outside you control what your body does?
Any sensations in your body that you feel are being done to you?
No. My feelings are mine. I'm just scared.
My right hand. It taps by itself. They're working it. Like I'm a puppet.
A buzzing in my stomach. It's their machines.
J
Get it done

Delusional perception

Normal perception, then sudden unconnected delusional meaning

When did it first become clear to you what was happening? Was there a particular moment?
What happened when you saw it?
Was there anything about the van itself that told you that, like writing on it?
Back in March. I saw a red van parked outside Mum's.
I just knew. Straight away. I'd been chosen for their experiment.
No. It was just a van. But I knew. Completely.
K
Get it done

Summarise and plan the next steps

Reflect present and absent; handover; bloods, weight, BP, ECG, urine drug screen; consultant

Can I check I've got it right? You hear two men discussing you, describing what you do and echoing your thoughts.
Thoughts feel put in, taken out and known by the neighbours. Your hand is moved, there's a buzzing put in your stomach, your feelings are your own, and the red van made it clear. Have I missed anything?
We'd like to do some blood tests, a heart tracing and a urine test, including for drugs. Would that be okay?
No. That's it. It's a relief someone gets it.
A urine test? For drugs? I don't do drugs.
L
Law and safety net

Consent, confidentiality and safety net

Adult: ask before sharing with mum; EIP 2-week standard; MHA not needed; duty number, NHS 111 option 2

Your mum's worried. Is it okay if I tell her what we talked about today? How much would you like her to know?
We see many people with experiences like these, and most are helped at home.
If things get worse before then, ring our duty number on this card, or NHS 111 and choose option 2.
If I tell you everything, will you lock me up?
She can know I'm getting help. Not all the details.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Firing a closed checklist from minute one
Open question first; use his words as bridges
Accepting 'yes' as a symptom
Ask for an example and test outside origin every time
Counting second-person voices as first rank
Ask 'he or you?'; need third person discussing or commentary
Confusing delusional perception
Normal perception first, then sudden unrelated meaning; not a delusion following a voice
Arguing with his beliefs
Validate the experience; neither argue nor collude
Finish

Close it well

YouThank you, Kieran. I'm glad you felt able to tell me all this.
YouYour care coordinator will see you next to understand more about your life and how you're coping. Then we'll plan help together.
YouIf things get worse before then, ring our duty number on this card, or NHS 111 and choose option 2.
KieranOkay. Thanks.
