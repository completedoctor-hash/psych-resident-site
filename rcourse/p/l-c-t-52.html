Refeeding syndrome in a 16-year-old: advising the CAMHS nurse

Wednesday 11:30, CAMHS duty doctor · CAMHS community team base, phone call back to a nurse

The task

Ms Rhiannon Bell is a newly qualified CAMHS community nurse who has been visiting Amira Hughes, a 16-year-old girl with anorexia nervosa, at home for the past two weeks to monitor her weight and eating. Last week Amira's mother pressed her to eat large amounts and she put on 3 kg in seven days. Since then her hands and feet have become swollen and she is finding it hard to swallow. The GP checked bloods a week ago, which showed only a mildly reduced phosphate. Rhiannon has asked to talk with you because she is concerned. History taking is not needed. Please address her concerns, explain what is causing these symptoms and discuss management.

Your job: Without taking a history, address the nurse's concerns, explain refeeding syndrome and the cause of the swelling and swallowing difficulty, arrange same-day medical assessment, and discuss treatment and prevention.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Thank her; confirm the facts given; ask ONE safety question; no history
  2. Name refeeding syndrome; explain the insulin shift in one picture
  3. Explain the swelling (salt and water, heart strain) and the swallowing (low phosphate, weak muscles)
  4. Arrange same-day paediatric assessment: ECG, bloods, salts replaced, thiamine
  5. Feeding does not stop; dietitian sets a steady plan; no diuretic
  6. Support Mum: not her fault; prevention steps
  7. Agree who does what, 999 signs, documentation, consultant told
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Rhiannon, Amira's nurse?
NurseYes, thanks for calling back.
YouI understand Amira has gained weight quickly and you've noticed some new symptoms. That's worth worrying about.
NurseHer hands and feet are puffy and she says swallowing is hard.
YouThank you. Let me check a few facts, then explain what I think is happening and what we do today.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, role, thank her, agree urgency)
  • 2. Clarify the picture (3 kg in a week, swelling, swallowing, low phosphate; one safety question)
  • 3. What this is (refeeding syndrome; insulin drives phosphate, potassium, magnesium into cells)
  • 4. Why the symptoms (fluid retention and heart strain; weak swallowing muscles)
  • 5. Immediate action (same-day paediatric review, ECG, bloods, likely admission)
  • 6. Medical treatment (replace salts, thiamine, daily bloods, steady feeding, no diuretic)
  • 7. Family, prevention and consent (not Mum's fault, MEED steps, Amira's capacity at 16)
  • 8. Hand over and close (who calls whom, 999 signs, consultant told, notes, debrief)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Back the nurse first

She did the right thing; this can develop in days; what matters is today

You've done exactly the right thing by noticing and calling.
This can develop within days.
What matters now is that she's seen today, and you've made that happen.
Did I miss something? I've been seeing her every few days.
Her mum was so relieved she was eating.
B
Get it done

Confirm the facts, one safety question

No history: confirm 3 kg in 7 days, swelling, swallowing, low phosphate; ask about collapse signs now

She gained three kilos in a week after her mum increased her meals, and her phosphate was a little low a week ago?
One important question. Right now, is she breathless, faint, confused, or getting chest pain or palpitations?
That's right.
Not this morning. She was tired and weak, sitting down most of the time.
C
Get it done

Name it: refeeding syndrome, in one picture

Starved body; food arrives fast; insulin surges; phosphate, potassium, magnesium pulled into cells; thiamine used up

I think this is refeeding syndrome.
Her body has been running on fat and muscle. When lots of food arrived, insulin surged and drove phosphate, potassium and magnesium into the cells.
It also burns through her thiamine, one of the B vitamins.
So the low phosphate was the start of it.
So her cells are grabbing everything.
D
Get it done

Why her hands and feet are swollen

Insulin makes kidneys hold salt and water; starved heart muscle is thin; extra fluid strains it; no diuretic

Insulin also tells the kidneys to keep salt and water, so fluid pools in her hands and feet.
Months of starving have weakened her heart muscle, and that extra fluid loads it.
Swelling may be the first sign of strain.
Why are her hands and feet puffy?
I didn't realise it was about her heart.
E
Get it done

Why swallowing is hard

Low phosphate weakens all muscles, including swallowing and breathing; choking risk; nothing pushed by mouth until assessed

Every muscle uses phosphate for fuel. Short of it, they tire and weaken.
That includes the muscles for swallowing and for breathing.
For now nobody should push food on her. She could choke.
Why would she struggle to swallow?
Is it dangerous?
F
Get it done

Seen today, by name

You ring the paediatric registrar now; assessment unit this afternoon; likely admission; not GP bloods tomorrow

I'll ring the paediatric registrar as soon as we finish.
I'll ask for her to be seen this afternoon on the assessment unit.
She'll probably stay in for a few days.
Can't she just have a blood test at the GP tomorrow?
OK. So it's today.
G
Get it done

What the ward will do

ECG; BP and pulse lying and standing; phosphate, K, Mg, Ca, glucose, U&E, LFT, FBC daily; replace salts; thiamine, vitamin B compound strong, multivitamin

They'll do an ECG, blood pressure and pulse.
Bloods for phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium, plus glucose, kidney and liver tests and a blood count, repeated daily.
Anything low gets topped up, and she'll get thiamine and vitamins.
OK. What will they do?
So daily bloods.
H
Get it done

Feeding does not stop

Stopping food is dangerous too; dietitian sets a steady plan; bloods guide the pace; 0.5 to 1 kg a week

No, and that's really important. Stopping food is dangerous too.
The dietitian will set a steady plan and build it up, with the bloods guiding the pace.
The target is around half a kilo to one kilo a week.
So should her mum stop giving her food?
So she doesn't stop eating. Just slower and watched.
I
Get it done

Mum and prevention

Not her fault; most of the gain is fluid; MEED steps next time: bloods first, thiamine, gradual increase, close monitoring

She acted out of love, and lots of parents do the same.
Most of that weight was fluid. The ward will show her a safe pace.
Next time intake goes up, bloods first, thiamine started, a gradual build-up and frequent bloods.
Her mum will be devastated. She thought this meant Amira was getting better.
Could we have stopped this happening?
J
Law and safety net

Consent and capacity at sixteen

Presumed capacity (MCA 2005); seek Amira's consent; tell her you are sharing with Mum; if she refuses, consultants assess capacity; MHA possible

Amira's sixteen, so we ask her consent for admission and tests.
We'll share this with her mum in her interests, and tell Amira we're doing so.
If she refuses, the consultants will assess her capacity.
What if Amira won't go?
Can't her mum just sign for her?
K
Law and safety net

Hand over: who does what, and 999

Nurse rings Mum; nothing pushed by mouth; 999 signs; you tell consultant and paeds; record time and advice; supervisor debrief

Can you tell me back what you'll do after this call?
If she gets chest pain, palpitations, faints, becomes breathless or confused, or has a fit, call 999.
I'll tell her consultant and the paediatric team. Please write up our call and debrief with your supervisor today.
Ring her mum now, assessment unit this afternoon, nothing pushed by mouth.
Should I tell anyone else?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Taking a full eating disorder history
Confirm the given facts, one safety question, then explain
Explaining well but no assessment today
Name the place, the time and who rings
'Stop feeding her'
Feeding continues at a steady, monitored pace
Blaming the mother or the nurse
Both acted in good faith; focus on today
Suggesting a diuretic for the swelling
Correct salts, slow the pace; diuretics drop potassium
