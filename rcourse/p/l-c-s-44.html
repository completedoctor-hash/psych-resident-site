Explaining morbid jealousy to his partner, and her safety

Friday 17:30 · A&E, liaison psychiatry interview room

The task

Craig Lennox, 32, was treated in A&E for minor cuts after a cycling accident when staff became worried about him. You have assessed him: he is convinced his partner, Fiona Dalgleish, is having an affair with a work colleague, based on a photo from a party five months ago. He checks her phone, logs her movements, has followed the man home and has bought a flick-knife, saying it is for protection. Records show a past stalking and assault conviction against an ex-partner, a two-year stay in a medium-secure unit for morbid jealousy, and that he stopped antipsychotic treatment. He refuses community follow-up and informal admission. He has consented to you talking to Fiona, who has arrived. Please explain the likely diagnosis and discuss a management plan with Ms Dalgleish.

Your job: Explain to Hannah the likely diagnosis of morbid jealousy and the management plan, including admission, and talk honestly about her safety, including whether to separate.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Craig's consent; hear what Fiona has noticed first
  2. Ask kindly about an affair, threats, the knife, children and living together
  3. Explain morbid jealousy plainly: cause, treatment, honest outlook
  4. Link the past assault to today's knife and following: risk to her and the colleague
  5. Explain why admission now, under the MHA if he refuses, and why tablets at home won't do
  6. Discuss separation honestly; safety plan: somewhere tonight, helpline, IDVA, DASH, follow-up call
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Fiona Dalgleish?
FionaYes. Is Craig hurt? Nobody's told me anything.
YouHis cuts are minor. Craig has agreed I can talk to you about him, and some of it is difficult.
FionaOkay. What's going on?
YouCan I first ask what you've noticed at home lately?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, injuries minor, Craig's consent, her view first)
  • 2. What she has seen (phone checks, logging times, the photo, the knife)
  • 3. Hard questions (any affair, threats, harm, children, living together)
  • 4. The diagnosis (morbid jealousy, a delusion, a type of psychosis, not her fault)
  • 5. The past and the risk (stalking and assault, secure unit, stopped treatment, knife, the colleague)
  • 6. Admission and treatment (why hospital now, MHA, appeal, outlook)
  • 7. Her safety (separation, controlling behaviour, DASH, IDVA, MARAC, Clare's Law)
  • 8. Summary and close (somewhere tonight, helpline, teach-back, call within 48 hours)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Her view before your explanation

What she's noticed, how she sees it; 'a bit jealous'; she may like it

It must be confusing to arrive and find us involved.
Can I start by asking what you've noticed about Craig over the last few months?
"I don't understand. He fell off his bike. Why is a psychiatrist involved?"
"He's a bit jealous sometimes, but I like that. It makes me feel special."
B
Get it done

The checking, the logging, the photo

Reads her messages, writes down her times, the party photo, how she answered him

Has he checked your phone, or kept track of when you come home?
Did he ever mention a photo from a party?
"He reads my messages. He writes down when I get in. He's just being protective."
"Yeah, months ago. Some guy from work was next to me. I told him to stop being paranoid."
C
Get it done

The hard questions, asked kindly

Any affair (no judgement), threats or harm, knew of knife, children, living together

I have to ask this, and I'm not judging at all: has anything happened between you and that colleague?
Has Craig ever threatened you or hurt you? And did you know he had a knife?
Do you live together? Do any children live with you, or come to visit?
"No! I hardly know him."
"Never touched me. I saw a knife last week. He said it's for protection."
"No children. Just us."
D
Get it done

Name the illness, in plain words

Morbid jealousy; a fixed false belief; a type of psychosis; not caused by her; teach-back

Craig has an illness called morbid jealousy. His brain has fixed on a false belief that you're being unfaithful.
Normal jealousy comes and goes. This is different: from one photo, he's become certain, and nothing you say changes it.
If you were explaining this to your sister, what would you say?
"Jealousy's an illness?"
"Psychosis? He's not mad. What causes it?"
"He really believes I'm cheating, even though I'm not, and it's an illness."
E
Get it done

Link the past to today

Same belief before: stalking, assault, conviction, 2 years medium secure; stopped antipsychotic

Craig's agreed I can share something difficult. He had this same belief about a previous partner. He stalked and assaulted her, was convicted, and spent two years in a secure hospital.
I can see that's a huge shock, and I'm sorry you're hearing it this way. Take a moment.
What worries me is that then, like now, it was the same belief, and it ended in violence.
"(stunned) What? He never told me."
"That was years ago. He must have been stressed."
"(tearful) I thought I knew him."
F
Law and safety net

The knife, the colleague, and the police

Followed colleague home, flick knife: risk to Fiona and colleague; police told; GMC disclosure; record why

He's also followed your colleague home and bought a flick knife. So I'm worried for you and for your colleague.
We have a duty to protect him, so we've told the police.
It's to prevent harm before it happens, not to punish Craig.
"The police? He hasn't done anything!"
"(tearful) I just... I thought I knew him."
G
Law and safety net

Why hospital, and why now

Tablets take weeks and he refuses; home is where the belief lives; MHA assessment, s2 or s3; right to appeal

I wish it were that simple. The tablets take weeks to work, he says he won't take them, and he's carrying a knife. Home isn't safe yet.
He's refused, so a specialist social worker and two doctors are assessing him under the Mental Health Act.
If they agree, he'll be admitted, and he has the right to appeal.
"Can't you just give him his tablets back and let me take him home? I'll watch him."
"So you're sectioning him. That'll destroy him."
H
Get it done

Outlook and separation, honestly

Many improve; it can return, even with a new partner; long-term follow-up; her choice; not alone with him; don't argue the belief

Many people improve with medicine and later talking therapy. But it can come back, so he'll need follow-up for a long time.
That's your decision, and I can hear how much you love him. Some couples do well once treatment works. If it doesn't, living apart is sometimes the only safe option.
For now, while he's this unwell, please don't be alone with him, and don't argue with the belief.
"Will he get better?"
"Should I leave him? I love him."
I
Law and safety net

Controlling behaviour, and the support she is owed

Checking and tracking can be coercive control (SCA 2015 s76; DA Act 2021); DASH; IDVA; MARAC if high; Clare's Law

The reading of your messages and tracking you is part of the illness, but it's also controlling, and you deserve support.
Can I go through a short safety checklist with you, and refer you to an adviser who supports partners, called an IDVA?
You can also ask the police about a partner's past, under a scheme called Clare's Law.
"He reads my messages every night. He says it's because he loves me."
"I suppose so. Yes."
J
Get it done

Tonight's safety plan, and who you ring next

Somewhere safe tonight; helpline 0808 2000 247; 999; told before any leave or discharge; old forensic team; ward call in 48 h

Is there somewhere safe you can stay tonight?
Here's the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, 0808 2000 247, open 24 hours. If you're ever in danger, ring 999.
The ward will tell you before any leave or discharge, and ring you within two days.
"My sister's. She's ten minutes away."
"Okay. I hadn't seen it like that."
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Agreeing to home on tablets
Tablets take weeks, he refuses them, he has a knife
Avoiding the past assault
Share it with his consent; link it to today
Calling her naive or ordering her to leave
Her decision; be honest about when apart is safest
Forgetting the colleague
Name the risk to him; police told; record why
No safety plan
Somewhere tonight, helpline, DASH, IDVA, ward call
Finish

Close it well

YouWhat will you take from today?
FionaIt's an illness, he believes it completely. He hurt someone before. Hospital's safest. I stay at my sister's and call that number if I need to.
YouThat's exactly right. The ward will ring you within two days and invite you to a meeting. Any questions?
YouThank you for listening. It took a lot to hear all that tonight.
