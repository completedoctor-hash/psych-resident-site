ECT: will it damage my memory?

Thursday 11:00 · Older adults inpatient ward, interview room

The task

Mr Desmond Clarke is a 65-year-old retired bus driver admitted to an older adults ward with a severe depressive episode. He took fluoxetine 40 mg daily for six weeks and then venlafaxine 150 mg daily for six weeks, and appears to have taken both reliably, but he has not got better. He has high blood pressure controlled on ramipril and mild kidney impairment. His consultant has suggested electroconvulsive therapy. He knows very little about it and is worried it will harm his memory. Please give Mr Clarke clear information about ECT to help him decide whether he wants it, and explore his beliefs, worries and expectations. You do not need to assess his capacity or take his consent today.

Your job: Give Mr Clarke clear information about ECT to help him decide whether he wants it, and explore his beliefs, worries and expectations. Capacity and consent are not needed today.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask what he has heard, what scares him most, and what he hopes for
  2. Link 'why ECT' to his story: two adequate trials, not eating
  3. Describe the day: workup, fasting, anaesthetic, relaxant, waking, tea
  4. Give the course: twice weekly, usually 6 to 12, review after each
  5. Be honest on memory: common gaps, usual recovery, rare lasting loss, safeguards
  6. Raise blood pressure, ramipril and kidneys before he asks
  7. Leave the choice with him: can say no or stop, alternatives, time, family
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the ward psychiatrists. Are you Desmond Clarke?
DesYes. Des.
YouThank you, Des. Your consultant mentioned ECT, and I hear you're worried about your memory.
DesI am. I've heard terrible things.
YouThat's a fair worry. Could I hear what you've heard first, then explain it properly? We're not deciding anything today.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, consultant's suggestion, memory worry, no decision today)
  • 2. His ideas, fears and hopes (the film, the crossword, 'feel like me', feeling a burden)
  • 3. Why ECT now (two adequate trials, not eating, older adults respond well)
  • 4. What happens (workup, fasting, anaesthetic, relaxant, brief seizure, mouth guard, tea; course)
  • 5. Memory and side effects (headache, aches, confusion; memory gaps, recovery, safeguards)
  • 6. Physical health (blood pressure, ramipril, kidneys, ECG, anaesthetist)
  • 7. Choice and alternatives (his decision, can stop, lithium or other medicine, time)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflet, video, ECT suite visit, family meeting, consultant consent talk)

Why ECT, and why now

Two adequate trials failed; very unwell and not eating; ECT the most effective option in severe depression; older adults respond well; NICE NG222

You've had two proper courses of tablets and you're still very unwell and not eating.
Nothing we have works better for depression this severe.
Most people improve, often within a few weeks.

The treatment itself

Short anaesthetic, muscle relaxant, small current, brief controlled seizure about a minute, mouth guard, wake in minutes, tea

You have a short anaesthetic and a muscle relaxant, so you're asleep and your body barely moves. You feel nothing.

You wake within minutes, have a cup of tea, and come back to the ward.

Short-term side effects

Headache, aching muscles, muddled for an hour or so; paracetamol; serious complications very rare, similar to any short anaesthetic

Afterwards some people have a headache, aching muscles or feel muddled for an hour or so. Paracetamol helps.
Serious complications are very rare, about the same as any short anaesthetic.
Is that all?

Memory, honestly

Gaps for events around treatment common; usually improve over weeks to months; a few report lasting gaps; unilateral placement; memory tests before and during; depression itself impairs memory

Many people have gaps for events around the weeks of treatment. Those usually improve over weeks to months. A few people say some gaps last.
We'd usually start by placing the current on one side of the head, and test your memory at the start and as we go.
If it's affected, we adjust or stop.
And my memory? Be honest.
Would I still be me? Could I still do my crossword?
It is. I can't finish one at the moment.

During treatment your blood pressure rises briefly, and the anaesthetist manages that.
We'll check your kidney bloods first, and the anaesthetist will tell you whether to take your ramipril on treatment mornings.

Law and safety net

His choice, his rights, the alternatives

Informal: needs his valid consent, can withdraw any time; consent and capacity later on the trust ECT form; s58A, s62 emergency only; alternatives: lithium or other augmentation, slower; lithium with ramipril and CKD needs care

This is entirely your decision. You can say no, and if you say yes you can stop at any time, even after one treatment.
If you'd rather not, we'd look at adding lithium or another medicine to your venlafaxine. Those can work, but usually take longer.
You don't need to decide today.

Watch out

Traps to avoid

Lecturing before listening
Ask what he has heard and fears first
'ECT doesn't affect memory'
Gaps common, usually recover, a few lasting; explain safeguards
Jargon
Plain words: asleep, relaxant, one side of the head
Ignoring BP and kidneys
Raise them first: workup, anaesthetist, ramipril plan
Pressing for a yes today
Time, leaflet, video, family meeting, consultant consent talk
