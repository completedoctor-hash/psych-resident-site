Speaking up: the consultant is discharging a high-risk patient

Lesson 9 of 14 · about 13 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Wednesday 11:45 · Acute adult inpatient ward

The task

You are a CT2, 29, on the ward. Mr Paul Hughes, 47, was admitted informally 6 days ago after a planned overdose of 40 co-codamol tablets, found by a neighbour. Last month his wife left and he lost his job; he lives alone. On this morning's ward round he said he 'feels much better' and wants to go home. Dr Martin Fraser, 58, the consultant, agreed discharge this afternoon with GP follow-up, as the bed is needed. Afterwards the healthcare assistant tells you Mr Hughes gave his dog to his brother yesterday and said, 'I've sorted everything now.' Raise your concern with the consultant using a graded assertiveness approach, agree a safer plan, document it and escalate if needed.

Your job: Raise your concern with the consultant using a graded assertiveness approach, agree a safer plan, document it and escalate if needed.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Check the new information first-hand: who said what, when; see Mr Hughes yourself
  2. Ask Dr Fraser privately, with a time limit: 'two minutes about Mr Hughes'
  3. State the facts, your concern and a specific request (joint review at 14:00)
  4. Step up from question to clear challenge if brushed off; no accusation
  5. Agree interim safety: discharge paused, 15-minute obs, collateral from wife and brother
  6. If discharged later: HTT, written safety plan, means removed, follow-up within 72 h
  7. Document facts and outcome; name the escalation route (clinical director)
The exact words

Your opening

YouDr Fraser, have you got two minutes, somewhere private? It's about Mr Hughes's discharge.
Dr FraserMake it quick, I've a meeting at quarter past. What's up?
YouSince the ward round, I've learned something new that worries me.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Check the facts (HCA's exact words, see him yourself, tablets at home)
  • 2. Private moment (two minutes, away from the patient and team)
  • 3. Probe and alert (a question first, then the facts and your worry)
  • 4. Challenge (not comfortable, safety issue, joint review at two)
  • 5. Interim safety (pause discharge, 15-minute obs, collateral, holding powers)
  • 6. Safer discharge plan (home treatment, safety plan, means removed, 72-hour follow-up)
  • 7. Document and escalate (notes, clinical director, supervisor, Freedom to Speak Up)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Check the facts yourself first

HCA's exact words and time; see Mr Hughes; 'made my peace'; tablets at home; wife's information

Jo, can you tell me exactly what he said, and when?
Mr Hughes, I'm glad you feel more settled. When you say you've made your peace, what do you mean?
What are your plans for the weekend?
Jo: He gave the dog to his brother yesterday. He said, 'I've sorted everything now.' Quite calm.
Mr Hughes: I just want to get home. I've made my peace with things.
B
Get it done

Probe with a question

PACE step one: an open question that invites him to look again

Can I check something first? Did you get a chance to ask about his plans for the weekend?
Dr Fraser: Not in detail. He looked brighter.
C
Get it done

Alert: the facts and your worry

Dog given away, 'sorted everything', guarded, painkillers at home, lives alone, 40 co-codamol a week ago

After the ward round, Jo told me he gave his dog to his brother yesterday. And he said to her, 'I've sorted everything now.'
He denied any plans, but he was guarded about the weekend. His wife told the nurses there are painkillers at home. He lives alone, he's lost his job and his marriage, and he took forty co-codamol a week ago.
Dr Fraser: People rehome pets when they're unwell. He was bright as a button this morning. He's fine, don't worry.
D
Law and safety net

Your duty to speak up

GMC Good medical practice (2024); Raising and acting on concerns (2012): duty overrides loyalty; PIDA 1998

No. You saw him this morning, and you've far more experience than me. This came up after the ward round, so you didn't have it.
Dr Fraser: (pause) Are you saying I've got it wrong?
E
Get it done

Challenge: a clear statement and a request

'I'm not comfortable with him going home today'; name what the calm may mean; joint review at 14:00

I understand. But I'm not comfortable with him going home today. I think the calm, the dog and 'sorted everything' could mean he's made a decision, not that he's better.
Could we see him together before he goes? I'll fit around you.
Dr Fraser: We've been through all that. Three people are waiting for beds. The GP will see him.
Dr Fraser: (sighs) Sorry. It's been a hell of a week.
Dr Fraser: I'm in the meeting till half one. Two o'clock?
F
Get it done

Interim safety until two

Pause discharge paperwork; 15-minute obs (tell Ade); call wife and brother for collateral

Meanwhile, can I ask the nurse in charge to pause the discharge paperwork, and put him on fifteen-minute checks until we see him?
I'll also ring his wife and brother before two, with his agreement, to hear how he's been and what's at home.
Dr Fraser: Yes, fine. Tell Ade.
Mr Hughes: You said I could go. Now you're keeping me here?
G
Law and safety net

If he tries to leave

Informal: Section 5(4) nurse's holding power up to 6 h; Section 5(2) RC or nominated deputy up to 72 h; never block the door

He's informal. If he tries to leave and we're worried, the nurse can use Section 5(4) for up to six hours, and you or your nominated deputy can use 5(2) while an assessment is arranged.
Dr Fraser: What if he wants to go before then?
H
Get it done

If he goes, make it a safer discharge

Home treatment team seen before he leaves; written safety plan; brother clears tablets; follow-up within 72 h

And if we do decide he can go, I'd like home treatment to see him first, a written safety plan, his brother to clear the tablets, and follow-up within seventy-two hours.
Dr Fraser: That's sensible. You've thought about it.
I
Get it done

Document it: facts only

What Jo said and when; your review; the discussion; the agreed plan for 14:00

I'll write a note now: what Jo told me, my review, our discussion and the plan for two o'clock. Facts only.
No. I'll record that we discussed it and agreed the plan together. You can add your own review at two.
Dr Fraser: Fine. Do you need me to sign anything?
J
Law and safety net

If you still disagree: escalate, openly

Same day to clinical director or another senior consultant, telling him first; supervisor debrief; FTSU Guardian

If I was still very worried, I'd want to talk it through with Dr Shah as clinical director, and I'd tell you first.
Dr Fraser: And if at two I still think he's fit to go?
Dr Fraser: Fair enough. I'd rather be told than read it in a report.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Hinting: 'I just wondered...'
State the facts, 'I'm concerned', and a specific request with a time
Raising it in front of the patient or ward round
Ask for two minutes in private
Accusing him of being unsafe or bed-driven
Frame it as new information he didn't have
Dropping it after the first brush-off
Climb the ladder: probe, alert, challenge, escalate
Not writing it down
Facts, discussion and agreed plan in the notes now
Finish

Close it well

YouJust to recap. I'll ask Ade to pause discharge and start fifteen-minute checks now. I'll call his wife and brother by half one. We see him together at two. I'll document it all.
Dr FraserAnd home treatment?
YouI'll give them a heads-up now, so they can see him today if we do discharge him. Is that everything?
Dr FraserThat's everything. Go. You did it well. Next time, grab me straight away, even mid ward round.
