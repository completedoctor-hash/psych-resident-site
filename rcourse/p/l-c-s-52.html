Unsteady and confabulating: the cerebellar check before neurology

Thursday 10:00 · Community alcohol service clinic

The task

Mr Clifford Osei-Bonsu is a 60-year-old man on an older adult psychiatry ward with many years of heavy, dependent drinking. Over the past fortnight the nursing team have noticed that he is unsteady, has become more forgetful and seemed briefly confused on admission. The team are worried about Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome and want a clear record of his cerebellar signs before he sees the neurologist. He is calm, cooperative and sitting in the clinical room. Please carry out a physical assessment of his cerebellar function, explaining each step to him as you go.

Your job: Carry out a full cerebellar examination, adapting to his memory problems, then interpret the findings and decide what must happen today.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm identity, simple consent, chaperone offered, pain checked
  2. Look first: posture, stick, sitting balance, liver signs
  3. Gait, heel-to-toe and Romberg with someone ready to catch him
  4. Speech, eye movements and nystagmus
  5. Arms and legs on both sides: tone, rapid alternating movements, finger-nose, reflexes, heel-shin
  6. Demonstrate and repeat each instruction for his memory
  7. Interpret, and arrange same-day IV Pabrinex for possible Wernicke's
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Can I check your name, please?
CliffordClifford Osei-Bonsu.
YouThank you, Clifford. I'd like to check your balance and coordination, as you've been a little unsteady. It takes about ten minutes. Is that alright?
CliffordFine by me.
YouAny pain anywhere? And would you like a chaperone?
CliffordNo pain. I'm okay without.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, explain, consent, chaperone, pain, hand hygiene)
  • 2. General inspection (stick, posture, sitting balance, tremor, liver signs, alertness)
  • 3. Gait and Romberg (broad-based gait, heel-to-toe, Romberg, guard against falls)
  • 4. Speech (slurred or scanning; 'British constitution', 'baby hippopotamus')
  • 5. Eyes (nystagmus on held lateral gaze, ophthalmoplegia, double vision)
  • 6. Arms (tone, dysdiadochokinesia, finger-nose, rebound)
  • 7. Legs (tone, reflexes, heel-shin, sensation)
  • 8. Interpret and act (Wernicke's on Korsakoff's, IV Pabrinex today, bloods, falls)
  • 9. Capacity, chaperone, close (MCA, best interests, record, thank him)

Set up for a failing memory

One instruction at a time; demonstrate; repeat without blame; gel hands; spot confabulation

Look first, including sitting balance

Stick or frame, bruises from falls, tremor, liver stigmata, alertness; sit forward, arms folded: truncal ataxia

Could you sit forward, away from the back of the chair, with your arms folded? Like this.

Gait and heel-to-toe

Walk to the door and back beside him; wide base, short steps, veering; tandem walk; turning

Could you stand up? I'm right beside you. Walk to the door and back at your normal pace.
Now walk heel to toe, like on a tightrope. Watch me first.

Romberg, safely and correctly read

Feet together, eyes open first, then closed; two people ready to catch; unsteady eyes open = cerebellar; much worse eyes closed = sensory

Feet side by side, touching, hands by your sides, eyes open.
Now close your eyes for a few seconds. I'm right here and I'll catch you.
I don't like this. I'll go over.
Whoa.

Speech

Get it done

Eyes: nystagmus and eye movement weakness

Head still, follow finger; hold lateral gaze 30° a few seconds; nystagmus, double vision, lateral rectus weakness, saccades

Keep your head still; just your eyes follow my finger. Say if you see two of it.
Now look up, and down.
Goes double out to the side a bit.
G
Get it done

Arms, both sides

Tone; arms outstretched; dysdiadochokinesia; finger-nose for intention tremor and past-pointing; rebound

Let your arm go floppy; I'll move it at the elbow and wrist.
Watch me: pat one palm with the other hand, flipping front, back, front, back, quickly. You try.
Finger to your nose, then out to my finger, and keep going. Then swap hands.
What was I doing again?
Moving target, that.
H
Get it done

Legs, reflexes and sensation

Tone, knee and ankle jerks, heel-shin both sides; numb feet and absent ankle jerks suggest neuropathy

Let your legs dangle. I'll tap your knees and ankles with this hammer.
Lie back. Put this heel on the other knee and slide it down your shin, like this. Then the other side.
Feet are numb anyway. Like cotton wool.
Not very good, am I?

Get it done

Put it together

Truncal and gait ataxia, legs > arms (vermis pattern), plus neuropathy; new nystagmus + ataxia + confusion = possible Wernicke's on Korsakoff's

He has a wide-based ataxic gait, truncal ataxia, leg ataxia worse than arms, absent ankle jerks with numb feet, and new nystagmus.
With confabulation and recent confusion, I'm treating this as possible Wernicke's on top of Korsakoff's.
So it's not just his memory?

Get it done

Act today, not by letter

IV Pabrinex today (2-3 pairs TDS for 3-5 days); tell consultant, record times; bloods incl Mg, phosphate, B12, folate; MRI must not delay; falls, physio, OT; cognition once treated

I'm going to ring my consultant and the medical team now. He needs intravenous Pabrinex today.
Can we put him on falls precautions until physio has seen him?
Do we wait for the neurologist first?

Law and safety net

Capacity for the drip

MCA 2005: memory loss alone ≠ incapacity; assess the decision; if lacking, best interests with keyworker or family

Your balance and eyes tell me your body is short of a vitamin called thiamine. A drip today can stop things getting worse. Would you be willing?
Can you tell me in your words what the drip is for?
A drip? I've got things to do. But if it helps my legs...
A vitamin. For my legs.

Law and safety net

Chaperone and record

GMC 2024: offer chaperone for close-contact checks; record offer and his decline; separate falls helper for standing tests

You said you're okay without a chaperone. I'll still ask a nurse to stand by while you're on your feet, just for safety. Is that alright?
Fine, if it stops me falling.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Eyes closed with no one ready to catch
Stand close with a second person before Romberg
Long multi-step instructions
One step at a time; demonstrate; repeat
Testing one side only
Every limb test on both sides
Calling Romberg a cerebellar sign
Positive Romberg = lost position sense
Oral thiamine and a routine letter
IV Pabrinex today; tell consultant and medics
