'They think she's making it up': functional seizures, to her father

Tuesday 11:20 · Neuropsychiatry outpatient clinic, general hospital

The task

Aisha Mensah, a 26-year-old woman, has had seizures for a year. Neurology tests, including bloods, brain scans and repeated EEGs, have all been normal. Video-EEG telemetry showed events with eyes open, asymmetric movement of all limbs and preserved awareness during a normal EEG, strongly suggesting non-epileptic attacks. Neurology told her two weeks ago that the seizures were 'psychogenic' and referred her to the neuropsychiatry clinic. She has had mild anxiety and depression treated by her GP but never seen a psychiatrist. She refused to come today, feeling the referral means doctors think she is faking. Her father, Kofi Mensah, has come with her agreement to learn about the diagnosis and the plan. Please explain the diagnosis and address his concerns.

Your job: Explain functional (dissociative) seizures to her father without blame, address the 'faking' fear, answer his questions, and outline treatment, outlook and safety advice.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Aisha's consent and its scope
  2. Hear his understanding and worries; answer 'faking' early and plainly
  3. Explain functional seizures: real, involuntary, how telemetry proved it
  4. Ask about her mood, self-harm and stressors through him
  5. Explain AEDs won't help (no sudden stop), the therapy that does, the outlook
  6. Give first aid and when to call 999; mention driving
  7. Agree how to invite Aisha back
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the neuropsychiatry clinic. Are you Aisha's father, Mr Mensah?
Mr MensahYes, Kofi.
YouThank you for coming, Kofi. I understand Aisha has agreed for us to talk, but felt unable to come herself.
Mr MensahShe thinks you'll say she's putting it on.
YouThat's important to hear, and I want to be clear that we believe her seizures are real. Could you tell me what you understand so far?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Aisha's consent, her fear of being disbelieved)
  • 2. His understanding (what neurology said, 'psychogenic', worries)
  • 3. The attacks (frequency, triggers, injuries, impact)
  • 4. What the diagnosis means (functional, real, not faking, how we know)
  • 5. Why they happen (software not hardware, stress, loss)
  • 6. Treatment and outlook (AEDs tapered, therapy, many improve)
  • 7. Risk and safety (mood, self-harm, first aid, driving)
  • 8. Getting Aisha engaged (letter, video, no pressure)
  • 9. Summary and close (teach-back, neurosymptoms.org, review)

'Non-epileptic.' 'Psychogenic.' She looked it up and it basically says it's in her head. I've watched them. She's not faking.

How often, first one, triggers, injuries, ambulances, life

When was the first one, and what was going on?
Has she been hurt in one, or needed an ambulance?
Several a week now. The first was before a big job interview. Now they happen any time.

Name it, and show how we know

Functional or dissociative seizures; filmed attacks with normal brain waves

They're called functional, or dissociative, seizures. The brain is healthy, but the way it's working goes wrong for a while.
In hospital her usual attacks were filmed while her brain waves were recorded. They stayed normal throughout. That's how we can be sure.
Then why send her to a psychiatrist?
I didn't know that. Nobody explained it like that.

Why: software, not hardware

Computer analogy; blushing; stress; no blame

Think of a computer. The hardware's fine, which is why the scans were normal. But the software crashes.
Embarrassment makes us blush; nerves make the heart race. The body reacts without us choosing.
But all the tests were normal. How can there be anything wrong?
So what causes it?

Stressors and the loss, asked gently

Recent years, her mum's death, family talk, no blame

Has anything else been hard for Aisha in the last few years?
I'm so sorry. How has Aisha been since?
Losing her mum may be part of this picture. But nobody caused it.
Her mum died eighteen months ago. It was quick.
I keep thinking I should have done something.

Mood and safety, through him

GP-treated anxiety and low mood; sleep; self-harm; suicidal talk

How has her mood been? Sleep, worry, getting out?
Has she seemed low, or said anything about wishing she wasn't here, or harming herself?
Quieter. Anxious. Not sleeping.
No. Never anything like that. Just withdrawn.

The tablets: no sudden stop

AEDs don't help these; neurologist tapers slowly

The epilepsy medicine works on electrical seizures, so it won't help these.
Please don't stop it suddenly. Her neurologist will reduce it slowly and safely.
Will the epilepsy tablets stop them?
So she can just stop it?

Therapy and the outlook

About 12 sessions, warning signs, grounding; many improve

The main treatment is a talking therapy designed for these seizures, usually about twelve sessions.
She learns to spot warning signs and use grounding: slow breathing, feet on the floor, naming what she can see.
Many people improve a lot, and some stop having attacks altogether.
Then what does help?
Will she get better?

First aid, and when to call 999

Stay calm, protect, don't restrain, talk, time it

Stay calm, move things she could hit, don't hold her down or put anything in her mouth, and talk to her quietly. Time it.
Call 999 if she's injured, struggling to breathe, or it's very different from usual.
What do I do when she has one? I usually panic and call an ambulance.
Okay. That's actually a relief.

Law and safety net

Consent, words, and driving

Keep to the consent scope; plain non-stigmatising language; DVLA

Aisha has agreed we can talk about her diagnosis and treatment, so that's what I'll keep to.
She shouldn't drive for now. Her neurologist will advise her on what the DVLA needs.
She hasn't driven since this started.
K
Law and safety net

A way back for Aisha

Don't discharge for not attending; her choice of format; written information

What do you think would help Aisha come and talk to us? With you, or by video?
I'll write to her today, offering that, with a website called neurosymptoms.org.
Video, I think. She'd feel safer at home.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Saying 'psychogenic', 'pseudoseizures' or 'all in her head'
Say functional or dissociative seizures; real and involuntary
Implying she could stop them if she tried
Automatic shutdown, not a choice
Advising a sudden stop of AEDs
Neurologist tapers slowly
Skipping mood and risk
Ask through him; plan to ask her directly
No route back for Aisha
Letter, video option, written information
Finish

Close it well

YouSo I know I've been clear, how will you explain it to her tonight?
Mr MensahIt's real, she's not faking, it's the brain's software crashing. The tablets won't help, but therapy will. And she's not the only one.
YouThat's perfect. I'll write to her and copy her GP in, and we'll speak again in four weeks. Any other questions?
