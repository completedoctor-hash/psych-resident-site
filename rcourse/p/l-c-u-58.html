Barcode fears: belief or psychosis?

Tuesday 14:30, CMHT new assessment · Community mental health team clinic room

The task

Mr Neil Corrigan is a 45-year-old delivery driver referred by his GP to the community mental health team. When the GP was arranging some routine blood tests, Mr Corrigan said the barcode on the sample tube carried the 'mark of the beast' and was part of a worldwide plot. The GP wonders whether he has a psychotic illness. Mr Corrigan has agreed to come in to talk about his concerns. Please assess him to explore what his beliefs are, how firmly held, and whether he has a psychotic illness. You will then share your diagnostic impression.

Your job: In about ten minutes, explore Mr Corrigan's belief (where it came from, how firmly it is held, how it affects his life), screen for psychotic illness, and tell him your diagnostic impression.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask where the belief came from and whether his church and wife share it
  2. Test conviction once with a gentle alternative; record his exact reply
  3. Ask how often it is on his mind and what he avoids
  4. Screen voices, passivity, thought interference, reference, mood, substances
  5. Ask whether it has stopped care for him or his daughter; any confrontations
  6. State 'overvalued idea, not psychosis' with three reasons
  7. Agree a blood test workaround and a GP letter
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Neil Corrigan?
NeilYes. I don't see why I'm here.
YouThanks for coming, Neil. Your GP said you had concerns about the blood tubes. I'd really like to understand your view. It'll take about ten minutes.
NeilMost people laugh.
YouI'm sorry that's happened. I'm not here to laugh. Could you tell me how it started?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, respect his view, GP's referral, purpose)
  • 2. The belief (origin, when it began, faith links, who shares it)
  • 3. Fixity and reasoning (one gentle alternative, preoccupation, distress, actions)
  • 4. Psychotic screen (reference, voices, passivity, thought interference)
  • 5. Mood and background (mood, sleep, OCD features, substances, past and family history, work)
  • 6. Risk (his own care, his daughter's care, confrontations, harm)
  • 7. Diagnostic impression (overvalued idea, not psychosis, three reasons, differential)
  • 8. Summary and close (blood test workaround, GP letter, open door, teach-back)

Get it done

Give your impression in plain words

'No sign of mental illness'; three reasons; overvalued idea; differential

No, Neil. I see no sign of a mental illness. This is a firmly held belief that comes from your faith.
Here's why: it's shared by your church, you can consider other views, and you've none of the other experiences we see in psychosis, like voices.
I can see why that felt hurtful. Your GP wanted to be careful, and that's fair.
So do you think I'm mad?
Well. That's a relief. My GP sent me here for my religion.

Law and safety net

Blood tests, capacity and the GP letter

He may refuse with capacity; explain the test; nurse labels tube later; letter to GP

Can I explain what that blood test is checking for?
Would it help if the nurse labelled the tube after you'd left the room?
I'll write to your GP. Is that all right with you?
I'm not touching those tubes.
That I could live with.
Will this go on my record?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling a shared belief a delusion
Ask who else holds it; a culturally shared belief is not a delusion.
Arguing about Revelation
Offer one alternative linked to his words, then listen.
Skipping the psychotic screen
'I ask everyone these': reference, voices, passivity, thought interference.
Forgetting his children
Ask directly whether the belief has stopped their medical care.
A label with no plan
Agree the blood test workaround and the GP letter before he leaves.
Finish

Close it well

YouSo, Neil: no sign of mental illness, a firmly held belief from your faith. I'll write to your GP and suggest the nurse labels the tube after you've left.
YouIf the worry starts to take over your days, there's talking help through your GP. If anything new happens, like voices, your GP can send you straight back.
YouHow would you sum up, in your words, what we've agreed?
NeilNot ill. GP sorts the label. I come back if anything changes.
