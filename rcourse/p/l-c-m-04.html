Working with an interpreter: low mood in an Arabic-speaking woman

Tuesday 14:00 · Community mental health team clinic

The task

You are the CT2 in the CMHT. Mrs Rania Khalil, a 45-year-old woman from Syria who came to the UK four years ago, is referred by her GP with six months of low mood, poor sleep and weight loss. She speaks little English. A female Arabic interpreter, Ms Huda Saleh, has been booked in person. At reception, Mrs Khalil's husband says, 'We don't need the interpreter. Our son is sixteen and his English is perfect.' Brief the interpreter, assess Mrs Khalil's mood and risk speaking directly to her (seeing her without family), explain the plan and check she has understood it.

Your job: Brief the interpreter, assess Mrs Khalil's mood and risk speaking directly to her (seeing her without family), explain the plan and check she has understood it.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Decline the son as interpreter, kindly, with a reason; see her without family
  2. Brief Ms Saleh: first person, everything interpreted, cultural notes said openly, confidentiality, suicide
  3. Sit in a triangle; speak to Mrs Khalil, not 'ask her'; short sentences, no idioms
  4. Assess core depressive symptoms and explore what 'my heart is squeezed' means
  5. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts and plans, and safety at home, while she is alone
  6. Explain depression, sertraline and therapy with an interpreter; teach-back; written Arabic info
  7. Debrief the interpreter, book the next one now, record interpreter details
The exact words

Your opening

Mr KhalilWe don't need the interpreter. Our son is sixteen and his English is perfect.
YouThank you for offering, and I can see you both want to help. We always use a professional interpreter. It protects your son from hearing difficult things, and it lets your wife speak freely.
YouI'll see Mrs Khalil on her own first. Your son is welcome to join at the end, if she wishes.
Mr KhalilFine. If she agrees.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Decline the son, book it right (professional, female, dialect, double time, seen alone)
  • 2. Brief the interpreter (first person, everything, cultural notes, confidentiality, suicide)
  • 3. Room and introductions (triangle, speak to her, short chunks)
  • 4. Her story in her words (squeezed heart, mother's death, grief)
  • 5. Mood symptoms (sleep, appetite, energy, pleasure, guilt, trauma)
  • 6. Risk (suicide, faith, safety at home, abuse)
  • 7. Diagnosis and plan (depression not madness, sertraline, therapy, bloods)
  • 8. Teach-back and after (her words, Arabic leaflet, son at the end, interpreter debrief)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Law and safety net

Why not the son

NHS England interpreting guidance (2018): professionals; avoid family, never children; Equality Act 2010

Thank you for offering, but we always use a professional interpreter.
He's welcome to join at the end, if she wishes.
Mrs Khalil: My son can translate. He's outside. Why do we need a stranger?
Mrs Khalil: Yes. Maybe that's better. He's only sixteen.
B
Get it done

Book it right, and check the match

In person; female if requested; right dialect (Syrian Arabic); double time; does she know the family?

Mrs Khalil is 45, from Syria, referred by her GP with low mood. Will Syrian Arabic be comfortable for you?
Do you know her or her family? It's a small community.
Ms Saleh: Yes, I'm from Damascus. No problem.
Ms Saleh: I don't think so. I'll tell you if I recognise her.
C
Get it done

Brief the interpreter in two minutes

First person; interpret everything; cultural notes said openly to both; confidentiality; suicide will come up

Please interpret everything, as closely as you can, in the first person: 'I feel', not 'she says she feels'. Even things that seem off-topic.
If a phrase needs explaining, please do, and just tell us both you're adding an explanation.
We may talk about painful things, including suicide. Is that OK for you?
Ms Saleh: Yes. Sometimes a phrase needs explaining.
Ms Saleh: That's fine. Thank you for asking.
D
Get it done

Sit in a triangle, speak to her

Face her, not the interpreter; 'How are you?', not 'ask her'; short chunks; introduce roles and confidentiality

Mrs Khalil, I'm one of the psychiatry doctors. This is Ms Saleh, a professional interpreter. She'll repeat everything we both say, and it's all confidential.
Can you tell me what's been happening?
Doctor, my heart is squeezed. All the time. Here. And I don't sleep.
E
Get it done

What does 'my heart is squeezed' mean?

Explore the idiom; interpreter's cultural note; onset with mother's death; grief and guilt

When you say your heart is squeezed, what is it like for you?
How long have you felt this heaviness?
I'm so sorry. Not being able to say goodbye to your mother must be very painful.
Like a heavy stone. The sadness sits here.
Ms Saleh: Doctor, I'm adding something. This phrase is commonly used for deep sadness or worry, not only for pain.
About six months. Since my mother died in Syria. I couldn't go to her funeral.

Get it done

The core symptoms, in plain words

Early waking, appetite, weight, energy, pleasure, guilt; war nightmares; no idioms like 'feeling blue'

How is your sleep?
And your appetite? Your energy?
Is there anything you still get pleasure from?
Do you have bad dreams or memories of the war?
I wake at four or five and can't sleep again.
I'm not hungry. My clothes are loose. I'm tired all the time.
Nothing. Even with my children, I'm there but not there. I feel guilty.
G
Get it done

Ask about suicide, faith or not

Normalise, then ask; passive wish vs active thoughts and plans; faith and children as protective factors

Sometimes when people feel this low, they feel life isn't worth living. Have you had thoughts like that?
Thank you for telling me. Have you ever thought of ending your life yourself, or made any plans?
If those thoughts ever got stronger, I'd want you to tell us straight away.
Sometimes I ask God to take me. In my sleep.
No. Never. My children need me. And in my faith it is forbidden.
H
Law and safety net

Safety at home, while she is alone

Ask everyone, privately; if abuse disclosed: safeguarding adults procedures, offer IDVA referral

I ask everyone this. Do you feel safe at home? Has anyone hurt you, or made you afraid?
If that ever changed, you could tell us or your GP privately, always with an interpreter, never family.
No, no. My husband is a good man. He is tired too. He worries about money.
I
Get it done

The diagnosis and the plan, simply

Depression is an illness, not madness; sertraline daily, 2 to 4 weeks, early nausea; 6 months after well; therapy with interpreter; GP bloods

Mrs Khalil, I think you have depression. It's a common illness, and it can be treated. It's not weakness, and it's not your fault.
First, a tablet called sertraline. It's not addictive. One each morning. It takes two to four weeks to help. At first you may feel sick or more nervous; that usually settles.
Second, talking therapy, with an Arabic interpreter every time. I'll also ask your GP for blood tests, to check things like your thyroid and iron.
Depression. In my family we don't talk of this. People will say I'm mad.
Will I take it forever?
J
Get it done

Teach-back, the son, and after

Her words back; safety net 111 press 2, ask for Arabic; written info in Arabic; she decides what son hears; debrief interpreter

To make sure I explained it well, can you tell me in your words what we've agreed?
Would you like your son to come in now? What would you like us to tell him?
Ms Saleh, thank you. Some of that was heavy. How are you doing?
I have depression. It's an illness. One tablet in the morning; it helps after some weeks. Talking therapy with an interpreter. Blood tests with my GP.
Only that I have depression and will take medicine. Not about my mother.
Ms Saleh: It reminded me of home, but I'm OK.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

'Tell her...', 'ask her...'
Speak directly to Mrs Khalil, in the first person, and look at her
Long, compound questions
One short question at a time, then pause
Accepting a family member to save time
Professional interpreter; son joins at the end if she wishes
Assuming faith rules out suicide
Ask about passive and active thoughts and plans
Idioms like 'feeling blue'
Plain words: sleep, appetite, pleasure, guilt
Finish

Close it well

YouThat's exactly right. If those thoughts get stronger, call NHS 111 and press 2, and say 'Arabic' for an interpreter.
YouI'll give you this in writing in Arabic. I'll see you in two weeks, with an interpreter.
Mrs KhalilThank you, doctor.
