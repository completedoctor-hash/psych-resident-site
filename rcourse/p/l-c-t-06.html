Refusing insulin: does Geoffrey have capacity to decide?

Tuesday 14:20, liaison psychiatry · Acute medical ward, teaching hospital

The task

Geoffrey Amankwah is a 56-year-old man with paranoid schizophrenia and type 2 diabetes on a medical ward at a teaching hospital. He often does not take his diabetes medicines and has eye, kidney and vascular complications. Three days ago he was found collapsed in the street and admitted with a hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state; after emergency care he is now stable. The endocrinology consultant has told him he needs insulin, but he has refused every dose. The ward has asked liaison psychiatry to decide whether he has capacity to refuse insulin. Please assess his capacity to make this decision.

Your job: Assess his capacity to refuse insulin: check understanding, retention, use and weighing, and communication; explore what is driving the refusal; reach a decision-specific conclusion and agree the least restrictive next steps.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Explain honestly why a psychiatrist is here, in the first minute
  2. Ask what he understands, then fill the gaps in plain words
  3. Plant the key risks and test retention again near the end
  4. Elicit the belief behind the refusal: content, onset, conviction 0-100
  5. Screen by name for delirium, low mood and suicidal thinking, and check olanzapine
  6. Give a decision- and time-specific conclusion without blame
  7. Agree least restrictive steps: oral options, his sister, daily reassessment
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Geoffrey Amankwah?
GeoffreyYes. Is this about the injections?
YouIt is. The diabetes team asked me to talk with you about your decision. I'll be honest: they've asked whether you're able to make it.
GeoffreySo you think I'm mad. I've already told them no.
YouThat's your right to say. I'd just like to understand your reasons.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, honest purpose, his right to decide)
  • 2. His understanding (the collapse, diabetes, why insulin; fill the gaps)
  • 3. Plant the facts (risks of refusing; say it back)
  • 4. Using and weighing (the belief behind the refusal, conviction, alternatives)
  • 5. Communicating (clear, consistent choice)
  • 6. Mental state (delirium, mood, suicidal thoughts, psychosis, olanzapine)
  • 7. Conclusion (retention check, MCA test, decision- and time-specific)
  • 8. Next steps and close (tablets query, his sister, no force, review tomorrow)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

Get it done

Open honestly, take the sting out

Name your role and purpose; apologise for the smoking ban; capacity is presumed

I can hear how insulting that feels, and being kept in without a smoke on top of it.
I don't start from the view that you can't decide. Most people I see can.
Can I ask what you've been told?
So you think I'm mad. That's why they've sent a psychiatrist. They won't even let me out for a smoke.
Fine. Ask your questions.
B
Get it done

Give the information, then test it

His account of the collapse and insulin; then add: dehydration, insulin works most reliably now

Can you tell me what happened when you collapsed, and what caused it?
What have they told you about insulin, and why they want it now?
Let me add a bit. The sugar got so high it dried your body out badly, which is why you collapsed.
Sugar went sky-high. I'd felt rotten for days, drinking pop all the time.
Type 2. They say tablets and diet aren't enough any more.
C
Get it done

Plant the facts for retention

Risks of refusing: another collapse, coma, death; long term eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart; say it back

The team's worry is that without insulin the sugar climbs again within days: another collapse, a coma, even death.
Over years it also harms eyes, kidneys, nerves and heart.
Could you say that back to me? I'll ask again before I go.
Sugar goes up, I collapse again, coma, maybe die. Eyes and kidneys get worse.
D
Get it done

Find the belief behind the no

Ask his reasons; chips in the vials; what they do, when it began (TV at home before), conviction 100/100

So help me understand. With all that, what makes you say no?
Tell me more about the chips. What do they do?
When did you first notice this? How sure are you, from nought to a hundred?
It's not the insulin. It's what's in it. The nurses put chips in the bottles.
Tracking chips. They read my thoughts, then control them.
A hundred. Why else would she shake it?
E
Get it done

Weighing, and the gentler options

Ask him to weigh death against the chips; offer a sealed pen and tablets; note what he would accept

So on one side there's another collapse or dying. On the other, the chips. How do you weigh those up?
What if it came as a sealed pen you opened yourself? Or tablets?
Sugar's a risk, I know. But the chip's worse. Once it's in, I'm not me.
They'd have got to the pen first. Tablets, maybe. Tablets can't carry chips.
F
Get it done

A clear, consistent choice

Ask his decision today and whether it has changed; any form of communication counts

So what's your decision about insulin today?
Has that been the same each day?
No insulin. Same as yesterday. Same tomorrow.
G
Get it done

Name the cause: delirium, mood, psychosis

Orientation; mood and suicidal thoughts; olanzapine adherence; voices; past episodes

A few questions I ask everyone. What day is it today, and where are we?
And your mood lately? Any thoughts about ending your life, or wishing you weren't here?
Have you been taking your olanzapine? Hearing any voices?
Tuesday. Ward 7, the General.
Fed up, not depressed. I want to live. I just want to go home.
On and off. No voices. Only the TV, before.
H
Law and safety net

Retention check and the legal test

Ask the risks again near the end; MCA ss2-3, functional question first then causal link (JB 2021); decision- and time-specific

Before I sum up, can you tell me again what the team said could happen without insulin?
You understand and remember it all, and your answer is clear.
But the worry about chips outweighs everything else, and I think it comes from your illness. So for this one decision, today, I don't think you can weigh it up.
Collapse, coma, maybe die. Eyes and kidneys worse. I told you.
So that's it. You've decided I'm mad.
I
Law and safety net

Least restrictive next step and best interests

No forcing; ask diabetes team re oral agents; ring his sister Abena; IMCA only if nobody to consult; restraint and DoLS need senior legal advice; MHA not for diabetes

Nobody is going to hold you down.
I'll ask the diabetes team today whether tablets could be safe for now.
Is there someone you'd want involved? I'll ring her with your permission.
Now they'll hold me down.
My sister, Abena. She's sensible.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Incapable because of schizophrenia, or because it's unwise
Functional test first; show the belief stops weighing
Testing understanding before informing
Ask, then fill the gaps in plain words
Persuading instead of assessing
Explore his reasons; offer options, don't argue
Forgetting retention
Ask the risks again near the end
Mental Health Act for insulin
MCA best interests, least restrictive, legal advice if force
Finish

YouSo: I'll ask the diabetes team about tablets today, ring Abena, and come back tomorrow. If your thinking about the chips changes, my answer may too.
YouWhat do you understand happens next?
GeoffreyYou talk to the sugar doctors about tablets, ring Abena, and come back tomorrow.
YouExactly. I'll let your care coordinator know too. Thanks for talking so openly, Geoffrey.
