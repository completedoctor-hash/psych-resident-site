Stopping sertraline: what's the real problem?

Monday 15:40, CMHT outpatient clinic · Clinic room, community mental health team base

The task

Shona Gallagher, a 28-year-old woman, has been referred to the community mental health team by her GP. She began sertraline a month ago for depression and is now on 100 mg once daily. Her GP's mood scores show she has improved, so the GP was surprised when she asked to stop it because she 'doesn't like the side effects'. She would not say more to her GP, despite a good relationship, and asked to see a psychiatrist instead. Please speak to Shona, explore her concerns about sertraline and agree a plan with her.

Your job: In about ten minutes, find out exactly which side effect is troubling Shona, check her mood and other causes, and agree a safe plan with her that does not involve stopping sertraline suddenly.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Name the likely topic so she can say yes
  2. Separate desire, arousal and orgasm, and alone versus with partner
  3. Put it on a timeline: before depression, during, on sertraline
  4. Check mood now, past episodes and suicidal thoughts
  5. Screen at least four other causes, including unwanted sexual contact
  6. Offer 3 options, each with a benefit and a drawback
  7. Agree the plan, the GP letter wording, review date and crisis route by teach-back
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Shona Gallagher?
ShonaYes, hi.
YouThanks for coming, Shona. Dr Kaur said you'd like to stop sertraline. I'm keen to understand why.
ShonaIt's just the side effects. I don't like them.
YouSome side effects are awkward to talk about. Everything here is confidential, and nothing will shock me. Is that okay?
ShonaOkay. It's a bit embarrassing.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, normalise awkward topics, confidentiality, aim)
  • 2. The side effect (desire, arousal, orgasm; alone or with partner; onset)
  • 3. Timeline and impact (before depression, during, on sertraline; boyfriend; self-esteem)
  • 4. Mood now (response, past episodes, suicidal thoughts)
  • 5. Other causes (pill, alcohol, drugs, physical health, pregnancy plans, unwanted contact)
  • 6. Options (lower dose, switch, wait; benefit and drawback each)
  • 7. Stopping safely (discontinuation, relapse risk, how long to continue)
  • 8. Shared decision and close (her choice, GP letter wording, teach-back, review, leaflet, crisis line)

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

The impact on her and her boyfriend

Arguments, 'gone off him', self-esteem; reframe and offer to involve him

How is it affecting you and your boyfriend?
From what you've said, the wanting is there. It's your body's response the tablet is affecting.

He thinks I've gone off him. We keep arguing. Maybe I have gone off him, I don't know.
I hadn't thought of it like that.

Check her mood now

Response so far, previous episodes, self-harm and suicidal thoughts

How's your mood now, compared with a month ago?
Have you been depressed before?
Have you had thoughts of self-harm or wishing you weren't here, this time round?
Loads better. I'm sleeping, I'm back at work.
Once, when I was twenty-two. And no, never anything like that.

Get it done

Other causes before you blame the drug

Pill, pregnancy plans, alcohol, drugs, physical health, unwanted sexual contact

A few other things can affect sex. Any contraception? Any plans for pregnancy?
How much do you drink, and any drugs? Any physical health problems?
I ask everyone this. Has anything sexual ever happened that you didn't want?
The pill, for about five years. No baby plans this year.
A couple of glasses at weekends. No drugs. Dr Kaur did bloods recently, all normal.
No. Nothing like that.
G
Get it done

Real choices with trade-offs

Lower to 50 mg; switch, eg mirtazapine; wait. Benefit and drawback each

You don't have to choose between your mood and your sex life.
Option one: lower to 50 mg. It may ease the side effect, but there's a small risk your mood slips.
Option two: switch to a medicine with fewer sexual effects, like mirtazapine, which can cause sleepiness and weight gain.
Waiting it out is a third option, but this often doesn't settle on its own.
So do I have to choose between being depressed and having a sex life?
I'd rather try the lower dose first.
H
Get it done

Never stop suddenly

Dizziness, irritability, electric-shock feelings; relapse; continue 6+ months after well

Stopping suddenly can make you dizzy and irritable, with electric-shock feelings.
A month in, the depression could easily come back.
We usually continue at least six months after you're well, and longer as this is your second episode.
Honestly, I was going to just stop them this weekend.

You can say it's a sexual side effect. I just couldn't say it out loud.
Maybe. I'll ask him.

Law and safety net

If she had disclosed unwanted contact

Support, SARC or specialist service with consent; safeguarding if others at risk

Thank you for telling me. You're not to blame. Would you like support with that?
There's a specialist service that can help. Shall I put you in touch?
(Here: No. Nothing like that.)
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Looking embarrassed or using vague words
Name the topic calmly and use precise words
Assuming sertraline is the only cause
Timeline plus pill, alcohol, health and unwanted contact
Telling her to just wait and see
Offer 3 options with trade-offs
Agreeing to stop at once because her mood is better
Explain discontinuation and relapse; continue 6+ months
Writing details she didn't agree to share
Agree the GP letter wording with her
Finish

Close it well

YouSo: 50 mg from tomorrow, and we'll review your mood and the side effect in four weeks. If your mood dips, we go back up.
YouCan you tell me the plan in your words?
ShonaFifty milligrams, don't stop suddenly, see you in four weeks.
YouPerfect. If your mood drops or dark thoughts come, ring your GP the same day, NHS 111 option 2, or Samaritans, free, on 116 123. Here's a leaflet on sertraline.
