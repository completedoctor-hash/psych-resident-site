His coach wants the methylphenidate stopped: ADHD, consent and the TUE

Wednesday 16:00 · Adult ADHD clinic, outpatient department

The task

Mr Gareth Pugh is a 45-year-old judo coach who trains Ryan Doyle, a 19-year-old man doing well in regional competitions. Ryan has had ADHD since age seven and has taken methylphenidate for years. The judo association says methylphenidate is a banned stimulant in competition, so Mr Pugh wants Ryan switched to something else. At 16 Ryan tried atomoxetine; his symptoms worsened and his liver tests became abnormal, so he went back on methylphenidate. Ryan still makes careless mistakes, struggles to concentrate and follow coaching, talks over teammates, is short-tempered, has got into fights, was stopped for dangerous driving on the motorway and uses cannabis at parties. Ryan saw the psychiatrist this morning and has given consent for you to speak with his coach. Please answer Mr Pugh's questions. A collateral history is not needed.

Your job: Explain adult ADHD and its treatment to Tom's manager within the limits of Tom's consent, answer his questions, advise against stopping, explain the exemption route and suggest reasonable workplace adjustments.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. State the limits of Ryan's consent out loud, first
  2. Explain why this is adult ADHD using what the coach sees
  3. Advise against stopping; say why switching and herbal remedies won't work
  4. Explain the UK Anti-Doping TUE and what the application needs
  5. Listen to new information without confirming or adding anything
  6. Agree training adjustments and that Ryan makes the final decision

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Ryan's consent, its limits, agenda)
  • 2. His concern (anti-doping rule, wants a switch)
  • 3. ADHD now (inattention, impulsivity, temper)
  • 4. Why not just stop or switch (symptoms return, atomoxetine and liver)
  • 5. Therapeutic Use Exemption (UK Anti-Doping, specialist report)
  • 6. Other options (guanfacine, herbal remedies, strategies)
  • 7. Risks to address (cannabis, driving and DVLA, temper)
  • 8. Summary and close (recap, Ryan decides, letter, review)

Why not just stop it

Not dangerous to stop, but symptoms return within a day

I'd strongly advise against stopping.
The medicine works day by day. Within a day his focus and self-control would drop: in training, at home, even driving.
Well, he can't take it now. So he stops.

Why switching isn't simple

Atomoxetine at 16: worse symptoms, abnormal liver tests; other first choices are stimulants too

He tried the main non-stimulant, atomoxetine, at sixteen. His symptoms got worse and it affected his liver.
The other first-choice medicines are also stimulants, so they're banned too.
So swap him to something that isn't banned.
So there's nothing else?

Herbal remedies and the 'performance drug' worry

No evidence; supplements can contain banned substances; strict liability

There's no good evidence those work for ADHD, and supplements sometimes contain banned substances.
It's called strict liability. Whatever's in his body is his responsibility.
In ADHD, the medicine brings his focus up to an ordinary level. It doesn't give him an edge.
I've found a herbal calming remedy online. Natural.
Isn't it just a performance drug, then? That's why it's banned.

The lawful route: a Therapeutic Use Exemption

UK Anti-Doping grants it; specialist report; apply early

The proper route is a Therapeutic Use Exemption, from UK Anti-Doping.
It needs a specialist report: the diagnosis, what's been tried, and why he needs this medicine.
I can't grant it myself; only UK Anti-Doping can. We can write the report this week.
Brilliant. So you can write him a certificate today?
How long does it take?

I'd encourage you to talk to him, and we'll make sure we cover it with him too.

Driving and the DVLA

Ryan's legal duty; drug driving; discuss with Ryan

Driving is something we'll go through with Ryan himself.
In general, anyone whose condition affects their driving must tell the DVLA, and driving after cannabis is an offence.
He got pulled over on the motorway for dangerous driving. Scared me to death.

Adjustments in training

One instruction at a time, show then do, repeat back, cool-down breaks

Give one coaching point at a time, and show it before he does it.
Ask him to say the point back before he starts.
Agree a cool-down signal for when his temper rises.
I could do that. One thing at a time.
He'd go for a signal, actually.

Law and safety net

Equality Act, and Ryan decides

ADHD can be a disability; reasonable adjustments; decision is Ryan's

ADHD can count as a disability under the Equality Act, if it has a substantial, long-term effect on daily life.
It's Ryan's treatment and his decision. Shall we bring him in now?
So it's not up to me, then.
Fair enough. Get him in.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Agreeing to stop because a competition is coming
Advise against; explain the TUE
Discussing things outside Ryan's consent
Listen; don't confirm; raise with Ryan
Promising a certificate
Only UK Anti-Doping grants a TUE; you write the report
Calling it a performance enhancer, or dismissing the rules
It restores ordinary focus; rules exist for misuse
Forgetting adjustments
Agree concrete training changes
