Frightened about ECT: memory effects, the law and the alternatives

Wednesday 16:00 · Acute adult inpatient ward, family room

The task

Raymond Kowalczyk is a 64-year-old man admitted because his psychotic depression has returned. He has not responded to two courses of high-dose antidepressants, lithium augmentation or an antipsychotic. Over three days he has refused all food and fluid, his creatinine is rising and there is concern about kidney injury and blood clots. He no longer speaks and appears to be in a depressive stupor. The consultant has recommended ECT; the team has judged that he lacks capacity to consent. His daughter, Natalie, is unhappy with this and has asked to see you alone. Please explore her concerns and explain why ECT is advised, its side effects and the alternatives.

Your job: Meet his daughter: hear her fears, explain why ECT is recommended, what happens, the side effects including memory, the alternatives and the legal safeguards.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Hear her fears and images of ECT before explaining anything
  2. Explain why now: 3 days without food or fluid, rising creatinine, clot risk
  3. Describe the procedure and course: twice weekly, usually 6-12 treatments
  4. Give an honest account of memory effects and how they are reduced
  5. Explain the alternatives and the risk of waiting
  6. Explain capacity, the SOAD and her role; ask about advance decisions or LPA
  7. Agree an ECT suite visit, leaflet and meeting with the consultant
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors on your dad's ward. Are you Natalie Kowalczyk?
NatalieYes. I'm not happy about this ECT.
YouThank you for telling me directly. It's right that you ask questions.
NatalieI just don't want them frying his brain.
YouI understand. Can you tell me what worries you most, then I'll explain our thinking?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, acknowledge distress, invite her concerns)
  • 2. Her concerns (images from films, guilt, what she has read)
  • 3. Why now (stupor, no food or fluid for 3 days, creatinine, clots)
  • 4. What ECT involves (anaesthetic, relaxant, brief seizure, twice weekly, 6-12 sessions)
  • 5. Side effects (headache, confusion, memory, heart and anaesthetic)
  • 6. Alternatives (other medicines, fluids, the risk of waiting)
  • 7. Consent and law (capacity, Section 58A, SOAD, Section 62, advance decision, LPA, her role)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, suite visit, leaflet, consultant meeting, carer support)

Why now: the risk of not treating

Stupor; no food or fluid for 3 days; rising creatinine; clots and infection; failed treatments

Your dad's depression has become very severe. For three days he's eaten and drunk nothing, and his kidney blood test is getting worse.
Lying still for days also risks clots and infections.
ECT is the most effective treatment we have for depression this severe, and it works faster than tablets.
His kidneys? Nobody told me that.
But why would it work when nothing else has?

Get it done

What actually happens

Nil by mouth from midnight; anaesthetic and muscle relaxant; seizure under a minute; recovery; twice weekly, 6-12 treatments

He'd have nothing to eat from midnight. An anaesthetist puts him fully to sleep and gives a muscle relaxant, so his body barely moves.
A brief current causes a seizure in the brain lasting under a minute. He wakes in recovery within minutes.
usually twice weekly, often for six to twelve treatments. We review him after each one.
What actually happens? Does it hurt?
So he wouldn't feel anything?

Get it done

Side effects and memory, honestly

Headache, aching muscles, nausea, confusion; patchy memory for weeks around treatment; some lasting gaps; unilateral first; memory checks

Afterwards, a headache, aching muscles, feeling sick or confused for an hour or so are common. Those settle.
I'll be honest with you. Memory for the weeks around the treatment is often patchy.
Most of it improves, but some people say a few older memories don't come back.
And his memory? I've read people lose years.
Will he still know who I am?
E
Get it done

His heart and the physical work-up

U&E, FBC, LFTs, ECG, fluid balance chart, VTE assessment, anaesthetic review

His blood pressure and pulse rise briefly during treatment.
He'll have an ECG, blood tests and a check by the anaesthetist first.
Serious problems are very rare, about the same as any short anaesthetic.
What about his heart? He's sixty-four.

Alternatives and the risk of waiting

Another medicine change takes weeks; fluids support but don't treat; waiting risks kidney failure, clots, infection

We could change his medicine again, but that usually takes weeks to work.
With him barely drinking, waiting risks kidney failure, clots and infection.
Does that make sense so far?
Can't you just wait and try another tablet?
So waiting isn't really safe either.
G
Law and safety net

Who decides: capacity and the SOAD

Lacks capacity; detained, so Section 58A needs a SOAD certificate; Section 62 only if immediately necessary

At the moment your dad can't take in and weigh up this decision, so he can't consent himself.
Because he's detained, an independent doctor appointed by the Care Quality Commission must agree ECT is appropriate.
Your views will go to that doctor and the consultant.
So who decides, then? Me?
H
Law and safety net

His wishes, advance decisions and her role

Valid advance refusal or health and welfare attorney must be respected; relatives cannot consent; her role is his wishes; nearest relative

If he'd ever written a refusal of ECT, it would be respected.
Did he ever say what he'd want if he became this ill, or give anyone lasting power of attorney?
You're not making this decision or doing this to him. Telling us what he'd want is the most helpful thing you can do.
No. He never talked about it. He just always wanted to stand on his own feet. He'd hate being like this.
If I let you do this, he'll never forgive me.

Get it done

Support her, and give her time

Acknowledge shock and grief; ECT suite visit with ECT nurse lead; leaflet; carer's assessment

Would it help to visit the ECT suite tomorrow with the nurse who runs it?
Here's a leaflet to take home, and the Royal College of Psychiatrists website has clear information too.
How are you managing with all this yourself?
I didn't even know he'd been so low. Mum used to say he got quiet sometimes.
Yes, I'd like to see it.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Promising no lasting memory loss
Patchy memory is common; most improves; some gaps can last.
Law before listening
Hear her fears first; law comes later.
Implying she must consent
Her role is to tell you his wishes.
Forgetting advance decisions and LPA
Ask directly and record the answer.
Skipping the risk of not treating
Kidneys, clots and infection if you wait.
Finish

Close it well

NatalieHe's very ill and his kidneys are suffering. He'd be asleep. Twice a week. Memory might be patchy, mostly comes back. And an independent doctor has to agree.
YouThat's exactly right. I'll pass your views to the consultant, and we'll meet her tomorrow at four. If you think of questions tonight, ring the ward and ask for me.
