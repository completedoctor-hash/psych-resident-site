Seeing her late husband at home: grief or mental illness?

Lesson 31 of 90 · about 15 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Monday 10:00, new patient · General adult psychiatry outpatient clinic

The task

Mrs Bernadette Quinn is a 53-year-old school administrator whose husband, Frank, died suddenly three months ago. Her GP has referred her to the outpatient psychiatry clinic because she mentioned that she sometimes sees Frank around the house, and the GP is unsure whether this is a sign of mental illness. Please take a relevant history from Mrs Quinn and give her a brief formulation of what she is experiencing.

Your job: Take a history of the bereavement and her experiences, screen for depression, psychosis and risk, and give her a brief formulation of what is happening.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Get the story of Frank's sudden death and her first reactions
  2. Pin down each perception: where, how long, insight, feeling
  3. Ask about other senses, and whether he speaks or commands
  4. Screen depression: does mood lift, self-worth, coping at work
  5. Screen psychosis, alcohol and medicines
  6. Ask the wish to be with Frank, then suicide thoughts and plans, plainly
  7. Give a clear formulation: not madness, grief in waves, Cruse, review in 6 weeks
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Bernadette Quinn?
BernieYes. Bernie.
YouThank you for coming, Bernie. I'm very sorry about Frank.
BernieThank you.
YouYour GP mentioned some experiences since he died. Could you tell me about Frank and how things have been?
BernieWe were married twenty-nine years. It still doesn't feel real.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, condolences, purpose, plan)
  • 2. The loss (how Frank died, warning, funeral, relationship)
  • 3. Seeing Frank (where, when, how long, insight, other senses, commands)
  • 4. Grief reactions (numbness, guilt, anger, crying, sleep, appetite)
  • 5. Depression screen (mood lifts?, enjoyment, self-worth, work)
  • 6. Psychosis and other screen (voices, persecution, alcohol, medicines)
  • 7. Supports and history (son, daughter, friends, past losses, past mental health)
  • 8. Risk (wish to be with Frank, suicide thoughts, plans, protective factors)
  • 9. Formulation and close (not mad, why it happens, waves, Cruse, safety net, follow-up)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Let her tell Frank's story

How he died, any warning, the funeral, the marriage

What happened when Frank died? Was there any warning?
Please don't apologise. That was a terrible shock. Take as long as you need.
What was the funeral like, and how were things between you before?
He had chest pain on the Sunday. He said it was indigestion. He collapsed in the hall and died in A&E that night.
The funeral was lovely. Everyone came.
B
Get it done

Pin down what she sees

Where, when, how long, how often, sleep-wake link

Your GP said you sometimes see Frank. Tell me what happens. Where, when, and for how long?
Does it happen at any particular time, like as you wake up or drift off?
In his armchair, out of the corner of my eye. When I look properly, he's gone. A second, maybe.
When I wake up he's at the end of the bed sometimes, then he fades.
C
Get it done

Insight, feeling and the other senses

Knows he's not there? Comforting or frightening? Hearing, smell, commands

At the time, do you know he isn't really there? How do you feel when it happens?
Anything else? Hearing him, smelling him, sensing he's near?
Does he ever speak to you, or tell you to do anything?
I know he's gone. But it's nice, in a way. Like he's checking on me.
I've heard him clattering in the kitchen. And his aftershave, now and then.
No. It's just him being there.
D
Get it done

Grief reactions, including guilt

Numbness, anger, guilt, daily crying, sleep, appetite

How did you feel in those first weeks, and how has it changed since?
Do you find yourself feeling angry or guilty about anything?
How are your sleep and appetite? How often do the tears come?
Numb. On autopilot. Then I was furious with myself. I should have made him see the GP.
I cry every day, usually at night. I wake a lot. I'm eating, just not much.
E
Get it done

Screen for depression: ask about the good moments

Mood lifts?, enjoyment, self-worth, future, work and home

Is your mood low all the time, or are there moments when it lifts?
What do you think of yourself these days? And what lies ahead?
How are you managing at work and at home?
It lifts. I laugh with my granddaughter. Work helps, it keeps me busy.
I don't feel useless. Just lost. I can't imagine the future without him yet.
F
Get it done

Screen psychosis, alcohol and medicines

Voices about her, persecution, being watched, drinking, tablets

Have you had any other unusual experiences, like voices talking about you, or feeling people are against you or watching you?
How much are you drinking these days? Do you take any tablets or medicines?
No. Nothing like that.
Just a glass of wine on Saturdays. No tablets.
G
Get it done

Supports and past history

Son, daughter, friends, previous losses, past mental health

Who's been there for you since Frank died?
Have you lost anyone close before? And have you ever needed help with your mental health?
My son and daughter live round the corner. Friends from work.
My mum died ten years ago, but she'd been ill. Never seen anyone before.
H
Get it done

Ask the risk question kindly but plainly

Wish to be with Frank, suicide thoughts, plans, protective factors

Some people tell me they'd like to be with the person they've lost. Have you ever felt like that?
That's a very natural feeling. I do need to ask: have you thought about taking your own life, or planned anything?
What keeps you going?
Sometimes I just want to be with him.
No. I'd never. The grandchildren need me. I just miss him.
I
Get it done

Answer 'am I going mad?' and give grief a shape

Not mad; insight is the difference; sudden loss; waves not stages; no tablets; Cruse

No, Bernie, you're not going mad. Many people see, hear or sense someone they've lost.
The key is that you know it isn't real, and it comforts you rather than frightens you.
Grief comes in waves. Over months the waves usually come less often.
For grief like this, tablets don't help. Can I suggest Cruse Bereavement Support?
Really? I've been so frightened to tell anyone.
Everyone says it gets easier. It doesn't feel like it.
Should I be taking something for it?
J
Law and safety net

Her choices, her family and her rights

Informal, full capacity; consent before family contact; fit note only if she asks

Would you like your son or daughter involved, or to come with you next time?
Is work helping, or would some time off help? That's your choice.
My daughter would come. She's been brilliant.
Work helps. I'd rather keep going.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling the perceptions hallucinations that need treatment
Describe them as grief-related perceptions with insight
Rushing to symptoms before acknowledging the loss
Condolence and Frank's story first
Skipping the risk question because she seems to cope
Ask the wish to be with him, then suicide, every time
Offering an antidepressant or sleeping tablet for normal grief
Talking support, Cruse, review; tablets only if depression develops
Vague reassurance with no explanation
Explain insight, sleep-waking images and sudden loss
Finish

Close it well

YouWhat will you take from today, in your words?
BernieIt's grief, not madness. Seeing him is normal. I'll ring Cruse.
YouExactly. If your mood becomes low all the time, or the wish to be with Frank turns into thoughts about ending your life, see your GP, ring 111 option 2, or Samaritans, free, on 116 123.
YouI'll see you again in six weeks and write to your GP today.
