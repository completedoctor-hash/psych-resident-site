A wife's complaint: her husband waited 9 hours in A&E for a bed

Lesson 8 of 14 · about 14 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

Listen

Your place is saved.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Monday 15:10 · Corridor, then a quiet room, male acute ward

The task

You are the CT2 on the ward. Mr Brian Doyle, 64, was admitted informally overnight with severe depression and suicidal thoughts. The liaison team decided he needed admission at 21:30, but no bed was free until 06:30, so he waited 9 hours on a corridor chair in A&E. His wife, Mrs Pat Doyle, 61, stops you in the ward corridor: 'Are you a doctor? I want to make a complaint, and I want it done properly.' This morning Mr Doyle told the nurse he is happy for his wife to be told about his care. Listen to the whole complaint, apologise appropriately, put right what you can today, explain PALS and the formal complaints process, and record and escalate it.

Your job: Listen to the whole complaint, apologise appropriately, put right what you can today, explain PALS and the formal complaints process, and record and escalate it.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Move to a private room; confirm who she is and Brian's consent
  2. Hear the whole complaint without interrupting; ask 'Was there anything else?'
  3. Summarise all six concerns back and check none is missing
  4. Apologise for the specific failures; one honest 'why'; no blaming A&E
  5. Today: tablets checked, glasses returned, 6 pm daily call, consultant meeting
  6. Explain PALS, formal complaint (3-working-day acknowledgement) and the Ombudsman
  7. Report the wait and missed medicines, tell the ward manager, offer a carer's assessment
The exact words

Your opening

Mrs DoyleAre you a doctor? I want to make a complaint, and I want it done properly.
YouI'm one of the doctors on this ward, and I'd like to hear it properly. Let's not do it in the corridor. There's a quiet room here. Can I get you a drink?
Mrs DoyleWater. Fine.
YouCan I check who you are?
Mrs DoylePat Doyle. Brian's wife. He came in last night.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (private room, who she is, Brian's consent)
  • 2. Listen fully (open question, silence, 'anything else?', clarify)
  • 3. Summarise (all six concerns, check nothing missed)
  • 4. Feeling and apology (name her fear, apologise for specific failures)
  • 5. The honest why (no beds; explanation not excuse; no blame)
  • 6. Fix it today (tablets, glasses, plan, 6 pm call, consultant meeting)
  • 7. Formal routes (PALS, formal complaint, Ombudsman, consent, care unaffected)
  • 8. Carer and escalation (carer's assessment, incident reports, ward manager)
  • 9. Close (recap, written details, notes)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Check consent, then invite the story

Brian agreed this morning; she can hear about his care; open question 'from the start'

Thank you, Mrs Doyle. Brian told the team this morning he's happy for us to talk to you, so I can be open with you.
Please tell me what happened, from the start.
We got to A&E at seven. The mental health nurse said at half nine he needed a bed. Then nothing. Nine hours. On a plastic chair in a corridor.
B
Get it done

Listen, then ask 'anything else?'

Silence and 'Go on'; 'Was there anything else?' until nothing; clarify tablets and who was with him

Go on.
Was there anything else?
Which tablets did he miss, do you know?
And while he waited, was someone with him?
Nobody gave him anything to eat till three in the morning. He missed his evening tablets. And they sent me home at midnight. Nobody rang me.
A nurse said, 'You're lucky, some people wait two days.' And he left his glasses down there.
A security man, mostly. Like he was a criminal.
C
Get it done

Summarise all six concerns

9-hour corridor wait; no food till 3 am; missed tablets; she wasn't told; hurtful remark; glasses in A&E

Let me check I've got it all. He waited nine hours on a corridor chair after the decision to admit him. No food till three. Missed evening tablets. You were sent home and not told when he moved. A hurtful remark when he arrived. And his glasses are still in A&E. Have I missed anything?
No. That's it. I sat at home all night thinking he'd walk out of there and... I thought I'd get a call to say he'd done something.
D
Get it done

Name the fear, then a specific apology

Name her night of fear; apologise for what happened, not 'if you feel upset'

That sounds terrifying. He's very unwell, and you were at home all night with no news. Anyone would be frightened and angry.
I am sorry. Brian should not have waited that long, in those conditions, and you should have been kept informed. I'm also sorry for what was said when he arrived. That wasn't acceptable.
Forty-one years we've been married. Sorry. I'm not a crier.
Thank you. That's all I wanted to hear at first.
E
Get it done

One honest sentence on why

No free beds until a discharge this morning; explanation, not excuse; no blaming A&E staff

I'll be honest with you. There were no free beds in the trust last night until someone was discharged this morning. That's an explanation, not an excuse.
It might. That's exactly why your complaint matters.
Why did it take nine hours?
So it'll happen to someone else tonight.
F
Get it done

Fix what you can today

Check chart and today's doses (no doubling); BP check; glasses from A&E; plan; Mo rings 6 pm; Dr Hughes Weds

As soon as we finish, I'll check his chart to make sure he's had this morning's tablets and that he carries on as normal tonight.
Next, I'll ring A&E and ask them to send his glasses up today.
His named nurse, Mo, will ring you every evening at six. His consultant, Dr Hughes, will see you both at the ward round on Wednesday.
He said he'd had them.
Yes. Nobody's told me anything.
G
Law and safety net

PALS or a formal complaint

Complaints Regulations 2009: oral or written, within 12 months; acknowledged in 3 working days; written response

It will be on record. There are two routes. PALS, our Patient Advice and Liaison Service, can take your concerns and get quick answers, often within days. Or you can make a formal complaint to the trust, by letter, email or phone.
They acknowledge it within three working days, agree with you what they'll look into, including A&E, and send you a written response.
And the complaint? I want it on record. Not a pat on the head.
What happens with a formal one?
H
Law and safety net

His consent, her rights, the Ombudsman

Complaint about his care needs his consent; her own experience in her own right; then PHSO

For anything about his care, yes, they'll ask him. Your own experience, being sent home and not told, you can complain about yourself.
If you're not satisfied with the trust's final response, you can go to the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman.
Does Brian have to agree?
I
Law and safety net

Complaining won't affect his care

GMC Good medical practice (2024): respond honestly; complaint must not affect care; NHS Constitution

No. Complaining won't affect his care in any way. I'd rather know when we get things wrong.
Will it go against him? Will they treat him differently?
J
Law and safety net

She is a carer too

Care Act 2014 s10: carer's assessment from the council; carers' lead can arrange

You're also his carer. You're able to ask for a carer's assessment from the council, for support for you. Our carers' lead can arrange it.
I've never thought of myself as a carer.
K
Get it done

Record and escalate today

Incident reports: long wait and missed medicines; tell the ward manager about the remark; write it in the notes

I'll tell the ward manager today about what was said.
I'm also reporting the long wait and the missed tablets on our incident system, and I'll write what you've told me in his notes.
And the nurse who said that?
Right. Thank you for listening, at least.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Arguing the facts or defending A&E early
Listen to the end; one honest 'why' later; no blame
'I'm sorry you feel that way'
Apologise for what happened: 'I'm sorry he waited nine hours'
Promising 'this will never happen again'
Promise what you control: report, review, today's actions
Forgetting missed medicines are reportable
Incident report for the missed doses and the wait
Handing over a PALS leaflet instead of listening
Listen, apologise and act first; leaflet at the end
Finish

Close it well

YouSo, I'll check his tablets and get his glasses up. Mo rings you at six. Dr Hughes sees you on Wednesday. Here are the PALS and complaints details in writing. Does that cover everything?
Mrs DoyleI think so.
YouThank you for telling me. If anything else worries you, ring the ward, day or night.
