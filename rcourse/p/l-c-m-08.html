Saying no safely: a man demanding diazepam who threatens a complaint

Lesson 10 of 14 · about 13 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

Monday 15:20 · Community mental health team outpatient clinic

The task

You are the CT1 in a CMHT clinic. Mr Lee Barker, 38, was referred by his GP with generalised anxiety disorder. In August an out-of-hours GP gave him 5 days of diazepam 2 mg three times a day. Since then he has been buying 'blues' online. Today he says only diazepam works and wants a month's supply. When you hesitate, he stands, raises his voice and says he will complain and 'go to the papers'. The clinic nurse is in the next room. Say no to diazepam clearly and kindly, offer real alternatives, keep yourself and him safe, and respond to the complaint threat.

Your job: Say no to diazepam clearly and kindly, offer real alternatives, keep yourself and him safe, and respond to the complaint threat.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask about the anxiety first and name his distress before any refusal
  2. Say one clear, kind no to diazepam with one reason; don't re-argue
  3. Stay seated and calm when he stands; set a limit; know the exit and the nurse
  4. Ask: blues per day, since when, last dose, withdrawal, fits, alcohol, other drugs
  5. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts and plans
  6. Offer sertraline 25 mg then 50 mg, Talking Therapies self-referral, drug service referral
  7. Give harm-reduction advice, PALS details, review in 2 weeks, GP letter
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, Mr Barker. I'm one of the doctors here. Your GP asked us to see you about anxiety. Where would you like to start?
Mr BarkerLook, I'll save us both time. Diazepam is the only thing that works. I need a month's worth.
YouOkay. Before we get to that, tell me what the anxiety is like at the moment.
Mr BarkerMy heart pounds all day. Some days I can't get to the shop.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. His anxiety first (symptoms, impact, name the distress)
  • 2. One clear, kind no (once, with a reason, then stop)
  • 3. When he stands (calm, seated, limit, exit, nurse, complaint welcomed)
  • 4. What he is really taking (blues, alcohol, other drugs, withdrawal, fits)
  • 5. Suicide risk (thoughts, plans, protective factors)
  • 6. Real alternatives (sertraline, Talking Therapies, drug and alcohol service)
  • 7. Harm reduction and safety net (no mixing, no extra, not alone, 999, 111 option 2)
  • 8. Close and follow-up (teach-back, 2-week review, GP letter, consultant, incident report)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Name the distress before the no

Heart pounding, can't get to the shop, diazepam made him feel normal: reflect it

That sounds exhausting. Living with that every day, then finding something that made you feel normal: I can see why you want it back.
The diazepam the out-of-hours doctor gave me in August was the first time I felt normal in years.
So you'll give it me?
B
Law and safety net

One clear, kind no

GMC prescribing guidance (2021): you need not prescribe what isn't in his interests; diazepam is Schedule 4 (Part 1) CD

I won't prescribe diazepam today. I know that's not what you want to hear.
In the long run it makes anxiety worse and it's hard to come off. But I do want to help, and there are things I can do.
Why should I suffer when there's a tablet that works?
C
Law and safety net

When he stands: calm, seated, a limit

Low, slow voice; stay seated; name the anger; invite him to sit; exit and nurse in mind; end if unsafe

I can hear how angry and let down you feel. I do want to listen. Would you sit back down with me so we can sort out a plan?
I want to help, but I can't carry on while you're shouting. Can we sit down?
(stands, louder) Unbelievable. You lot never listen! I'll put in a complaint. I'll go to the papers!
(pause, sits) Go on then.
D
Law and safety net

Welcome the complaint

His right; PALS details; GMC Good medical practice (2024): a complaint must not affect his care

Thank you. And you have every right to complain. I'll give you the PALS details before you go. It won't change how I look after you.
Right. Fine.
E
Get it done

The blues: how many, how long, withdrawal

Tablets per day, since when, last dose, missed-day symptoms, fits ever

Your GP mentioned you've been getting tablets online. How many a day, and since when?
When was your last one?
What happens if you miss a day? Any shakes, sweats, or ever a fit?
Three, four a day. Blues. About two months. Last one this morning.
Shaky and sweaty. Never had a fit.
F
Get it done

Alcohol, other drugs, and why blues are dangerous

4 cans most nights; ask pregabalin, opioids; street 'diazepam' often stronger, unknown dose; dependence explained

And alcohol, or anything else, like painkillers or pregabalin?
Many tablets sold online as diazepam are actually stronger drugs, and you can't tell the dose. With alcohol on top, people have stopped breathing.
No. It's what happens to anyone's body after weeks of daily use. You haven't done anything wrong.
Four cans most nights. Nothing else.
They're from the same seller every time.
(quieter) So I'm an addict now?
G
Get it done

Ask about suicide, directly

Thoughts, plans, acts; protective factors (his child); alcohol and benzodiazepines raise risk

Can I ask: when things are this bad, do you ever feel it's not worth going on?
Have you made any plans, or come close to acting on it?
(tearful) I just want to be able to take my kid to school.
Sometimes I think, what's the point. I'd never do anything. My kid.
H
Get it done

Sertraline, explained properly

25 mg each morning for 7 days, then 50 mg; jittery or sick early; ~4 weeks to help; no dependence; review in 2 weeks

First, a tablet called sertraline. It's one of the main treatments for anxiety, and it doesn't cause dependence.
We'd start low, 25 milligrams each morning, then 50 after a week. You might feel jittery or sick for the first week or two. It takes about four weeks to really help.
Antidepressants? I'm not depressed. And citalopram made me sick.
Four weeks. And in the meantime?
I
Get it done

Talking Therapies and the drug service

Self-refer to NHS Talking Therapies (CBT, applied relaxation); refer to drug and alcohol service this week

You can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies today for CBT. And I'll refer you to the drug and alcohol service this week, so they can help you cut down the blues safely.
No. Stopping suddenly could be risky. They'll assess you and agree a safe plan, sometimes a prescribed reducing dose that they supervise.
Will they just tell me to stop?
Just give me a week's worth. Then I'll do your therapy.
J
Law and safety net

Harm reduction and the safety net

No more than now, no alcohol with them, not alone; fit: 999; crisis: 111 option 2; Samaritans 116 123

Until then: don't take more than you do now, don't mix them with drink, and don't use alone.
If you have a fit, call 999. If things feel desperate, ring 111 and choose option 2, or Samaritans, free, on 116 123.
If you won't give me any, I'll just buy more.
Got it.
K
Get it done

After he leaves

GP letter: request declined, plan, recommend no benzodiazepines; tell consultant today; incident report if aggression

I'll see you in two weeks to see how the sertraline's going, and I'll write to your GP.
Sorry for shouting.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Giving 'just a few' to end the conflict
One clear no; repeat it kindly if pushed
Saying no before acknowledging distress
Ask about the anxiety and name it first
Arguing back or matching his volume
Stay seated, voice low, invite him to sit, set a limit
Not asking about blues, alcohol or withdrawal
Dose, duration, last dose, shakes, fits, cans per night
Discouraging the complaint or sounding defensive
'That's your right' and the PALS details
Finish

Close it well

YouLet me check I've been clear. What's the plan?
Mr BarkerStart sertraline, sign up for therapy, you refer me to the drug people. No diazepam.
YouExactly. No diazepam, but a real plan. I'll see you in two weeks, and I'll write to your GP. Here's the PALS leaflet too.
Mr BarkerSorry for shouting.
YouThank you for saying that. You've been under a lot of strain. See you in two weeks.
