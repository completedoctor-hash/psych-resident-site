Burnt hands after a bin fire: why did she set it?

Tuesday 19:15, liaison psychiatry · Emergency department, minors cubicle

The task

Rhiannon Achebe, 20, is in her second year at university. She came to A&E with minor burns on both hands and told the triage nurse she got them after lighting a fire in a recycling bin outside her student house. She would not say why she did it but agreed to talk to the psychiatric liaison team. She has never been known to mental health services. Please explore what happened, work out the most likely diagnosis, and complete a risk assessment.

Your job: In about ten minutes, find out what happened and why, map the fire-setting before, during and after, rule out other causes, reach the likely diagnosis and grade the risk of further fires.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Answer her hands question, then give confidentiality and its one limit before any detail
  2. Map feelings before, during, after the fire
  3. Ask how she felt watching the fire engines
  4. Ask about past fires, age fire first interested her, the lighter in her pocket
  5. Rule out revenge, self-harm, psychosis, mood, substances, learning difficulty
  6. Ask who sleeps in the house and what she'd do if the urge came tonight
  7. Agree two safety actions plus GP and psychology referral; check the plan back
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Rhiannon?
RhiannonYes. Are my hands going to be okay?
YouThe burns team say they're minor and should heal well. I'd like about ten minutes to understand how today happened.
RhiannonDoes this go to the police?
YouThis is private between us. I'd only share if someone faced real danger, and I'd tell you first.
RhiannonOkay.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (hands first, confidentiality and its limit)
  • 2. Today's events (ex kissing someone, half a bottle of wine, the urge)
  • 3. The fire (tension before, relief, stamping on it, ran inside, fire engines)
  • 4. Past fire-setting (school bin, garden, since childhood, the lighter)
  • 5. Rule out causes (revenge, self-harm, psychosis, mood, drugs, learning, personality)
  • 6. Stress and support (coursework, dad's cancer, housemates, who knows)
  • 7. Risk (four in the house, urge tonight, self-harm, legal)
  • 8. Summary and close (pyromania, lighter, no alcohol, GP and psychology, university, 111)

Name it and hand over the lighter

Pyromania explained, the lighter swap, no alcohol for now

What you describe, tension that builds and only eases when you light a fire, sounds like something called pyromania. There's a specific talking therapy that helps.
Two things tonight. Could you leave the lighter with me? And avoid alcohol until we've got help in place.
Could you try squeezing a stress ball or texting a friend instead, just till you're seen?
So there is something wrong with me. What happens now?
The lighter's what calms me down.

Law and safety net

Arson, the police and confidentiality

Arson is a crime; fire service may investigate; legal advice; no duty to report; consultant if high risk

I won't contact the police. The fire service may ask questions, so it's worth getting legal advice if they do.
If I ever thought someone was at serious risk, I'd talk to my consultant, and I'd tell you first.
Are you going to tell the police?

Safety plan, capacity and the university

Informal, has capacity; plan in writing; GP and psychology within a week; university only with consent

I'll write to your GP today asking for a referral to a psychologist who specialises in this, and ask them to see you within a week.

Watch out

Traps to avoid

Promising total confidentiality
One honest limit, given before she discloses
Calling it a drunken one-off
Ask past fires, childhood interest, sober episodes
Never asking about the fire engines
Ask how she felt watching them
Forgetting the people asleep in the house
Ask who lives there and where the bin is
Threatening police involvement
Be honest about arson; suggest legal advice
