Her mother came instead: collateral history of low mood

Wednesday 10:15 · Community mental health team clinic

The task

Chloe Brennan is a 21-year-old woman referred to the CMHT by her GP with low mood and not eating properly. She did not attend her first appointment today, but her mother, Siobhan Brennan, has come in her place, worried. Chloe has given written consent for the team to speak with her mother. Please take a collateral history from Mrs Brennan about Chloe's difficulties, covering mood, eating, functioning, background and risk, and answer her questions about what happens next.

Your job: Take a collateral history from the mother, with the daughter's consent, covering onset, symptoms, eating, risk, functioning and background, then agree the next steps.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Chloe's written consent and say what can and cannot be shared
  2. Establish onset, trigger and course
  3. Cover mood, interest, energy, sleep, appetite, weight and slowing
  4. Ask 3 eating questions: what she eats, why, how she sees her body
  5. Screen psychosis, mania and substances
  6. Ask about death wishes, plans, self-harm, means, risk to others and family suicide
  7. Agree urgent next steps, safety advice, crisis numbers and carer support
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Mrs Brennan, Chloe's mum?
Mrs BrennanYes. She wouldn't come, so I did. I'm so sorry.
YouI'm glad you did. Chloe has agreed I can talk with you. Anything private she has told us stays private.
Mrs BrennanThat's fine. I just want her to get help.
YouCould you tell me what you've noticed and what worries you most?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Chloe's consent, confidentiality, thanks)
  • 2. Main concerns (what changed, when, trigger, course, why she didn't come)
  • 3. Mood symptoms (mood, interest, energy, sleep, appetite, weight, slowing)
  • 4. Eating (what she eats, why, weight and shape, purging, exercise)
  • 5. Thinking (concentration, guilt, hopelessness, psychosis, mania, substances)
  • 6. Functioning (self-care, work, university, going out, GP tests)
  • 7. Risk and family history (death wishes, plans, self-harm, means, others, father's suicide)
  • 8. Summary and close (impression, urgent visit, lock away medicines, crisis numbers, carer support)

Risk: death wishes, plans, means, others

Passive wishes, plans, self-harm; medicines at home; anger towards ex

These next questions are hard, but important. Has she ever said she wishes she wasn't here, or talked about dying?
Has she talked about ending her life, made any plans, or hurt herself in any way?

Family history of suicide

Ask explicitly; father's suicide when Chloe was 14 raises urgency

Has anyone in the family had depression, or other mental health problems?
Has anyone in the family died by suicide?
I'm so sorry. Take your time.
Her dad. He had depression. He took his own life when Chloe was fourteen.

Law and safety net

The plan, safety tonight and carer support

See Chloe at home within 2 days; lock away medicines; crisis line, 111 option 2, 999; don't force tablets; Care Act carer's assessment

We'll ring Chloe today, and with her agreement two of us will see her at home within two days.
Please lock away the paracetamol and the sertraline tonight.
You can ask the council for a carer's assessment.
Should I make her start the tablets the GP gave her?
Why can't you just admit her and fix this?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Hiding behind confidentiality
Chloe consented; and you can always listen.
Missing or assuming an eating disorder
Ask what she eats, why, and how she sees her body.
Not asking family history of suicide
Ask it explicitly; it changes urgency.
Forgetting self-neglect and risk to others
Ask about washing, going out, and anger at anyone.
A routine rebooking
Visit within 2 days, lock away medicines, give crisis numbers.
Finish

Close it well

YouThis sounds like depression, and a fairly severe one: low mood, no interest or energy, early waking, weight loss, slowing, and feeling worthless and hopeless. It doesn't sound like an eating disorder.
YouIf she talks about ending her life, makes plans, or you're worried she isn't safe, ring the crisis line, or NHS 111, then pick the mental health option, or 999 in an emergency.
YouCould you tell me back what you'll do tonight?
Mrs BrennanLock the tablets away. If she says anything about ending things, ring the crisis line, or 999 if it's urgent.
