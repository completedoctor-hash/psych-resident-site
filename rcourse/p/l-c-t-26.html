Explaining bulimia nervosa and its treatment

Tuesday 14:00, outpatient clinic · Community eating disorder service

The task

Ms Hannah Mwangi, a 24-year-old account manager at a media agency, was sent by her GP to the community eating disorder service with two years of bingeing and making herself sick. Her weight has troubled her since she was a teenager and tried several diets at university. The move into a demanding job with long days and after-work drinks led to weight gain and comments from family and colleagues. She began exercising more and skipping food in the day, then arrives home ravenous and eats large amounts quickly, feeling out of control. Afterwards she feels guilty and ashamed and vomits using her fingers, now every weekday. She is more preoccupied with food, though her weight is stable and BMI is 22. Observations are normal and she has never seen a psychiatrist. Please discuss the diagnosis and treatment options with her. A risk assessment is not needed today.

Your job: Check the pattern briefly, explain the diagnosis of bulimia nervosa and the cycle that keeps it going, outline the physical risks, and agree the treatment options with her. No risk assessment is needed today.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Lower shame in the first minute: calm, unshockable
  2. Confirm binges, loss of control, vomiting, every other purging method, weekends
  3. Name bulimia nervosa; say why not anorexia or binge eating disorder
  4. Draw the restrict-binge-purge cycle from her own week
  5. Explain physical risks; potassium blood test today; dental advice
  6. Guided self-help and regular eating first; CBT-ED next; no tablets first-line
  7. Teach-back, referral, Beat, safety net, review in 4 weeks
The exact words

Your opening

HannahYes.

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, lower shame, agree the plan)
  • 2. Brief check (typical weekday, loss of control, purging by name, weekends, self-worth)
  • 3. The diagnosis (bulimia nervosa; not anorexia, not binge eating disorder)
  • 4. The cycle (daytime restriction, hunger, binge, guilt, vomiting, more restriction)
  • 5. Physical effects (potassium and heart rhythm, teeth, throat, glands; blood test today)
  • 6. Treatment options (guided self-help, regular eating, food diary; CBT-ED if needed)
  • 7. Medication, work and driving (no tablets first-line; confidentiality; blackouts)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, referral, Beat, safety net, review)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

Walk through her weekday

Hour by hour: breakfast, lunch, gym, evening binge, what she eats, how fast

Could you walk me through a normal weekday, food-wise, from when you wake up?
What happens when you get home?
While that's happening, how much do you feel in control?
No breakfast. Gym at lunch instead of eating. Then I get home starving and just keep going.
Not at all. It's like I'm watching myself.

Every other way of purging, by name

Laxatives, water tablets, diet pills, fasting, exercise; insulin if diabetic

Apart from being sick, do you use anything else to get rid of food or calories: laxatives, water tablets, diet pills, or extra exercise?
Do you have diabetes, or take insulin?
What's different at weekends?
Just the gym. And not eating in the day. No pills.
Weekends are weirdly fine. I eat proper meals with my flatmate.

Weight, shape and self-worth

How much weight and shape drive her self-worth; checking; avoiding eating with others

How much does your weight or shape affect how you feel about yourself as a person?
Do you check yourself, in the mirror or on the scales?
Has it stopped you doing things you enjoy?
Massively. I check myself in the mirror all the time.
I've stopped going to dinners with friends.

Get it done

Name it: bulimia nervosa, and the two near misses

Binges + loss of control + vomiting to compensate + shape-driven self-worth; BMI 22 rules out anorexia; purging rules out BED

What you're describing fits a condition called bulimia nervosa.
It isn't anorexia, because your weight is in the healthy range.
It's more than binge eating disorder, because of the vomiting.
So it has a name. I thought I was just disgusting.
I'm an adult. I should be able to control what I eat.
E
Get it done

Draw the cycle from her own week

Skip food, evening hunger, binge, guilt, vomit, promise to eat less, repeat; weekends prove it

When you skip food all day, your body is starving by evening, so it pushes you to eat a lot, fast.
Then the guilt hits, you're sick, and you promise to eat less tomorrow.
Notice your weekends. When you eat regularly, there are no binges.
Oh my God. That's literally my week.
Right.

Get it done

Physical effects and the potassium test

Low potassium, heart rhythm, teeth, throat, swollen salivary glands, knuckle calluses; U&E today; dentist

Being sick often can lower your potassium, which your heart needs for a steady rhythm.
Any palpitations, dizziness or cramps? I'd like to check your potassium with a blood test today.
After being sick, rinse with water and wait an hour before brushing.
Awful heartburn. My cheeks look puffy. No palpitations.
My BMI's normal, though.
G
Get it done

First step: guided self-help and regular eating

Book-based guided self-help, 4 to 9 short sessions over about 4 months; 3 meals + 2 to 3 snacks, no gap over 3 to 4 hours; food and mood diary

The first step is guided self-help: a book-based programme with short sessions with a therapist.
Three meals and two or three snacks, with no gap over three or four hours.
Keep a food and mood diary, to spot what sets binges off.
I don't have time for weekly therapy with my job.
And what does it actually get me to do?
H
Get it done

The weight fear, said before she asks

Aim is regular eating, not weight loss; vomiting removes less than she thinks; weight usually steady

Treatment aims for regular eating, not weight change.
Being sick doesn't get rid of most of what you've eaten.
When people eat regularly, binges fall, and weight usually stays fairly steady.
But if I eat breakfast and lunch, I'll put on loads of weight.
OK. That makes sense, actually.
I
Get it done

Next steps: CBT-ED and where tablets fit

If no progress after ~4 weeks: CBT-ED, about 20 sessions; fluoxetine only as an add-on, at a higher dose than for depression

If guided self-help isn't helping after about four weeks, we move to CBT for eating disorders, about 20 sessions.
Tablets aren't the first treatment. Therapy works better.
Fluoxetine can be added later if therapy alone isn't enough, but never on its own.
Is there a tablet that just stops the binges?
I'd rather not take tablets anyway.
J
Law and safety net

Consent, work and driving

Adult with capacity: voluntary treatment; family or employer told only with consent; OH only if she agrees; no driving if blackouts

This stays between you, our team and your GP unless you decide otherwise.
If you ever want support at work, we can help you plan what to say.
Bulimia doesn't usually affect driving, but if you faint or black out, don't drive and tell us.
I'm terrified work will find out.
Good. Thank you.
K
Law and safety net

Safety net, referral and review

Palpitations, fainting, bad cramps, vomiting blood: 111 or 999; GSH referral; GP letter; Beat; review 4 weeks

If you get palpitations, faint, have bad cramps or vomit blood, call 111 or 999 straight away.
I'll refer you for guided self-help today and write to your GP.
Beat, the eating disorder charity, has a helpline and online groups.
OK. I didn't know vomiting blood could happen.
When will I hear from them?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Lecturing about risks before it feels safe to talk
Lower shame first; risks after the cycle makes sense
Missing laxatives, diuretics, exercise, insulin misuse
Ask about each one by name
Offering fluoxetine as the main treatment
Guided self-help first; fluoxetine only as an add-on
Promising weight loss
Aim is regular eating; weight usually stays steady
No potassium check
U&E today in anyone vomiting daily
Finish
