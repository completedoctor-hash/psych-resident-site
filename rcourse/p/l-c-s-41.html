First-episode psychosis and possible detention: talking to his father

Your place is saved.

Tuesday 18:40 · Paediatric area of A&E, relatives' room

The task

Mr Patrick Doherty has asked to speak with you after you assessed his 16-year-old son Liam in A&E today. Paramedics were called by passers-by and found Liam frightened, pacing up and down the street, talking loudly to himself and barely making sense. In A&E his blood tests, physical checks, ECG and CT head were normal. You think this is most likely a first psychotic episode; Liam has no insight and lacks capacity to decide about the treatment plan. You think he needs admission for further assessment and treatment and are considering detention under the Mental Health Act. Liam lives with both parents; his mother is working an ICU shift and asked you to speak to Patrick. Please explain Liam's diagnosis and plan to him.

Your job: Explain to Josh's father what psychosis is, the treatment plan, early intervention and why Josh may be detained under the Mental Health Act, and answer his fears.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Tell him Liam is safe; ask what he has noticed and fears
  2. Give the normal test results, then name psychosis
  3. Explain psychosis using Liam's own symptoms; answer 'mad' and 'dangerous'
  4. Explain capacity and why parental consent can't keep Liam in
  5. Explain Section 2: 28 days, AMHP, appeal, IMHA, nearest relative
  6. Explain treatment: low-dose antipsychotic, therapy, family work, EIP
  7. Teach-back, leaflets, ward contacts, family meeting date
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Mr Doherty, Liam's dad?
Mr DohertyPatrick. Please, is he alright?
YouHe's safe and being looked after. I know this has been a frightening day.
YouLiam's mum asked me to update you. Can I first ask what you've heard so far?
Mr DohertyNothing. Just that an ambulance picked him up in the street. Why am I seeing a psychiatrist?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (Liam safe, what he knows, his worries)
  • 2. What he has noticed (recent months, school, drugs, sleep, family)
  • 3. Tests and diagnosis (normal results, psychosis explained in Liam's terms)
  • 4. Cause, blame and danger (genes, stress, cannabis; not his fault; frightened, not violent)
  • 5. Why hospital, and capacity (no insight, lacks capacity, young people's unit)
  • 6. The Mental Health Act (Section 2, AMHP, parents can't consent, rights, Tribunal, IMHA)
  • 7. Treatment and outlook (low-dose antipsychotic, CBTp, family work, EIP, recovery)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflets, visiting, family meeting, crisis line)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

What has he noticed?

Months of change: withdrawal, muttering, grades, sport, sleep, odd beliefs

Before I explain, can you tell me what you've noticed in Liam recently?
Has anything else changed, at school or with friends?
What's worrying you most right now?
"He's been in his room for months. Muttering. I thought it was teenage stuff."
"His grades dropped. He quit football. Last week he unplugged the router, said someone was watching."
"That he's hit his head or taken something."
B
Get it done

Tests first, then name it

Bloods, physical checks, ECG, CT head all normal; so this is psychosis

The medical team checked him carefully: blood tests, a physical check, a heart tracing and a brain scan. All of them are normal.
We think Liam has an illness called psychosis. Can I explain what that means?
"So what is it, then?"
"Psychosis? Are you saying he's mad?"
C
Get it done

Psychosis in Liam's own symptoms

Mind struggles to tell what's real; link to pacing, talking to himself, the router; no jargon

Psychosis means that, for now, his mind is struggling to tell what's real from what isn't.
That's why he was so frightened in the street, talking to himself.
How would you describe psychosis in your words?
"God. And I thought he was just being moody. I should have seen it."
"His brain's playing tricks on him, and he believes it."
D
Get it done

Cause, and is he dangerous?

Genes, stress, sometimes cannabis; ask drugs and family history; frightened rather than violent; main risk to himself

There's rarely one cause. Genes, stress and sometimes cannabis add up.
Has Liam used cannabis or other drugs? Any family history of mental health problems?
Most young people with psychosis are frightened, not violent.
"He smoked weed last year, says he's stopped. My mum's sister had a breakdown."
"Is he dangerous? Should we be scared of him?"
E
Law and safety net

Why hospital, and capacity

No insight; lacks capacity to decide; not safe home tonight; young people's unit under s131A

Tonight he isn't safe at home, so we're recommending a young people's psychiatric ward.
Liam doesn't feel he's ill, and right now he can't weigh up the decision about treatment.
"Can I take him home? I'll watch him."
"And he'll agree to go to this ward?"
F
Law and safety net

Why the Mental Health Act, not your signature

16 to 17: parents can't consent to admission against his wishes (s131(4)); if he lacks capacity and it deprives liberty, parents can't consent either (Re D)

Because Liam is sixteen, your consent can't lawfully keep him in hospital.
So we're considering the Mental Health Act.
"Can't I just sign him in?"
"Sectioned? Locked up for ever?"
G
Law and safety net

Section 2, in plain words

Up to 28 days for assessment and treatment; AMHP applies; 2 medical recommendations, one s12; AMHP consults parents

It's called Section 2. It lasts up to twenty-eight days, to assess him and start treatment.
A specialist social worker and two doctors assess him, and the social worker will speak with you before anything is decided.
"So I do get a say?"
H
Law and safety net

Liam's rights, and Patrick's

Told rights (s132); IMHA; Tribunal appeal within 14 days; nearest relative can order discharge with 72 hours' notice; RC can bar if dangerous

Liam will be told his rights, and he'll have a free independent advocate.
He can appeal to an independent tribunal within the first fourteen days.
As his nearest relative, you can ask in writing for his discharge.
"So if I think he's better, I can get him out?"
I
Get it done

Treatment, side effects and outlook

Low-dose antipsychotic, slow increase, weekly side-effect checks; CBT for psychosis; family intervention; EIP up to 3 years

We'd start a medicine called an antipsychotic at a low dose, raise it slowly, and check for side effects every week.
Alongside that, talking therapy for Liam, and family sessions for all of you.
An early intervention team will stay with him for up to three years.
"I don't want him turned into a zombie with drugs."
"Three years? So it's for life?"
"Will he ever be normal again?"
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Lecturing before finding out what he knows
Safety first, then 'What have you noticed?'
Jargon
No 'delusions', 'first-rank', 'section 12'; use Liam's own symptoms
Saying schizophrenia on day one
Say first episode of psychosis
Implying parents can sign him in
At 16, parental consent can't keep him in; MHA protects him
Forgetting appeal and nearest relative
Tribunal within 14 days, IMHA, discharge request
Finish

Close it well

YouBefore you go in to see him, what will you tell Liam's mum?
Mr DohertyThat he's got psychosis, it's treatable, no drugs or head injury. Section 2, 28 days, not for ever. He can appeal, and I can ask for him out.
YouThat's exactly it. Here are leaflets on psychosis and Section 2, with the ward number.
YouThe consultant will ring you both at nine tomorrow, and we'll arrange a family meeting this week. If you're worried tonight, ring the CAMHS crisis line on the leaflet.
