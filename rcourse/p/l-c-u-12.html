Out-of-body spells and odd face movements: is it epilepsy?

Tuesday 14:20, neuropsychiatry outpatients · Clinic room 4, general hospital outpatient block

The task

Funmilayo Bankole, a 22-year-old woman, has a GP referral to the neuropsychiatry outpatient clinic. For two years she has had short, strange episodes in which she feels she is watching herself from above and that everything around her is unreal. Each lasts a few minutes, and onlookers have noticed unusual movements of her face during them. Please take a focused history and explain what you think the most likely diagnosis is.

Your job: Take a focused history of the episodes in about ten minutes, enough to name the most likely diagnosis, then explain it, the tests and the immediate safety advice to Funmi.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask about three aura features: rising stomach, smell or taste, déjà vu or fear
  2. Get the witness account: staring, lip smacking, picking, unresponsive
  3. Ask about confusion, tiredness and memory gaps afterwards, plus frequency and triggers
  4. Sweep febrile convulsions, head injury, meningitis, family epilepsy, drugs
  5. Screen mood, suicidal thoughts, work and driving
  6. Name temporal lobe seizures in plain words; EEG, MRI, urgent neurology; DVLA and water safety
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in this clinic. Are you Funmilayo Bankole?
FunmiYes. Funmi's fine.
YouThanks for coming, Funmi. Your GP mentioned some unusual episodes. They sound unsettling.
FunmiThey really are. I didn't get why I'm seeing a psychiatrist, though. I'm not mad.
YouThat's a fair worry. Our clinic sees symptoms that sit between the mind and the brain. Could you walk me through one, from the very start?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, validate, why neuropsychiatry)
  • 2. Before (rising stomach, smell, déjà vu, fear)
  • 3. During (awareness, witness: staring, lip smacking, picking; generalised features)
  • 4. After (confusion, exhaustion, memory gaps, recovery time)
  • 5. Pattern (frequency, change, sleep, alcohol, stress)
  • 6. Background (febrile convulsions, head injury, meningitis, family, drugs)
  • 7. Other causes (depersonalisation disorder, panic, migraine)
  • 8. Risk and impact (mood, suicidal thoughts, work, driving, water, heights)
  • 9. Summary and close (temporal lobe seizures, EEG, MRI, neurology, DVLA, 999 advice)

Pattern and triggers

Frequency then and now; sleep loss, alcohol, stress, missed meals.

How often do they happen now, compared with two years ago?
Does anything bring them on: poor sleep, alcohol, stress, missed meals?
About once a month at first, twice a month now.
Worse when I'm tired or I've had a drink, so I've stopped drinking.

Background risk factors

Febrile convulsions; head injury; meningitis or encephalitis; family epilepsy; medicines and drugs.

Did you ever have fits as a baby or toddler, maybe with a high temperature?
Any serious head injury, meningitis or brain infection?
Anyone in the family with epilepsy? Any medicines or drugs?
Mum says I had a couple when I was two, with fevers.
No to all of those. I don't take anything.

Depersonalisation disorder (hours, no smell), panic (racing heart), migraine (headache), dissociation.

Do you ever feel detached or unreal for hours at a time, outside these spells?
Any panic, racing heart or headaches with them?
No. Only in the spells.
It's probably just stress, isn't it? I've been tired.

Mood, risk and impact

Cut hours, stopped going out; low mood six months; suicidal thoughts; drives, swims, heights.

How has all this affected your life, work and going out?
How's your mood been? Has it ever got so bad you've thought about wishing you weren't here?
Do you drive, swim or work at heights?
I've cut my hours at the pharmacy. I don't go out. My life's shrinking.
Lower, for six months or so. No, never that. I just want an answer.
I drive sometimes. I passed my test last year.

Get it done

Name it, and meet the fear

Temporal lobe seizures, a type of epilepsy; not mad; nothing points to a tumour.

I think these are small seizures starting in one part of the brain, the temporal lobe. That's a type of epilepsy.
Nothing you've told me points to a tumour, and the scan will check properly.
No, you're not going mad: the smell and the lip movements come from the brain.
Epilepsy? I looked it up. Could it be a brain tumour?
Do you think I'm mad?

Get it done

Tests and referral

Urgent neurology (first seizure clinic); EEG; MRI; GP bloods and ECG; flatmate films a spell.

I'm referring you urgently to the neurologists. They'll do a brain wave test, an EEG, and an MRI scan.
Your GP will do bloods and a heart tracing.
It helps a lot if your flatmate can film the next one on her phone.
What if the brain wave test is normal?

Law and safety net

Driving: DVLA and GMC

Stop driving now; she tells the DVLA; record advice; GMC allows disclosure if she keeps driving, after telling her.

Until neurology have seen you, please don't drive, and tell the DVLA.
I know that's hard. Most people get back to driving once seizures are controlled.
Not drive? I need the car for work.
K
Law and safety net

Everyday safety, valproate and consent

Showers not baths; no swimming alone; 999 if over 5 min or injured; valproate rules; flatmate only with consent.

Showers rather than baths, and no swimming on your own.
If a spell ever lasts over five minutes or you're hurt, call 999.
Is it all right if I share the plan with your flatmate?
Likely epilepsy, scan and EEG, no driving, tell the DVLA, flatmate films one.
Honestly, it's a relief to have a name for it.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling it depersonalisation or anxiety
Ask about smell, automatisms and post-ictal confusion first.
Forgetting driving
Advise no driving, tell the DVLA, and record it.
Promising a normal EEG settles it
A normal interictal EEG is common; history and video matter.
Jargon ('complex partial', 'ictal')
Say 'small seizures in the temporal lobe, a type of epilepsy'.
Ignoring her fear of a tumour or madness
Answer both directly: nothing points to a tumour; the scan will check.
