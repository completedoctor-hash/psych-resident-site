Sudden terrors: panic attacks history to a diagnosis

Tuesday 09:30 · Psychiatric outpatient clinic

The task

Adrian Szabo is a 34-year-old male quantity surveyor seen in a general adult outpatient clinic. His GP referral says he has been having sudden, overwhelming episodes of terror that are ruining his quality of life. Please take a history of these episodes, covering their features, triggers, avoidance, physical and substance causes, mood and risk, with the aim of reaching a diagnosis, and explain it to him.

Your job: Take a history of the attacks to reach a diagnosis of panic disorder: describe the attacks, exclude physical, medication and substance causes and other anxiety disorders, screen for agoraphobia, mood and psychosis, and explain your conclusion briefly.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Take one recent attack in slow motion: onset, peak, duration, frequency, body symptoms
  2. Ask the thought at the peak and check for derealisation
  3. Establish the attacks come out of the blue, incl. from sleep
  4. Ask worry between attacks, what he has stopped, and the 5 agoraphobia situations
  5. Screen GAD, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, health anxiety, mood, suicide, psychosis
  6. Count caffeine, alcohol, drugs; check meds and the GP's tests
  7. Name panic disorder, explain the alarm, refer for CBT, no benzodiazepine
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Adrian Szabo?
AdrianYes. Thanks for seeing me.
YouYour GP mentioned some frightening episodes. Could you talk me through the most recent one?
AdrianIt was on the train last week. I thought I was dying.
YouThat sounds awful. Let's go through it step by step.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, his fear of being fobbed off, the most recent episode)
  • 2. The attacks (onset, peak in minutes, body symptoms, thought at the peak)
  • 3. Pattern and triggers (out of the blue or cued, frequency, night attacks)
  • 4. Between attacks (worry about the next one, what he has stopped, agoraphobia)
  • 5. Other conditions (GAD, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, health anxiety, mood, psychosis)
  • 6. Physical and substances (GP tests, thyroid, medicines, caffeine, alcohol, cocaine)
  • 7. Explain and treat (panic disorder, the alarm, CBT, no benzodiazepine, DVLA)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, referral, review, safety net)

Dizzy. Sweating. And my lips and fingers tingled.

The thought at the peak

Catastrophic thought; derealisation; urge to escape

At the worst point, what goes through your mind?
Do you ever feel unreal, or as if you're watching yourself from outside?
What do you feel the urge to do?
That I'm having a heart attack. That I'm going to die.
This sounds stupid, but the world doesn't feel real. Like I'm going mad.
I have to get out.

Where they happen, any pattern, attacks from sleep

Between attacks, and agoraphobia

Fear of the next one; what he has stopped; the 5 situations

Between attacks, do you worry about having another one?
Is there anything you've stopped doing because of them?
Do you avoid crowds, queues, public transport, open spaces, or being far from home on your own?
I worry constantly. I stopped going to the gym because my heart races.
I turned down a promotion. I couldn't risk an attack in front of my team.
No, none of that. I'm fine on the train, in shops.

Get it done

Name it: the body's alarm

Panic disorder in plain words; honest results; no more tests; teach-back

This is panic disorder. Your body's alarm system is going off when there's no danger.
When the alarm goes off, adrenaline makes your heart pound and you breathe fast. Overbreathing causes the tingling and dizziness.
Can you tell me in your words what's going on?
So it's not my heart?
The tests must have missed something. It feels like a heart attack.
My alarm goes off for no reason, the breathing makes it worse, it's not my heart.

Law and safety net

Treatment by the rules

NICE: CBT first; no benzodiazepine; SSRI if medication; cut caffeine

The treatment that works best is a short course of CBT for panic. I'll refer you.
Tablets like diazepam make panic worse in the long run and can be habit-forming.
If you ever want medication as well, there is a tablet called an SSRI that is licensed for panic.
Cut the caffeine down gradually over a couple of weeks.
Can't you just give me something to take when it starts?
Is it the coffee?

Law and safety net

Driving and the safety net

DVLA advice; crisis numbers; what to document

Have you ever had an attack while driving?
If one ever starts at the wheel, pull over when it's safe, and check the DVLA guidance.
If you ever feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, see your GP or ring NHS 111 and choose option 2.
Not so far. It's usually at my desk or on the sofa.
OK. That's good to know.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Saying 'it's just anxiety'
Explain the alarm and link it to his own symptoms
Calling peak derealisation psychosis
Check it occurs only at the peak and passes
Missing caffeine and stimulants
Count coffees, energy drinks; ask cocaine by name
Forgetting agoraphobia and depression
Ask the 5 situations and mood every time
More heart tests or a benzodiazepine
Share normal results honestly; refer for CBT
Finish

YouI'll refer you for CBT for panic, write to your GP, and see you in eight weeks. Cut the caffeine down gradually.
YouIf you ever feel hopeless or think of harming yourself, see your GP or ring 111, option 2. Any other questions?
AdrianNo. Thank you. I feel taken seriously.
