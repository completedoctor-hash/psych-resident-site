Raw hands and CBT: explaining exposure and response prevention for OCD

Tuesday 14:20, outpatient clinic · General adult psychiatry outpatients

The task

Mr Tomasz Nowak, a 30-year-old warehouse supervisor, was referred to clinic by his GP because he washes his hands excessively, and he was recently diagnosed with obsessive-compulsive disorder. The consultant has referred him to psychology for cognitive behavioural therapy. He has no idea what this involves and would like an overview before his first appointment. Please talk with him about CBT for OCD and respond to any worries he has.

Your job: Find out briefly how the OCD affects him now, explain CBT with exposure and response prevention in plain words, describe how the sessions run, answer his worries honestly, mention medication as an option, and close with written information and next steps.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Count it: washes a day, hours a day, what he uses, effect on work
  2. Ask mood and suicidal thoughts before any explanation
  3. Draw his own cycle: pallet, 'germs', anxiety, wash, relief
  4. Explain ERP in plain words; no unexplained jargon
  5. Validate the fear of anxiety, then show the ladder starts at about 3/10
  6. Give numbers: weekly, ~1 hour, 12–20 sessions; sertraline as an option
  7. Teach-back, OCD-UK leaflet, stop bleach, GP letter, 6-week review

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, his aim, agree the plan)
  • 2. His OCD now (washing count, time, skin, bleach, work, mood, risk)
  • 3. How OCD works (thought, anxiety, wash, relief, the loop, drawn on paper)
  • 4. What CBT with ERP is (facing fears on purpose, not washing, anxiety falls by itself)
  • 5. How sessions run (therapist, weekly, 12–20 sessions, ladder, homework, ratings)
  • 6. His worries (too scary, getting ill, will it work, hospital, Kasia, work)
  • 7. Other options (sertraline alongside, combine if severe)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflet, skin care, review, crisis line)

Washes/day, hours, soap or bleach, skin, work

Your hands look sore. What do you use on them?
How is it affecting work?
Maybe sixty times a day. Three hours, probably. I know it's mad.
Soap mostly. Bleach when it's bad. They sting all the time.
Pallets are the worst. I'm late for shifts. My manager's noticed.

Mood and risk before any teaching

Low mood, hopelessness, self-harm, suicidal thoughts

And your mood lately? How have you been in yourself?
Have you ever felt like hurting yourself, or that life's not worth living?
No, nothing like that. Mood's fine. I just want this sorted.

Draw the cycle with his trigger

Pallet, 'germs', anxiety, wash, relief, urge returns stronger

CBT is the main talking treatment for OCD. Can I draw something?
You touch a pallet. The thought comes: germs, I'll get ill. Anxiety shoots up. You wash, and it drops.
That relief teaches your brain washing kept you safe, so next time the urge is stronger. Does that fit?
That's exactly it. It gets worse every month.
D
Get it done

Explain exposure and response prevention

Face trigger on purpose, then no washing; anxiety peaks then falls

CBT breaks that loop with something called exposure and response prevention.
Exposure means facing a trigger on purpose. Response prevention means not washing afterwards.
It sounds backwards, I know. Every wash tells your brain the danger was real.
So you want me to touch dirty things and not wash? How does that help?
E
Get it done

Meet the fear, then show the ladder

Validate first; ladder rated out of 10; start ~3/10; he chooses

That fear makes complete sense, and nearly everyone feels it at this point. Thank you for saying it.
You won't be thrown in at the deep end. You start with something about three out of ten, like a door handle at home.
You choose each step, and only move up when it feels manageable.
I don't think I could. Honestly, the anxiety would be too much. I'd lose it.
A door handle I could maybe do. Pallets, no way.
F
Get it done

How it runs, with honest numbers

Psychology therapist, weekly, ~1 hr, 12–20 sessions, homework, ratings, relapse plan

A therapist from the psychology team sees you weekly, about an hour, often twelve to twenty sessions, all as an outpatient.
Between sessions you practise most days and rate your anxiety before, during and after.
I can't promise, but nothing we have works better for OCD than this. It's hard work, and it's the treatment that works best.
So how long does it go on for? Who does it?
Do I have to go into hospital?
Will it actually work for me, though?

Kasia's reassurance keeps it going

Partner checking hands works like washing; invite her to a session

Thank you for telling me. She's clearly trying to help.
That reassurance works like washing, though: quick relief that keeps OCD going.
The therapist can invite her to a session so you both plan how she helps instead.
Kasia, my girlfriend, checks my hands for me. Tells me they're clean.
She'd like that, I think.

Medication as an option

Sertraline 50 mg, up to 12 weeks, nausea, sex drive; combine if severe

There's also a medicine some people take alongside therapy, called sertraline. It's optional.
It starts at 50 mg a day and can go up. In OCD it can take up to 12 weeks to help.
Early nausea is common, and it can affect sex drive. The offer stays open.
I'd rather try the therapy first.

Law and safety net

Consent and what NICE recommends

Voluntary therapy; NICE CG31 stepped care

This is your choice at every step. Nothing is forced.
You set the goals with the therapist, and you decide when to move up the ladder.
Who decides what I do each week?
J
Law and safety net

Work, privacy and Kasia's consent

He decides what employer hears; occupational health; consent before partner joins

Only what you choose to tell them.
Occupational health can arrange time for appointments without sharing the diagnosis.
If Kasia comes to a session, the therapist will check you're happy with that first.
Will work have to know? I'd need time off.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Jargon lecture
Use his words and his trigger; explain any technical term in the same breath.
Rushing past his fear, or implying force
Stop, validate, then show the ladder starts low and he chooses each step.
Promising a cure or no discomfort
Say it is hard work and the most effective treatment, without guarantees.
Skipping mood and risk
Ask mood and suicidal thoughts before explaining treatment.
Missing Kasia's reassurance
Ask about family checking; explain it acts like washing; offer a joint session.
