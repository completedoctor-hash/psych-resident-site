Nursery rhymes through the keyhole: voices a week after stopping drink

Monday 14:00, CMHT new patient clinic · Community mental health team, outpatient room

The task

Natalia Petrenko, 49, works as a cook at a primary school. Her GP has referred her to the community mental health team because of a recent decline in her mental health, mainly odd beliefs about her neighbour. The GP letter mentions a history of heavy drinking and abnormal liver tests. She is puzzled about why she has been sent to a psychiatrist. Please take a focused psychiatric history to establish the likely diagnosis.

Your job: Take a focused psychiatric history to establish the likely diagnosis: characterise the experiences, set out the drinking and when it stopped, exclude delirium tremens, a primary psychotic or mood disorder and a medical cause, and check the risk to herself and her neighbour.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Explain why she was referred; get her account in her words
  2. Establish a true hallucination: outside her head, clear, in clear consciousness
  3. Screen other psychotic and mood symptoms, and onset
  4. Alcohol in numbers: amount, duration, why it rose, dependence
  5. Last drink and withdrawal: shakes, sweats, fits, confusion
  6. Build the timeline aloud; exclude DTs, primary psychosis, medical causes
  7. Check risk to the neighbour and herself; explain diagnosis and plan
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Natalia Petrenko?
NataliaYes. I don't know why the doctor sent me here.
YouThat's a fair question. Your GP was concerned about how you've been sleeping and some worries about your neighbour. Can you tell me about that?
NataliaHe sings to me at night.
YouThank you. I'd like to hear it all, and then ask about some other parts of your life.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, why referred, her understanding)
  • 2. The experiences (nursery rhymes, at night, through the keyhole, no one there)
  • 3. Nature of the voice (true hallucination, belief about him, other psychotic symptoms, onset)
  • 4. Alcohol history (two bottles of wine a day, four years, since bereavement, dependence)
  • 5. Stopping drinking (last drink a week ago, shakes, sweats, fits, confusion)
  • 6. Timeline and causes (build it aloud; orientation, attention, drugs, illness, liver)
  • 7. Risk (confronting neighbour, self-harm, work, driving)
  • 8. Plan and the law (thiamine, bloods, alcohol service, consent, DVLA)
  • 9. Summary and close (alcoholic hallucinosis, follow-up, safety net)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

Let her describe it fully

What, when, how often, where from; listen before you mention drink

What exactly do you hear, and when?
How often does it happen?
That must be unsettling, night after night.
Nursery rhymes. Every night, once I'm in bed. Through the keyhole in my front door.

Get it done

Is it a true hallucination?

Heard outside her head, as clear as a real voice, in clear consciousness, no real source (door empty)

Is it as clear as my voice is now?
Where does it seem to come from: outside, or inside your head?
When you open the door, what do you find?
Just as clear. Like he's right outside.
Nobody. The landing is empty. Then I go back to bed and it starts again.

Get it done

Her belief, and what else is there

Why him, and why; other voices, visions, touch; persecution, thought interference; onset four or five nights ago

How do you know it's him? Why do you think he'd do that?
Do you hear any other voices, or see or feel things others don't?
Do you feel anyone is against you, or that your thoughts are interfered with? When did it start?
It's definitely him. I know his voice.
I don't know why. We always got on. I haven't said anything to him.
Four nights ago, maybe five.

Get it done

Alcohol in numbers

Ask how much, not whether; two bottles of red a day ≈ 20 units; four years; morning drinking, cutting down, tolerance

Your GP mentioned your liver tests. What do you usually drink, and how much on a typical day?
How long has it been that much, and what changed?
Did you ever drink first thing to steady yourself, or struggle to cut down?
Red wine. But I'm not an alcoholic; I never get drunk.
Two bottles. Most days.

Get it done

The loss behind the drinking

Husband died four years ago; evenings empty; acknowledge grief; screen mood and enjoyment

How long has it been that much, and what changed?
I'm so sorry. Take your time.
How's your mood been? Are you still enjoying things?
About four years. Since my husband died. The evenings are so empty.
A glass with him after work, then it was a bottle, then two.
Good, apart from the sleep. I love my job with the children.

The last drink and withdrawal

Last drink a week ago, stopped abruptly; shaky and sick 2-3 days; no fits, never confused; how she is now

When did you last have a drink?
Afterwards, did you have shakes, sweats, fits, or feel muddled?
How long did that last, and how are you now?
A week ago today. The GP said my liver was bad, so I stopped completely.
Shaky and sick for two or three days. Then it passed. No fits. Never muddled.
G
Get it done

Build the timeline out loud

Heavy drinking for years → abrupt stop a week ago → withdrawal for 2-3 days → voice began 4-5 nights ago

So you stopped a week ago, the shakes went, and the singing started a few nights later. Is that right?
Yes. I hadn't thought of it like that.
H
Get it done

Rule out delirium and other causes

Orientation, attention, tremor, sweating, pulse; medicines, street drugs, illness, head injury; liver (encephalopathy), glucose; family history

What's the date today, and what's this place? Any trouble with memory?
Could you tell me the months of the year backwards, starting from December?
Any illnesses, regular medicines, street drugs, or anyone in the family with mental illness?
The mental health clinic. My memory's fine.
Nothing. No medicines. Never any drugs. No one in the family.

Get it done

Risk: the neighbour, herself, work

Plans to confront or harm neighbour; self-harm; relapse; school kitchen safety (knives, ovens, children); driving

Have you thought about having it out with him?
Any thoughts of harming him, or yourself?
How are you managing at work in the kitchen with so little sleep?
No. I hate arguments. Never anything like that.
Work's fine. I love it.
J
Get it done

Explain it and plan

Alcoholic hallucinosis; usually fades over days to weeks if abstinent; oral thiamine via GP; bloods; alcohol service; bereavement support; review in 2 weeks; antipsychotic only if it persists

No, you're not going mad. When someone stops after years of heavy drinking, the brain can produce voices that sound completely real.
It's called alcoholic hallucinosis, and it usually fades if you stay off drink.
I'll ask your GP to start vitamin B1 and check bloods, refer you to the alcohol service and bereavement support, and see you in two weeks.
So am I going mad? Will it last forever?
So it's the wine. That makes sense, strangely. What do I do about my neighbour?
K
Law and safety net

Consent, confidentiality, work and driving

Capacity, informal outpatient; employer only via occupational health with consent; risk to neighbour would justify action; DVLA: dependence notifiable

Would you like us to talk to anyone at work, or keep this between you, me and your GP?
Do you drive at all?
Please don't tell the school. I need that job.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Seeing only a row with the neighbour
Always take the drink history in numbers
Calling it schizophrenia
Build the timeline: stop, withdrawal, then the voice
Not checking for confusion, tremor or fits
Test orientation and attention; ask about fits
Forgetting thiamine
Oral thiamine via GP, plus bloods
Missing the bereavement
Ask what changed; offer bereavement support
Finish

Close it well

YouPutting it together: two bottles a day for years, stopping suddenly a week ago, and the singing starting a few days later.
YouIf you become shaky, confused or have a fit, go to A&E or call 999. If the voices get worse, call us or 111, option 2.
YouWhat will you do if things change?
