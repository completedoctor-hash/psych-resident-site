Down's syndrome: low, forgetful and getting lost, a mother's account

Friday 10:30 · Community learning disability team clinic

The task

Owen Brightwell, 39, has Down's syndrome and has always lived with his mother, Bernadette Lyle, who is his main carer. Over the past month she has noticed he seems low and increasingly forgetful, and she has asked the community learning disability team for an urgent review because he has changed so quickly. She has come to clinic with him and is waiting to talk to you while Owen has a drink with a nurse. Please take a collateral history from Ms Lyle with the aim of reaching a likely diagnosis.

Your job: Take a collateral history from Owen's mother to reach a likely diagnosis, covering his baseline, the course of the change, memory, function and mood, physical, sensory and medication causes, seizures and psychosis, risk, and her coping.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Settle Bernadette; confirm Owen has agreed to the conversation
  2. Pin down his baseline and ask about the baseline assessment at about 30
  3. Time the onset: 1 month is fast; sudden or gradual
  4. Screen thyroid, hearing, vision, sleep apnoea, infection, medicines, life events
  5. Ask about fits, myoclonic jerks and psychosis
  6. Cover wandering, home safety, abuse and her own coping
  7. Explain depression versus early dementia; agree tests and review in 4 weeks
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Bernadette, Owen's mum?
BernadetteYes. I'm sorry, I'm a bit on edge.
YouThat's completely understandable. Owen is happy for us to talk. You know him better than anyone.
BernadetteI just want to know what's happening to him.
YouLet's start with what you've noticed, and I'll ask more as we go.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Owen's consent, her worry, plan)
  • 2. Usual Owen (skills, speech, independence, baseline test at 30)
  • 3. Memory and function (days, blanket, lost, buttons, faces, speed)
  • 4. Mood and biology (low, enjoyment, sleep, night walking, appetite)
  • 5. Other causes (thyroid, hearing, vision, sleep apnoea, illness, medicines, life events)
  • 6. Seizures and psychosis (fits, jerks, continence, voices, family history)
  • 7. Risk (wandering, door locking, cooker, self-care, abuse)
  • 8. Her coping (her health, support, carer's assessment)
  • 9. Summary and close (depression versus early dementia, tests, safety, review)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

Get it done

Usual Owen: anchor to his baseline

At his best, self-care, speech, day centre, baseline assessment at about 30

Before this started, what was Owen like at his best?
What could he do for himself?
Has he ever had a memory or skills assessment, maybe around thirty?
Chatty, always laughing. Dressed himself, helped me with the washing up. Football at the day centre twice a week.
Yes, your team did some tests then.
B
Get it done

Memory and function: what, when, how fast

Days muddled, lost blanket, got lost, buttons, recognises family, sudden or gradual

What has he been forgetting?
Has he got lost, or struggled with things he used to manage?
Does he still recognise family? Did it come on suddenly, or creep up over the month?
He thinks it's Saturday when it's Tuesday. And he keeps losing his blanket.
He went out looking for the blanket and got lost. A neighbour brought him back. He can't do his buttons now.
Gradually, but quickly, if that makes sense. Worse each week.

Get it done

Mood and the biological signs

Low, withdrawn, no football, night walking, eating less, crying

How has his mood been? Does he still enjoy football?
How is he sleeping and eating?
Has he cried, or said anything sad?
Low. He won't go. Sits in his room.
Up in the night, walking about. He's eating less too.
D
Get it done

Thyroid, hearing and eyes, every time

Last TFTs, hearing and eye checks, telly loud, cataracts

Some physical problems can cause exactly this and are treatable. When was his thyroid last checked?
Any change in his hearing or eyesight? When were they last tested?
Not for a while. A year or two?
About two years. He does have the telly loud, but he always has.

Get it done

Sleep apnoea, illness, medicines, life events

Snoring, pauses in breathing, infection, falls, head injury, new tablets, changes at home or day centre

Does he snore, or seem to stop breathing at night?
Any recent illness, falls, bumps to the head, or new tablets?
Any changes at home or at the day centre?
Terribly. Sometimes it stops and I hold my breath till he starts again.
No. No tablets. Nothing's changed.

Seizures, jerks and psychosis

Fits, morning jerks, staring spells, wetting; voices, fear; family history

Has he had any fits, sudden jerks, staring spells, or wet himself?
Has he talked to people who aren't there, or seemed frightened of things that aren't real?
Any family history of dementia or depression?
No, never.
No. Nothing like that.
G
Law and safety net

Risk: wandering, home safety, abuse

Got lost; cooker; washing; roads; anyone treating him badly; self-harm; Herbert Protocol

Since he got lost, what have you done to keep him safe?
Any worries about the kitchen, crossing roads, or his washing?
Any concerns about how anyone treats him, or anything he's said about harming himself?
He needs more help washing. I don't let him near the cooker now.
No. He's surrounded by people who love him.
H
Law and safety net

The locked door

Restriction of liberty; proportionate, recorded, reviewed; MCA; door alarm as less restrictive option

You did it to keep him safe, which is caring. We can look at other ways, like a door alarm.
I lock the front door. I feel awful, like I'm imprisoning him.
I
Law and safety net

Her coping

Her health, who else helps, her biggest worry, Care Act carer's assessment

And how are you managing, Bernadette?
Who else helps you?
What worries you most?
My arthritis is bad. It's just me. I'm sixty-eight. What happens to him when I can't cope?
J
Get it done

Explain the differential honestly

Higher dementia risk in Down's; but fast onset plus low mood; treatable causes first; compare with baseline

You're right that the risk is higher. But this has come on fast, his mood is low, and there are treatable causes we haven't checked.
We won't know until we've checked those and compared his memory with the tests at thirty, maybe more than once.
So, is it dementia? I've read people with Down's get it early.
So it might not be dementia?
K
Get it done

The tests and the plan

Bloods incl. TFTs, FBC, B12, folate, glucose, calcium, U&E; urine; audiology; optometry; sleep study; repeat cognitive tests; Owen's capacity for tests

Blood tests including thyroid, a urine test, hearing and eye checks, and a sleep assessment.
Our psychologist will repeat his memory tests. If it's depression, there's treatment that helps.
Yes. What happens now?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Diagnosing dementia on the day
Fast change plus low mood: treatable causes and depression first
Forgetting thyroid, hearing and vision
Ask every time in Down's syndrome
Not asking about seizures or jerks
Ask about fits, morning jerks, staring spells, continence
No comparison with baseline
Ask about the assessment at about 30; compare
Ignoring an exhausted older carer
Ask how she is; carer's assessment
Finish

Close it well

YouSo: a real change, but fast, with low mood. We'll check his thyroid and bloods, urine, hearing, eyes and sleep, and repeat his memory tests against the ones at thirty.
YouFor safety: a door alarm instead of locking, keep him away from the cooker for now, and fill in a Herbert Protocol form. If he goes missing, ring 999.
YouI'll see Owen now and then see you both in four weeks with the results. What questions do you have?
BernadetteNone. That's a relief. Thank you.
