Explaining depot injections to a father after repeated relapse

Wednesday 15:00 · Family room, acute adult inpatient ward

The task

Callum Rennie, aged 25, who has paranoid schizophrenia, came onto the ward a few days ago. He lives with his father, Graham Rennie, who noticed weeks of decline. Callum now believes the police are hunting him again and that his father is helping them, so he will not speak to him. He talks loudly to himself, says he is not ill, and is isolating and neglecting himself. He has never been violent when unwell. His prescription is risperidone 6 mg daily, yet his father found unopened boxes in his room. This is his third relapse this year after stopping medication once he feels better, despite advice from his team and family. He has refused all medication on the ward. Callum has agreed to his father being informed. Please explain depot (long-acting injectable) antipsychotics to Graham and answer his questions.

Your job: Explain long-acting injectable antipsychotics to his father: what they are, the choices, the schedule, test doses, side effects and monitoring, and how consent, Section 3 and a CTO apply. Answer his questions.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Callum's consent; ask what Graham knows and fears
  2. Establish the stop-start pattern and his good response to risperidone
  3. Explain a depot: same medicine, into muscle, slow release, not stronger
  4. Match the choice to his response: paliperidone; give the schedule and who gives it
  5. Explain test doses, side effects, that it can't be removed, and monitoring
  6. Explain consent, Section 3 and the three-month rule, and a CTO, accurately
  7. Check understanding; leaflet, carer's assessment, family meeting

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Callum's consent, acknowledge his efforts)

  • 3. Risk (self-neglect, vulnerability, Graham's safety at home)
  • 4. What a depot is (same medicine, into muscle, slow release)
  • 5. Choices and schedule (paliperidone, others, how often, who gives it)
  • 6. Side effects and monitoring (test doses, can't be removed, sore arm, bloods)
  • 7. Consent and the law (capacity, Section 3, three-month rule, CTO)
  • 8. Support for Graham (guilt, carer's assessment, leaflet)
  • 9. Summary and close (teach-back, ward round, family meeting)

What a depot is

Same kind of medicine; injected into muscle; slow release over weeks; not stronger

A depot is the same kind of medicine as a tablet, given as an injection into a muscle.
The medicine sits in the muscle and releases slowly into the blood over weeks.
It isn't stronger. It's a different way of getting the same medicine in.
So it's not a stronger drug?
E
Get it done

The choice, matched to his response

Paliperidone first; risperidone two-weekly; aripiprazole monthly; flupentixol, zuclopenthixol, haloperidol

Because risperidone helped him, the obvious choice is paliperidone. It's what risperidone turns into in the body.
There are others: a risperidone injection every two weeks, an aripiprazole one monthly, and older ones like flupentixol and zuclopenthixol.
We'd agree the choice with Callum and his consultant.
Are there other kinds?

The schedule, and who gives it

Paliperidone: arm day 1 and day 8, then monthly arm or buttock; three-monthly once stable; ward nurses, depot clinic or care coordinator

Two starting injections in the upper arm, a week apart. Then one a month, in the arm or buttock.
If he stays well for four months, it can go to once every three months.
On the ward, the nurses give it. At home, the depot clinic or his care coordinator, who can come to the house.
How often would he have to have it?
He won't go to a clinic.

Test doses, and why it can't be undone

Test dose for older depots; oral risperidone tolerance for paliperidone; lasts weeks once given

With the older injections, we give a small test dose first and wait.
With paliperidone, the months Callum did well on risperidone tablets already show he tolerates it.
Once it's given, we can't take it out, so we choose carefully and start from what he already knows.
What about a test first? I don't want him reacting badly to something that stays in him for weeks.
And if it doesn't suit him, it's stuck in him?

Side effects and monitoring

Weight, sleepiness, stiffness, restlessness, prolactin; sore site, rotate; weight, BP, glucose, lipids, prolactin at baseline, 3 months, yearly

The side effects are the same as the tablets: possible weight gain, sleepiness, stiffness or restlessness, and hormone changes.
The injection can be sore for a day or two. The nurses rotate the site.
We'd check his weight, blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and prolactin at the start, at three months, and then every year.
And side effects? I don't want to hurt him.
It sounds brutal.
I
Law and safety net

Consent, Section 3 and the three-month rule

Seek consent always; check status; if s3, first 3 months RC can authorise; then T2 or SOAD T3; restraint last resort

We'll try to get his agreement first, and offer him choices.
If he's detained under Section 3, for the first three months of treatment his consultant can authorise medicine even if he refuses.
After that, he must agree with capacity, or an independent second-opinion doctor must approve it.
He'll say no. He's refusing everything here.
So they'd hold him down?
J
Law and safety net

What if he refuses at home? The CTO

No forced injection at home; CTO condition; recall up to 72 hours; capacitous refusal can't be treated in community

Nobody can give an injection at home against his will.
A Community Treatment Order lets him live at home with conditions, such as having the injection.
If he refuses and becomes unwell, his consultant can recall him to hospital for up to 72 hours.
But what happens when he leaves? He won't go to a clinic.
That might be the only thing that works.
K
Law and safety net

Graham's guilt and his rights as a carer

Share within Callum's consent; not his decision; carer's assessment under Care Act; leaflet; family meeting

You're not going behind his back. You're trying to stop a fourth relapse, and the decision is the team's.
The ward will offer you a carer's assessment.
Would you like to come to the next family meeting?
I feel like I'm going behind his back.
Who helps me, though?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Saying a CTO means forced injections at home
No forcing at home; condition plus recall to hospital
Calling it a stronger drug or a punishment
Same medicine, slower release, dose matched to what worked
Forgetting a depot can't be removed
Say so; start from a drug he already tolerates
Lecturing without checking
Pause and ask him to explain it back
Ignoring his guilt
Name it; the decision is the team's
