Low after mania: explaining bipolar depression and treatment options

Friday 14:30, ward review · Adult acute admissions ward, interview room

The task

Mr Jordan Asante is a 26-year-old man recently diagnosed with a manic episode. Six weeks ago his girlfriend brought him to A&E after two weeks of increasingly elated, reckless behaviour, and he was admitted under the Mental Health Act. He refused olanzapine and quetiapine because of weight gain worries but accepted risperidone. His mania settled over three weeks, then he quickly became depressed with marked negative thinking. Sertraline was started and increased to 150 mg daily without much benefit. He and his family are worried about his continuing low mood. Please talk through the diagnosis with him, along with the options for future management.

Your job: Review his current mood and risk, explain that this is bipolar depression and why the current tablets are not working, and discuss medication, psychological and lifestyle options so he can make an informed choice.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Review low mood, sleep, energy, appetite, concentration
  2. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts and plans
  3. Give a clear diagnosis: bipolar disorder, current depressive episode
  4. Explain why sertraline and risperidone aren't lifting him
  5. Offer 2-3 options with doses, monitoring and weight effects
  6. Add therapy, sleep, substances, DVLA, work
  7. Agree his choice, a crisis plan and the next review
The exact words

Your opening

JordanYeah.

JordanPretty rough.
YouCould we talk about what's been happening, what the diagnosis means, and options to help?

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, acknowledge the low, agree the plan)
  • 2. Current picture (mood, sleep, energy, appetite, concentration, shame, suicide, cannabis)
  • 3. The diagnosis (bipolar disorder: mania then depression)
  • 4. Why treatment stalled (sertraline alone, risperidone treats highs)
  • 5. Medication options (taper sertraline; lamotrigine, lithium, quetiapine; weight)
  • 6. Psychological options (CBT, education group, mood diary, family)
  • 7. Lifestyle, law and risk (sleep, cannabis, DVLA, work, consent, crisis plan)
  • 8. Summary and close (his choice, teach-back, leaflets, Amara at review)

Guilt about spending, work behaviour; normalise; offer to plan what to say

Bipolar disorder; mania then depression = same illness; common, treatable

Why the tablets missed

Antidepressant alone poor fit in bipolar depression; can destabilise; risperidone treats highs

Antidepressants on their own don't work as well in bipolar lows, and can sometimes make mood less stable.
Risperidone was great for the high, but it mainly treats highs, not lows.
But why hasn't the sertraline worked?
F
Get it done

Taper sertraline, start lamotrigine

Taper over 1-2 wks; lamotrigine 25 mg x2 wks, 50 mg x2 wks, then 100 mg and up; rash = stop and tell us; weight-neutral

First, I'd suggest tapering off the sertraline over a week or two.
Lamotrigine is good for bipolar lows and doesn't usually cause weight gain.
It's started slowly, twenty-five milligrams a day for two weeks, then fifty, then a hundred. Any rash, you stop it and tell us straight away.
So what can I take that won't make me balloon?
Slow, then. How long before it works?
G
Get it done

Lithium, quetiapine, and the last resort

Lithium best long-term; levels then 3-monthly, kidneys + thyroid; quetiapine works but weight; ECT if severe

For the long term, lithium is the best protection against both highs and lows.
It needs blood tests, often at first, then every three months, plus kidney and thyroid checks.
Quetiapine also works well for the lows, but it can cause weight gain, which I know matters to you.
I'm not sure about lots of blood tests.
Lamotrigine sounds best. I'll think about lithium.

Talking therapy and knowing your signs

CBT for bipolar depression ~16 sessions; bipolar education group; mood diary; family work

Talking therapy also helps: CBT for bipolar depression, about sixteen sessions, works on the hopeless thoughts.
There's a bipolar education group where you learn your early warning signs.
Would a mood diary be something you'd keep?
The group sounds okay.

Law and safety net

Sleep, cannabis, driving and work

Regular sleep protects; cannabis and heavy drinking trigger; tell DVLA after mania; occupational health, phased return

Regular sleep is one of the strongest protectors. Drugs, including weed, and heavy drinking are big triggers.
After a manic episode you need to tell the DVLA and not drive until they say so.
Once your mood lifts, occupational health can help us plan a gradual return.
I don't drive anyway. What about work?

The crisis plan and relapse signs

Who he'd tell; crisis line, 111 option 2, 999; early signs of a high and a low

If those thoughts about everyone being better off get stronger, especially on leave, who would you tell?
the crisis team line, or 111 option 2 at any hour, or 999 in an emergency.
What would warn you a high was coming back?
Amara. Or the nurses.
Not sleeping. Talking fast. Big ideas.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Refusing to give a diagnosis
Say it: bipolar disorder, currently in a low
Increasing the antidepressant
Taper sertraline; explain why
Ignoring his weight worry
Lead with lamotrigine; be honest about quetiapine and olanzapine
Missing lamotrigine rash or lithium monitoring
State titration, rash action, levels, kidneys, thyroid
Skipping suicide because he's on a ward
Ask directly; plan for leave
Finish
