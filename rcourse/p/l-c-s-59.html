Explaining anorexia nervosa to a frightened mother

Monday 11:00 · Adult community eating disorders service, outpatient clinic

The task

Mrs Patricia Haworth is the mother of Ellie, 18, whom you assessed in clinic when her GP was worried she might have an eating disorder. Ellie was a sporty teenager who played netball, ran and swam. In her first months at university she ate a lot of takeaways and gained weight, which upset her because she had always seen herself as slim. She began counting calories, became preoccupied, lost a lot of weight and now exercises out of fear of gaining. She denies vomiting, laxatives or diet pills. She has paused her course with her tutor's agreement and lives at home. ECG was normal; bloods are normal apart from a white cell count of 3.2; BP 95/70, pulse 60, BMI 15. You have diagnosed anorexia nervosa. Ellie accepts she has a problem but fears treatment and weight gain, and has agreed you may share her assessment with her mum. Please explain the diagnosis to Mrs Haworth and answer her questions.

Your job: Explain anorexia nervosa to Chloe's mother, including the physical risks, treatment and outlook, answer her questions, and explain how she can help.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Ellie's consent; ask what Mum knows and fears most
  2. Explain anorexia in plain words using Ellie's own behaviours
  3. Lift guilt: many causes, nobody's fault
  4. Link each physical risk to a real result: pulse 60, BP 95/70, WCC 3.2, BMI 15
  5. Explain weekly monitoring, dietitian and therapy; no tablet
  6. Give Mum a role at home plus Beat, carers' group, Carer's assessment
  7. Teach-back, red flags, 111/999, leaflet
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Mrs Haworth, Ellie's mum?
MotherYes, Trish. Is she alright?
YouThank you for coming, Trish. Ellie has agreed I can share what we found. What have you noticed at home?
MotherShe's so thin and won't sit with us to eat.
YouThat sounds worrying. I'll explain what's going on, then answer your questions.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Ellie's consent, what Mum has seen and fears)
  • 2. The diagnosis (restriction, fear of gain, BMI 15, driven exercise)
  • 3. Why it happens (genes, personality, dieting, university move; no one's fault)
  • 4. Physical risks (heart, low white cells, bones, periods, fertility; weekly checks)
  • 5. Treatment plan (dietitian, CBT-ED or SSCM, no tablet, hospital only if worse)
  • 6. Outlook (about half recover fully; early help counts)
  • 7. Family role (calm meals, illness separate from Ellie, Beat, carers' group)
  • 8. Red flags and close (fainting, chest pain, weakness; 111 or 999; teach-back, leaflet)

What anorexia is, in Ellie's terms

Eating far too little + intense fear of gain; calorie counting, driven exercise, BMI 15

Anorexia is an illness where someone eats far too little and is very frightened of gaining weight.
Ellie counts calories, exercises to stop weight gain, and her weight is well below healthy.
Does that match what you've seen?
Yes. She runs even when it's dark and icy.
She disappears to her room straight after tea.

Why it happens, and lifting guilt

Genes, perfectionism, dieting, stress, the move to university; nobody's fault

There's no single cause. Genes, personality, dieting and stress all play a part.
Starting university and the worry about her weight may have tipped it.
You didn't cause this, and your support now matters a lot.
I had problems with food at sixteen. Did I pass this on to her?
I've always been so careful never to talk about weight.

Physical risks, tied to her results

Pulse 60, BP 95/70, WCC 3.2, ECG normal; heart, bones, periods, fertility

Very low weight strains the heart. Her pulse and blood pressure are on the low side.
Her heart tracing is normal, which is reassuring.
Her white cells, which fight infection, are a little low.
So her heart could stop?
Will it damage her long term?

Weekly checks and the dietitian

Weekly weight, pulse, BP and bloods shared with GP; dietitian next week

Each week we'll check her weight, pulse, blood pressure and blood tests.
We'll share the results with her GP.
She'll see our dietitian next week for a meal plan.
Weekly. Good. Who does that?

Treatment: therapy and a plan, not a tablet

Outpatient; dietitian meal plan; CBT-ED or SSCM for months; no medicine treats anorexia

Most people are treated as outpatients.
A therapist offers a talking therapy made for eating disorders, often weekly for many months.
I wish there were a tablet, but no medicine treats anorexia itself.
Is there a tablet that will fix this?
Why can't she just eat? She knows she's too thin.

Hospital, capacity and the Mental Health Act

Admission only if checks worsen; Ellie presumed to have capacity (MCA 2005); MHA feeding only if detained, last resort

Not at the moment. Most people are treated as outpatients.
If her weight or heart checks get worse, a hospital stay may be needed to keep her safe.
Ellie is an adult, so we'll make these decisions with her.
Will she have to go into hospital?
Can't you just make her eat?

Outlook: honest and hopeful

About half recover fully, many more improve; early help and family support count

Many people recover. About half recover fully, and many more improve a lot.
It takes time, often a year or more.
Starting early, as Ellie has, and family support both help.
Can people fully recover?
That's more hopeful than I thought.

How she can help at home

Regular calm meals together; no diet or weight talk; the illness is separate from Ellie

Keep mealtimes regular and calm, and eat together.
Talk about other things, not food, weight or shape.
When she argues about food, that's often the anorexia talking.
Every meal ends in tears. I don't know what to say to her.
I've been taking it personally.

Beat, the eating disorder charity, has a helpline and advice for parents.

Red flags and the safety net

Fainting, chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, weakness standing or on stairs, rapid loss, self-harm; same day, 111, 999

If she faints, has chest pain or palpitations, or gets breathless, ring us the same day.
If she's confused, or struggles to stand up or climb stairs, that's urgent too.
Out of hours, call 111, or 999 in an emergency.
Fainting, chest pain, struggling to stand. Okay.

Watch out

Traps to avoid

False reassurance that she'll grow out of it
Serious but treatable; about half recover fully, many more improve
Jargon: 'BMI criteria', 'bradycardia'
Weight well below healthy; pulse on the slow side
Missing the guilt cue
Stop, thank her, say clearly she did not cause this
Implying medication treats anorexia
No tablet treats it; meal plan and specialist therapy do
No written information or carer support
Leaflet, Beat number, carers' group, Carer's assessment before she leaves
