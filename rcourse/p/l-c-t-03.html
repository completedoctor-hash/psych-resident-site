Afghanistan memories that won't settle: a PTSD history

Tuesday 10:00, outpatient clinic · Community mental health team, assessment room

The task

Major Owen Fairbairn is a 45-year-old British Army officer referred by his GP to the CMHT. He is struggling to come to terms with events he experienced on operational deployment in Afghanistan some years ago. He has agreed to an assessment but is uneasy about seeing psychiatry. Please take a history from Major Fairbairn to reach a diagnosis, covering the trauma, current symptoms, comorbidity and risk, and explain your conclusions.

Your job: Take a history to reach a diagnosis: outline the trauma without forcing detail, cover re-experiencing, avoidance, numbing, hyperarousal, comorbidity including alcohol, and risk including firearms, then explain your conclusion.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Give him control and answer the chain-of-command worry
  2. Outline the trauma only: what, when, threat to life, gaps, guilt
  3. Check flashbacks feel like the present; nightmares; triggers
  4. Ask avoidance, numbing, and hyperarousal, each with an example
  5. Quantify alcohol; screen mood, guilt, head injury, drugs
  6. Ask suicide, weapons at home and work, anger at home
  7. Name PTSD; trauma-focused CBT works; consent-based referral

Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, reluctance, control, confidentiality)
  • 2. The trauma (outline only, when, threat to life, gaps, guilt)
  • 3. Re-experiencing (reliving now, nightmares, triggers, body)
  • 4. Avoidance and numbing (places, news, talk, cut off, lost interests)
  • 5. Hyperarousal (startle, on guard, sleep, temper, concentration)
  • 6. Comorbidity (alcohol, mood, guilt, head injury, drugs)
  • 7. Risk and function (suicide, weapons, anger at home, work)
  • 8. Explain and close (PTSD, therapy works, referral, law, safety net)

Normalise reluctance; confidentiality and the chain of command

Lots of people feel like that at first. Coming here doesn't make you a head case.
What you tell me stays confidential unless you agree, or there's a serious risk to you or someone else. I'll tell you before anything is shared.
My wife and the GP rather insisted. And I don't want this going to my CO.
I'm not a head case.

The trauma, outline only

What, when, threat to life, memory gaps; no forced detail

You don't need to go into detail. Could you give me a rough idea of what happened, and when?
Were you or others in danger of being killed or badly hurt?
Do you remember all of it, or are there gaps?
Convoy. An IED went off under the vehicle in front. Two of my lads were killed.
The next few hours are a blur. They were my lads. I chose that route.

Re-experiencing: is it happening now?

Intrusive images, reliving in the present, nightmares, triggers, body

Do memories of it come back when you don't want them to?
When they come, is it like remembering, or does it feel as if it's happening again, right now?
And at night, do you have nightmares? What sets the memories off?
All the time. Pictures of the vehicle. Of them.
Like I'm back there. Fireworks, a car backfiring, and my heart's pounding, I'm sweating, I can smell it.
Most nights. Same one. Riding along, then the bang.

Avoidance

Places, news, being driven, talking about it

Are there things you avoid because they bring it back: places, the news, talking about it?
Is there anything you won't do now that you used to?
I don't watch the news. I won't be driven by anyone else. And I don't talk about it. Ever.

Numbing and feeling cut off

Detachment from family; lost interests; negative beliefs

How do you feel with your wife and children? Close, or cut off?
What do you enjoy now? Anything you've stopped?
Like I'm behind glass. I know they love me. I just can't feel it.
I've stopped running too.

Hyperarousal

Startle, hypervigilance, sleep, temper, concentration, work

How do you react to sudden noises? Do you find yourself on guard, checking exits?
How are your sleep, temper and concentration?
I hit the deck in Tesco when a pallet dropped. I always sit facing the door. Like now.
Can't sleep before two. Short fuse. I can't read a page. I've been on sick leave four months.

Get it done

Alcohol, plainly and without judgement

Quantity, timing, dependence signs, drugs

Lots of people find ways to cope with nights like that. Some use a drink. How much do you have on a typical night?
Any shakes in the morning, or drinking earlier in the day? Any drugs?
Honestly? Half a bottle of whisky. It's the only thing that gets me to sleep.
No shakes. Never before evening. No drugs.

Mood, guilt and head injury

Low or numb; self-blame; blast head injury

Some people blame themselves after something like this. Do you?
Would you call your mood low, or more numb?
Did you lose consciousness or hit your head in the blast?
I chose the route.
Numb. Not sad exactly.
I wasn't hurt. Not a scratch. That's the worst bit.

Suicide, weapons and anger at home

Thoughts, plans, firearms access, temper with family

When things are this bad, has it ever crossed your mind to end your life? Any plans, ever?
Do you have access to any weapons, at home or at work?
Has your temper ever spilled over at home, towards your wife or the children?
Sometimes I think they'd be better off. But no. I wouldn't do that to the kids.
No plans. No guns at home since last year. I'm on leave, so no access at work.
I shout. I've never laid a finger on anyone.

Name it and give hope

PTSD in plain words; alcohol as coping; trauma-focused CBT works

What you've described is called post-traumatic stress disorder: the nightmares, the reliving, avoiding reminders, feeling cut off and always on guard.
It's a normal brain reacting to an abnormal event. Plenty of very capable soldiers get it.
A talking therapy called trauma-focused CBT helps most people, usually eight to twelve weekly sessions. Cutting down the drink alongside helps it work.
So I'm a head case after all.
Is there anything that actually works, or do I just live with it?
I don't want tablets turning me into a zombie.

Law and safety net

Confidentiality, firearms, DVLA, military care

Consent to share; GMC firearms disclosure; DVLA; DCMH

With your agreement, I'll refer you through your military GP to the Department of Community Mental Health, and write to your GP. Nothing goes to your CO without your say-so.
If things get bad, you can ring the Combat Stress helpline any time, or NHS 111 option 2.
Right. Who does it?
It's PTSD, there's a therapy for it, I cut the whisky down, and I ring someone if it gets bad.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Pushing for a detailed account of the blast
Outline only; give him control
Asking only 'any flashbacks?'
Ask if it feels like it's happening now
Skipping alcohol, or judging it
Ask it as coping; get units; check dependence
Not asking about weapons
Ask about access at home and at work
Ending without hope
Say trauma-focused CBT works for most people
