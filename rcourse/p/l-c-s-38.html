Explaining Isla's autism diagnosis to her mother

Wednesday 15:30 · CAMHS neurodevelopmental clinic

The task

Ms Helen Fraser is the mother of Isla, aged 14, who has just finished an autism assessment. Her parents sought help because Isla seemed different socially from other girls her age: her friendships centre on shared hobbies and she shows little curiosity about other people's lives, including her mother's. Her Social Communication Questionnaire score was 16, just above the cut-off of 15, and an autism-specific observational assessment followed; she narrowly met the criteria for autism spectrum disorder. Isla has agreed that her mother can attend alone and that all information can be shared. Please explain the diagnosis and its management to Ms Fraser.

Your job: Explain the autism diagnosis to Isla's mother: what it means for Isla, why it was recognised late, its causes, co-occurring conditions to watch for, support at school and home, and next steps including how Isla hears it.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Isla's consent to share; ask what Helen knows and wants
  2. Give the headline, then pause for her reaction
  3. Explain autism as a lifelong difference, with Isla's strengths
  4. Explain late recognition in girls, causes, and Asperger's
  5. Screen anxiety, mood, self-harm, sleep, eating, attendance
  6. No medicine for autism itself; agree SENCO adjustments and family support
  7. Agree how Isla hears it; teach-back and safety net
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the children's team. Are you Ms Fraser, Isla's mum?
Ms FraserYes, Helen.
YouThanks, Helen. Isla has said she's happy for me to share everything with you today.
YouBefore I explain the results, could you tell me how you've been feeling about the assessment?
Ms FraserNervous. I've got a list. Is it autism, and if it is, why did nobody notice for ten years?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (Isla's consent, what she knows, her list)
  • 2. The result (autistic, narrowly met, SCQ 16, observation)
  • 3. What autism means (social communication, flexibility, sensory, strengths)
  • 4. Why now, and causes (masking in girls, Asperger's, genes, no blame)
  • 5. Co-occurring problems (anxiety, mood, self-harm, sleep, eating, school)
  • 6. What helps (no tablet for autism, adapted CBT, sleep plan)
  • 7. School and family (SENCO, Equality Act, EHC, parent group, carer's assessment)
  • 8. Telling Isla and close (her pace, her choice, teach-back, report, safety net)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Law and safety net

Consent first, then her agenda

Isla agreed to sharing; record it; what Helen knows; what she most wants

Isla's happy for me to share everything with you today.
What do you already know about autism?
What would you most like to leave with?
"Bits from the internet. Asperger's, what causes it, whether there's a tablet, and what school will do."
B
Get it done

The headline, then silence

'The assessment shows Isla is autistic.' Pause. Name relief and sadness

The assessment shows that Isla is autistic.
How does that sit with you?
"Right. I thought so."
"Relief, honestly. And a bit sad. I feel I should have pushed sooner."
C
Get it done

What autism means for Isla

Reading people, conversation, change, sensory; lifelong; strengths: focus, honesty

It means her brain works differently in how she reads people, makes conversation and copes with change.
It's lifelong and part of who she is, not an illness she caught.
Does that fit the Isla you know?
"That's why her friends are all from her hobbies. And she never asks about my day."
D
Get it done

Why at fourteen, and Asperger's

Girls copy and mask; secondary school social demands; Asperger's is now autism

Many girls watch and copy others and hide their difficulties.
In secondary school, friendships get more complicated, and keeping up becomes exhausting.
Asperger's was the old name for autism without language delay.
"My friend's boy was diagnosed at four."
"That's exactly Year 7. She came home and just shut down."
"So she hasn't got mild autism?"
E
Get it done

What causes it, and lifting the blame

Mostly genetic, many genes; not vaccines, diet, parenting, working; family traits

Autism is mostly genetic, from many genes working together.
It isn't caused by parenting, diet or vaccines. Nothing you did caused this.
Is anyone in the family a bit like Isla?
"I did wonder about the vaccines. And I worked full time when she was small."
"Her dad, actually. Routines, hates parties."
F
Get it done

Screen what comes with it

Anxiety, low mood, self-harm, sleep, eating, ADHD, school attendance

Autistic young people more often have anxiety, low mood, sleep and eating difficulties. How is Isla with those?
How are her sleep and eating? Is her weight steady?
Has she ever talked about hurting herself, or wishing she wasn't here?
"Anxious, definitely. She refuses school some Mondays. Says she's exhausted."
"Awake an hour or two most nights. Same few foods, but her weight's fine."
"No. She says she hates being weird."
G
Get it done

What helps: no tablet for autism

No medicine for core autism; adapted CBT for anxiety; sleep plan, then melatonin

There's no medicine for autism itself.
We'll offer CBT adapted for autistic young people for her anxiety, and a sleep plan.
"Is there a tablet that helps?"
"So no medication for the autism. OK."
H
Law and safety net

School: adjustments and the law

SENCO letter with consent; time-out card, quiet space, warning of change; Equality Act; EHC needs assessment

With your and Isla's consent, I'll write to the SENCO.
We'd suggest a time-out card, a quiet space at lunch, and warning before timetable changes.
"They'd listen if it came from you."
I
Law and safety net

Support for Helen, and the long view

Post-diagnostic parent group, National Autistic Society, carer's assessment; adult outcomes

There's a parent group after diagnosis, and you can ask the council for a carer's assessment.
Will she manage as an adult? Many autistic adults live independently, work and have relationships.
"Will she be able to live on her own, have a job?"
J
Law and safety net

How Isla hears it

Isla owns the diagnosis; her pace; who is there; Gillick for each decision; who she tells

How would Isla like to hear this, and who should be there?
She can decide who else she tells.
"She'd want to read the report herself first, then talk. Maybe with me."
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling it a disease or promising a cure
Lifelong difference; support, not cure
Only deficits
Name strengths: focus, honesty, loyalty
Missing anxiety, school avoidance or eating
Screen each; follow up at every visit
Forgetting Isla owns the diagnosis
Agree how, when and from whom she hears it
Finish

Close it well

YouTo check I've been clear, what will you tell Isla's dad tonight?
Ms FraserShe's autistic, it's genetic, not our fault. No tablet for it, but help for her anxiety and sleep, and a letter to school.
YouExactly right. You'll get the written report, and so will your GP.
YouIf she starts self-harming, loses weight, or stops going to school, ring us. In an emergency, 111 or A&E.
