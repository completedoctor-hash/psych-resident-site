After CBT didn't help: is IPT right for Professor Iyer?

Lesson 8 of 10 · about 14 minutes. Press play and listen: the part being taught lights up on the page, so you never need to read unless you want to.

Your place is saved.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Thursday 10:00, outpatient clinic · Community mental health team, clinic room

The task

Professor Rohan Iyer is a 64-year-old retired engineering lecturer who self-referred to NHS Talking Therapies two months ago with mild to moderate depression. He attended eight sessions of CBT and took part well, but felt little better and was discharged. In the discharge letter to his GP, though not to him, the therapist suggested he might suit interpersonal therapy (IPT) if further therapy were tried. He went back to his GP asking for more help, and the GP has referred him to your community mental health team on that basis. This is his first appointment with you. Please explain what IPT is and when and why it is used, and assess and discuss whether it suits him.

Your job: In about ten minutes, check his mood and safety, find which IPT focus areas apply to him, explain what IPT is and why it might help, check his suitability, and agree a shared decision.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Record PHQ-9 (14) and ask directly about suicidal thoughts, alcohol and psychotic symptoms
  2. Ask what CBT was like before explaining IPT
  3. Ask about all 4 IPT areas; name the 2 that apply (role transition, role dispute)
  4. Explain IPT in 3 points: mood and relationships link, here and now, 12-16 weekly sessions in 3 phases
  5. Answer 'why not first?': both NICE-recommended, no therapy suits everyone
  6. Check suitability, agree one main focus, referral, GP review and a crisis number
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Professor Iyer?
RohanI am. Rohan, please.
YouThank you, Rohan. I understand the CBT didn't help as much as you'd hoped, and your GP mentioned another talking therapy.
RohanYes, something called IPT. I'd never heard of it.
YouI'll explain it, but first, in the next ten minutes or so, could I hear how you've been and what's been happening in your life?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, acknowledge CBT, agree the plan)
  • 2. Mood now (two weeks, sleep, interest, PHQ-9 14)
  • 3. Risk (suicidal thoughts, alcohol, psychotic symptoms)
  • 4. Life and relationships (what CBT was like, retirement, home)
  • 5. The four IPT areas (grief, dispute, transition, isolation: which fit)
  • 6. What IPT is (mood and relationships, here and now, why not first)
  • 7. Suitability (links mood to events, motivation, weekly attendance)
  • 8. Practicalities (12-16 sessions, 3 phases, wait, wife, tablets)
  • 9. Summary and close (decision, teach-back, referral, crisis numbers)
A
Get it done

Mood now, with a number

Last two weeks: mood, sleep, interest, concentration, energy; PHQ-9 = 14 (moderate)

How's your mood been over the last two weeks?
How are sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and enjoyment?
Your questionnaire came to 14, which is moderate. Does that sound right?
Flat, mostly. I sleep badly, I can't concentrate on a book, and nothing much interests me.
I suppose it does.
B
Law and safety net

Ask about risk, even when he seems mild

Suicidal thoughts and plans; alcohol; unusual experiences; protective factors

Have you felt life isn't worth carrying on with, or thought of hurting yourself?
How much do you drink?
Any unusual experiences, like hearing things others don't?
No. Never. I'm low, not desperate.
A glass of wine at the weekend. Nothing like that.
C
Get it done

What was CBT like?

Ask before explaining IPT; his answer tells you what to stress; handle 'I failed'

What was the CBT like for you?
It sounds like you worked hard at it and it missed the thing that mattered most.
You didn't fail. Not every therapy suits every person.
I did the worksheets diligently. But it felt abstract. Nobody asked what had actually changed.
Exactly. I felt I'd failed at it.
D
Get it done

When it started: the role transition

Onset after retiring; loss of role, routine, colleagues, identity

When did this start, and what was going on then?
That's a huge change. What did you lose when you retired?
And what's new in your life since?
Last spring, soon after I retired. Forty years in the department. Students, colleagues, coffee at ten. Then nothing.
E
Get it done

Home: the role dispute

Arguments with his wife about how he fills his days; mismatched expectations

How are things at home?
That sounds really painful, losing a big part of who you were and then feeling unwelcome at home.
What does each of you expect your days to look like now?
We argue. My wife says I'm under her feet. Forty years of teaching, and now I'm just in the way. (voice catches)
F
Get it done

Rule out grief and isolation

Ask about all 4 areas; IPT grief means a death, not retirement; friendships before retiring

Have you lost anyone close to you in the last few years?
Before you retired, did you have people you felt close to?
Do you find it easy to make friends?
No. My parents died long ago. No one recently.
Plenty, through work. I've always got on with people. I just don't see them now.
G
Get it done

What IPT is, and why not first

Mood and relationships affect each other; here and now; time-limited; both NICE-recommended

IPT looks at how mood and relationships affect each other, especially around big life changes.
It stays in the here and now, not your childhood, and it's time-limited.
It isn't better: both are recommended and both work. CBT is usually tried first.
If it's better, why wasn't I offered it first?
I see. That's reassuring, actually.
H
Law and safety net

Be open about the letter

IPT suggestion was in the discharge letter to his GP, not to him; he can see his records

You may wonder where the idea came from. Your CBT therapist suggested IPT in the discharge letter to your GP.
I'm sorry that wasn't shared with you directly.
You're entitled to see your records, including that letter, if you'd like.
Nobody told me that. I'd have liked to know.
I
Get it done

Is he suitable? Four checks

Links mood to events; motivated; can attend weekly; no contraindication; his goal

You've linked your mood to retiring yourself, which is exactly what IPT builds on.
Could you come weekly for about four months, and do some work between sessions?
What would you most like to be different by the end?
Yes. I've nothing but time.
I'd like to feel useful again. And to stop the rows.
J
Get it done

The numbers, and one main focus

12-16 weekly sessions of ~50 min; 3 phases; ~8-week wait; GP monthly; one main focus

It's 12 to 16 weekly sessions of about 50 minutes with a trained IPT therapist.
First we map your relationships and agree the focus, then work on it, then plan for setbacks.
I'd suggest the main focus is retiring, with the rows at home as part of that.
That sounds manageable.
K
Law and safety net

His choice, and his wife

Options: IPT, tablets, both, watchful waiting; wife joins only if he agrees; carer's conversation

At your level, therapy alone is a reasonable choice; some people prefer both. It's your decision.
You could bring your wife to one session if you'd like. Only if you'd like.
She could also have a conversation with us in her own right as a carer.
Do I need tablets as well?
I'd rather just do the therapy for now.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Explaining IPT before hearing his story
Ask about CBT, onset and home first; then explain
Saying IPT is better than CBT
Both are recommended; it's about fit
Calling retirement 'grief'
In IPT, grief means a death; retirement is a transition
Choosing all four areas as the focus
Agree one main focus: role transition
Skipping risk because he seems 'only mild'
Ask directly about suicide, alcohol and psychosis
Finish

Close it well

YouShall we go ahead with IPT, focusing on retiring and how things are at home?
YouHow would you sum up IPT, so I know I've explained it clearly?
RohanWeekly sessions for a few months, about how retiring changed things and the arguments with my wife.
YouExactly. If you ever have thoughts of hurting yourself, call 111 and choose option 2, or the Samaritans, free, on 116 123.
YouI'll send the referral today, copy your GP, and give you a leaflet.
