Ready for discharge, but he can't go home: planning where next

Your place is saved.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Thursday 11:00, ward round follow-up · Older adults psychiatric ward, quiet room

The task

Mr Bernard Kowalski is a 74-year-old man on an older adults psychiatric ward. He was arrested after assaulting his wife; police raised concerns about his memory and mental state, and he was bailed to hospital. He was treated with mirtazapine, then trazodone, for low mood and poor sleep, and after several weeks his mood is better. A CT head showed only age-related atrophy and other physical tests were normal. He no longer needs hospital care. His wife and son have now told the ward that he has drunk heavily for years and been repeatedly violent to family, recently to his wife. She will not have him home and has an injunction stopping him returning, which their children support. Please talk to Mr Kowalski, explore his care needs, plan his discharge and explain your reasons to him. Do not take a history or carry out a risk assessment.

Your job: In about ten minutes, tell Bernard he cannot return home, hear his reaction, establish what he can do for himself day to day and with money, explore where he would like to live, and agree realistic discharge options with your reasons.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Tell him about the court order in one clear sentence, then pause
  2. Hold his reaction without arguing the facts or judging
  3. Go task by task: washing, dressing, tablets, cooking, shopping, bills
  4. Record his preferred place to live and his main worry
  5. Offer 2–3 options, each with one benefit and one barrier
  6. Name owners: social worker (Care Act, housing), OT, police and solicitor (bail)
  7. No history, no risk assessment: both are done
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the ward psychiatrists. Are you Bernard Kowalski?
BernardYes. When can I go home?
YouThat's exactly what I'd like to talk about, Bernard. You've made real progress with your mood.
BernardI feel much better. Sleeping too.
YouThere's some difficult news about home too. Can we talk about what you'll need and plan it together? It'll take about ten minutes.
BernardWhat news?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, praise progress, warning shot, ten minutes)
  • 2. Breaking the news (his understanding, court order, children agree, pause, his reaction)
  • 3. The injunction (can't go home, no contact beyond the order, contact with son)
  • 4. Daily living (washing, dressing, tablets, cooking, laundry, shopping)
  • 5. Money and capacity (bills, pension, cash; decision about where to live)
  • 6. Accommodation options (his wish, his fear, 2–3 options with benefit and barrier, referrals)
  • 7. Alcohol and legal (how much, alcohol nurse, thiamine; bail address, solicitor)
  • 8. Follow-up and close (mood, memory, social worker, OT, discharge meeting, teach-back)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Check what he understands first

Why he came in, tests, tablets; note memory gaps, don't test now

Before I explain, can you tell me what you understand about why you came into hospital?
What's happened since you've been here: any tests, any new tablets?
You seem a bit unsure about some of it. Have you noticed problems with your memory?
Some trouble at home. The police came. I had a scan, I think. Some tablets.
It's not what it was. Halina remembers everything for me.
B
Get it done

Say the news plainly, then stop

Court order, can't go home, children support it; then silence

I'm afraid what I have to tell you is difficult.
Halina has got a court order, which means you can't go back to the house. Tomasz and your daughter support her.
She and Tomasz have told us about drinking, and about violence over the years. I'm sorry. This must be a real shock.
(silence) A court order? Against me?
Forty-eight years... over one argument?
C
Get it done

Hold the reaction, park the side issues

Denial, minimising, jealous belief: acknowledge, don't argue, tell the team

I can hear how hard this is to take in. It's not for me to judge what happened; that's for the court.
What are your thoughts on what I've just said?
It sounds like that's been on your mind a lot. I'll make sure the team knows. Today I want to focus on where you'll live.
I've never hurt her. Not really.
She's been seeing someone. I'm sure of it. That's what all this is about.
D
Law and safety net

The injunction is a court order

No return home; no contact beyond the order; breach is criminal; house rights = solicitor

I understand why that feels so unfair. The court order does mean you can't go back for now, and going there could get you into serious trouble.
Your solicitor can explain your rights about the house.
Tomasz supports his mum's decision, so that isn't possible right now. I'll make sure you can stay in touch with him if he's happy to.
It's my house! She can't stop me going to my own house.
Can't I just go to Tomasz's for a bit?
E
Get it done

Daily living, one task at a time

Washing, dressing, tablets, cooking, laundry, shopping; name the gaps

What did Halina do for you at home, and what did you do yourself?
Can you wash and dress yourself? And who sorted your tablets?
Have you ever cooked a meal, or done the washing or the shopping yourself?
I wash and dress myself, always have. Tablets, she put them out for me.
Toast. That's about it. I've never used the washing machine.
F
Get it done

Money

Bills, bank, pension, paying in shops; benefits check; LPA later

Who looked after the bills and the bank?
Do you know roughly what your pension is each month?
Could you pay for things in a shop and keep track?
Halina. I couldn't tell you what comes in. I just get cash when I need it.
G
Law and safety net

His capacity to choose where to live

Presumed capacity (MCA 2005); if doubt, assess this one decision

If you lived in a flat on your own with no help, what would a normal day look like?
What would happen with your meals and your tablets?
I'd manage. Well, I'd have toast.
I don't know. Halina did all that.
H
Get it done

His wishes, then options with a benefit and a barrier

Flat alone (not safe yet), extra-care/sheltered housing, residential home as fallback

It sounds like you'll need a fair bit of help each day. If home isn't possible, where would you like to be?
What worries you most about leaving?
One option is sheltered or extra-care housing: your own front door, staff on site, carers for meals and tablets. The barrier is waiting lists.
What do you think?
My own place, I suppose. With someone popping in. I don't want to die in some home.
The flat with staff. If they'll have me.
I
Law and safety net

Care Act and housing referrals

Care Act 2014 needs assessment; Homelessness Reduction Act 2017 duty to refer, with consent

Our social worker will look at what care you need. Is that alright with you?
Because you can't go back to the house, you count as at risk of homelessness. Can we refer you to the council's housing team?
Homeless? Me? I've paid tax all my life.
Yes, all right.
J
Law and safety net

Alcohol, then the bail address

4–5 cans most days: alcohol nurse, thiamine. Bailed to ward: officer in the case + solicitor agree new address

How much do you usually drink?
It's your choice, but drinking has played a big part in where you are now, so I'd really encourage you to see the alcohol nurse.
Your bail says you live here, on the ward, and that has to change before you can leave. Can we speak to your solicitor and the police?
Most days. Four or five cans. More at the weekend.
Do I have to see her? The alcohol one?
I thought all that was finished.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Taking a history or doing a risk assessment
Both are done: note new concerns, pass them on, return to the plan
Softening the injunction
Say 'court order' and 'can't go back' once, clearly
'He washes and dresses, so he'll cope'
Ask task by task: tablets, meals, laundry, money
One option, or none
Two or three options, each with a benefit and a barrier; record his choice
Forgetting bail and housing
Officer in the case + solicitor; housing referral with consent
Finish

Close it well

YouOur social worker will look at what care you need and refer you to the council's housing team, as you agreed. Our OT will see how you get on in the kitchen and with tablets.
YouWe'll speak to your solicitor and the police, because your bail address has to change first. And the alcohol nurse will come and see you.
YouSo what happens next, in your words?
BernardThe social worker, the kitchen test, the solicitor. And I can't go home.
YouExactly. We'll meet again at the discharge meeting next week, and you won't leave until there's a safe place ready. If you're struggling before then, tell the nurses.
