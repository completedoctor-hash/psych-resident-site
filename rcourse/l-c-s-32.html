Old Age Psychiatry · Mood and psychosis in later life

'My insides are rotten': psychotic depression at home

Your place is saved.

▶ Play ❚❚ Pause

Thursday 11:30, urgent home visit · Her terraced house; older adult community mental health team

The task

Mrs Gwen Pryce, a 78-year-old widow, is visited urgently at home by a doctor from the older adult community mental health team. Over the past few weeks her GP and her daughter have become very concerned: she has almost stopped eating and drinking and has started saying strange things, insisting that her body is rotting inside. She lives alone in a terraced house in a small town. Please take a history from Mrs Pryce, including a careful risk assessment.

Your job: Take a focused history and mental state, test how firmly the 'rotten insides' belief is held, assess risk including food and fluid intake, and decide how urgently she needs treatment.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Sit at her level and start with her loss
  2. Cover low mood, anhedonia, early waking, diurnal variation, weight
  3. Explore the rotting belief: evidence, certainty, effect on eating
  4. Screen guilt, poverty, voices, smells; check attention (months backwards)
  5. Count food and fluid for three days; urine, dizziness, falls
  6. Do a lying and standing blood pressure and pulse
  7. Ask about death wishes, plans, tablets; decide urgency and where she goes
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors from the older people's team. Are you Mrs Pryce?
Mrs PryceYes... you needn't have bothered.
YouI'm glad to meet you. Your GP and your daughter are worried you've been very unwell lately. May I sit here, next to you?
YouCould you tell me how things have been for you these past few weeks?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, why visiting, seated at her level, her loss)
  • 2. Mood and biology (low mood, no pleasure, early waking, mornings, weight)
  • 3. Nihilistic beliefs (rotting, evidence, certainty, guilt, voices, smells)
  • 4. Food and fluid (three days' intake, urine, dizziness, falls, BP)
  • 5. Risk (death wish, plans, tablets, self-neglect, alone)
  • 6. Physical and cognition (attention, delirium, cancer fear, new tablets)
  • 7. Past and support (previous depression, what helped, mirtazapine, Susan)
  • 8. Summary and close (severe depression with psychosis, hospital today, daughter)

Decide the urgency, with her

Hospital today: fluids and tests first, then the mental health ward

Law and safety net

Informal, or the Mental Health Act

Capacity to agree, AMHP, Section 2

Do you understand why we think you need hospital today?
If you stayed at home, what do you think would happen?
Hospital today. For water and tests.
K
Law and safety net

Fluids, ECT and consent

MCA for dehydration treatment; ECT, SOAD, Section 62

Will she have to have electric shock treatment?
Nothing happens without careful discussion.
Is this dementia, doctor? She keeps losing track of what she's saying.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Fluid left to the end
Count three days' intake early; do a postural BP
Arguing with the delusion
Explore certainty; acknowledge it feels real
Calling it grief
Look for anhedonia, early waking, delusions: severe depression
Accepting 'no plans'
Weigh the passive death wish and food refusal as lethal risk
Finish

Close it well

YouI think you have a severe depression. It's making your body feel this way, and it can be treated.
YouBecause you're hardly drinking, you need hospital today, first for fluids and blood tests, then our ward.
YouCan you tell me what's happening next? Could you try a few sips of water with me now?
