Wednesday 14:00 · Older adult outpatient clinic

The task

Dr Leonard Okoro, a 67-year-old retired pathologist, has been referred to the older adult outpatient clinic by his GP, who visited the house and was worried about the state of his home. He lives alone in a large house since his wife died eight years ago. He has three adult children; his youngest daughter has raised concerns with the GP. He is puzzled about the referral but curious to meet a psychiatrist. Please take a history and carry out a mental state assessment.

Your job: Take a history and carry out a mental state assessment, establishing whether this is hoarding disorder, screening for related conditions, and assessing the fire and other risks in his home.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Build respectful, curious rapport; explain the referral without judgement
  2. Establish what he keeps, how many, since when, the link to his wife
  3. Map the house room by room, including the kitchen and exits
  4. Explore discarding, acquiring and insight
  5. Screen OCD, psychosis, autism, depression and dementia
  6. Ask about smoking, heaters, the cooker, smoke alarms and blocked doors
  7. Complete the MSE; explain hoarding disorder; agree one safe step
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Dr Okoro?
Dr OkoroI am. Rather intrigued why I'm here, to be honest.
YouThank you for coming. Your GP mentioned some worries about how things are at home.
Dr OkoroHa, my daughter's doing, no doubt. She fusses.
YouIt sounds like she cares a lot. I'd like to hear about life at home in your words.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, collegial respect, referral explained, curiosity)
  • 2. The collection (journals, how many, since when, wife's link)
  • 3. Impact on the home (room by room, kitchen, oven, falls)
  • 4. Discarding and insight (distress at discarding, acquiring, daughter's view)
  • 5. Other explanations (OCD, psychosis, autism, depression, grief, dementia)
  • 6. Fire and safety (smoking, heaters, alarms, exits)
  • 7. Mental state (appearance, rapport, mood, beliefs, cognition, insight)
  • 8. Law, family and close (capacity, consent, safeguarding, one safe step)

Lead with curiosity, not concern

Collegial tone; let him talk about the journals with pride first

Tell me about your reading. I gather you have quite a collection.
That's a lovely way to stay connected to her. How long ago did she die?
Quiet. I read a great deal. I keep up with the literature, you see. Old habits.
Journals. It started with my wife's. After she died I kept them, then kept going.

Enough.
Over a thousand. Complete runs from the sixties. They're probably worth a fortune.
A few a month. Online.

Which rooms can you still use for what they're meant for? Can you cook and sit comfortably?

The study, originally. Now, well, everywhere. Landing, dining room, some in the kitchen.
The bedroom, mostly. A chair in the lounge. I don't use the oven any more.

Discarding, meaning and insight

Feelings about throwing out; what they mean; daughter's view; insight

How would you feel if some of them were thrown away?
No. Nobody's going to clear your house without you.
Your daughter sees it differently. What do you make of her worry?
I couldn't. So you want me to throw them all out, I suppose?
Reading them, I feel she's still there.
She exaggerates. Though the kitchen is perhaps a bit much.

Get it done

Not OCD, not psychosis

Checking, counting, intrusive thoughts; special meanings or messages

Do you ever have to check, count or arrange things a set way, or get unwanted thoughts you can't push away?
What would happen if someone took the journals? Do they hold any special meaning or message for you?
No. I like order, but nothing like that.
I'd be furious. But no messages. They're journals, doctor.

Autism, mood, grief and drink

Lifelong interests and social ease; mood, sleep, appetite; alcohol; suicidal thoughts

Have you had strong interests or routines all your life, or found social situations hard?
How's your mood been, and your sleep and appetite? Any drink?
Any thoughts of not wanting to go on?
I've always loved my subjects. Socially, fine. My wife was the life and soul, mind.
Mood's fine. I sleep well, eat well. A glass at dinners. Never thought of ending things. I still miss her.

Later-life onset; names, appointments, getting lost; orientation; formal cognitive test

Could I ask a few quick memory questions, just for completeness? What's today's date, and where are we?
Sharp as ever. I could quote you papers.
Wednesday the eleventh of November. Your clinic. Fire away.

Law and safety net

Capacity, self-neglect and safeguarding

Presume capacity; Care Act self-neglect; consent first; fire service visit by consent; environmental health last resort

The fire service do free home visits; they'd fit an alarm and help clear a path to both doors. Would you let them come?
Agreed. Until then, please keep the oven and the heater off and a torch by your bed.
If they don't touch the journals.
Very well.
K
Law and safety net

His daughter and confidentiality

Ask before sharing; involve her in the plan; no skip

With your agreement, I'd like to involve your daughter so she understands the plan.
Nobody will come in with a skip.
My daughter mustn't come in with a skip.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Opening with 'fire hazard'
Lead with curiosity about the journals; house second
Accepting 'enough'
Offer a range: hundreds, or more?
Calling it OCD
Ask about intrusive thoughts and forced rituals; hoarding feels meaningful
Skipping fire questions
Smoking, heaters, cooker, smoke alarm, both exits
Forgetting cognition
Orientation now; formal test and family history of change later
Finish

Close it well

YouWhat you describe has a name: hoarding disorder. It's common, it's nothing to be ashamed of, and it often begins after a loss.
YouThe problem isn't the collecting; it's that the rooms, the oven and the back door can't be used safely. Does that make sense?
YouThere's a talking therapy that helps people decide what to keep, at their own pace. Some people donate duplicates to medical libraries. Would you think about it?
YouI'll write to your GP and see you again in four weeks. Any questions?
