After a first seizure: temporal lobe testing

Wednesday 14:30, liaison referral · Elderly care ward, acute hospital

The task

Miss Grace Adebayo, a 68-year-old retired librarian, was admitted to the elderly care ward this morning after her first ever seizure at home. She is a little confused and frightened about what has happened, but willing to help. The medical team have asked for psychiatric input into her cognition. Please carry out a bedside physical assessment of her temporal lobe function, explaining each part as you go.

Your job: Carry out a bedside temporal lobe assessment (memory, language and comprehension, naming, visual fields, recognition and seizure phenomena) and say what the findings mean.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Check glasses, hearing and handedness; go at her pace
  2. Ask about auras, déjà vu, smells, rising stomach, lost time, automatisms
  3. Give a seven-part name and address in the first five minutes
  4. Test recent, remote, semantic memory and animal fluency
  5. Test commands, repetition, graded naming, reading and writing
  6. Test upper quadrants of each eye and a famous face
  7. Get delayed recall, call it provisional, plan repeat testing, give driving advice
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Is it Miss Adebayo?
GraceYes. I'm a bit muddled, sorry.
YouPlease don't apologise, Grace; you've had a frightening morning.
YouI'd like to try some memory and language tasks with you. We can stop whenever you like. Is that alright?
GraceOkay.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, reassurance, consent, hands, glasses, hearing, handedness)
  • 2. Seizure experiences (smells, tastes, rising stomach, déjà vu, fear, lost time, automatisms)
  • 3. Memory (7-part address, breakfast, journey, childhood, famous facts, animals)
  • 4. Language (speech, 3-step command, repetition, graded naming)
  • 5. Reading and writing (written command, own sentence)
  • 6. Fields and faces (upper quadrants each eye, famous face)
  • 7. Mood, psychosis, delayed recall (free recall, then cues)
  • 8. Close (left or right temporal, postictal caveat, repeat, EEG, MRI, DVLA)

Ask about auras first

Smell, taste, rising stomach feeling, déjà vu, sudden fear; how long, how often

Before the fit, or in the past few months, have you had any odd turns?
A strange smell or taste, a rising feeling in your tummy, or a feeling you'd lived a moment before?
How long do they last, and how often?
Now you say it... a smell like burning rubber, and a feeling I've been here before. A few seconds, then gone. For months.

Lost time and what others saw

Lost time, staring, lip-smacking, picking at clothes; ask witnesses

Has anyone seen you go blank, stare, smack your lips or fiddle with your clothes?
Have you ever lost a chunk of time you can't account for?
My sister said I stared and picked at my sleeve once. I didn't remember it.

Get it done

Register the address early

Seven-part name and address, repeated until correct, up to three tries

I'm going to give you a name and address. Repeat it back, and I'll ask you for it again later. Harry Barnes, 73 Orchard Street, Kendal.
Once more, so it sticks.
Harry Barnes, 73... Orchard Street... Kendal.

Where did you grow up, and what was your first school?

Margaret Thatcher.

Comprehension and repetition

Spontaneous speech, a three-step command, repeating a sentence

Can you tell me a bit about your family?
Point to the ceiling, then the door, then touch your nose.
Say after me: the red bus stopped by the old church.
My sister Jean's in Lancaster, and my son works in Leeds. He's coming tonight.

Graded naming

Common, then less common parts: watch, strap, buckle; pen, lid, clip

What's this called? And this part? And this?
What about this, and this part?
A watch. The strap. That's... the thing that does it up. I know it but I can't get the word.

Reading and writing

Read and obey a written command; write a sentence of her own

Could you read this card and do what it says?
Could you write any sentence you like?
Close... your... I can't make the words line up. This is stupid. I was a librarian.

Upper fields and faces

Four quadrants each eye, upper ones carefully; a famous face

Look at my nose and cover one eye. Tell me when you see my finger move, top and bottom on each side.
And the other eye.
Who is this?
Bottom right, yes. Top right... no. Oh, there it is.
That's the King.

Mood, psychosis, then delayed recall

Brief mood and psychosis screen; free recall first, then cues

Since all this, have you heard voices, or felt that people are against you? How's your mood?

No voices. Just frightened and a bit low.
Harry... something. I can't. Please just move on.

Law and safety net

Consent, capacity and the postictal caveat

She can decline; if postictal confusion removes capacity, wait unless needed now (MCA 2005); no dementia label today

Is this the start of dementia?
It's too early to say. Seizures can affect memory for a while afterwards, so we'll repeat these tests in a day or two.
Is this the start of dementia?

Law and safety net

Driving and the first-seizure pathway

Stop driving and tell the DVLA; GMC allows disclosure if she keeps driving; NICE NG217 specialist review within 2 weeks

After a seizure, the law says you must stop driving and tell the DVLA.
The neurology team will advise you how long.
Can't I just drive home on Friday? I feel fine now.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Giving the address late
Register the 7-part address in the first five minutes.
Naming only common objects
Grade it: watch, strap, buckle; pen, lid, clip.
Calling it dementia on the day of a seizure
Call findings provisional and book repeat testing in a day or two.
Not mentioning driving
Stop driving, tell the DVLA, document the advice.
Finish

Close it well

YouThank you, Grace, you've worked hard. You had trouble holding new information, finding some words, reading and writing, and part of your vision on the right.
YouTogether that points to the left side of the brain, near where the seizure may start. We'll repeat these tests in a day or two, once you're less tired, and the team will arrange a brain scan and an EEG.
YouUntil neurology advise otherwise, no driving, and you need to tell the DVLA. Any other questions for me?
