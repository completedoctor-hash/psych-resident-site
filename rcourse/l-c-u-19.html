Old Age Psychiatry · Delirium

Sudden confusion on the medical ward: ten minutes with his son

Wednesday 15:30, liaison referral · Acute medical ward, general hospital

The task

Mr Gordon Whitfield, a 71-year-old widower, came onto a general medical ward two days ago after a neighbour found him outside his house, half dressed and muttering. Since then he has been drowsy at times, has wandered into other bays, and the ward consultant thinks he may be psychotic. He takes tablets for blood pressure and cholesterol and has no psychiatric history that anyone knows of. His son Rhys, aged 35, is visiting and is very worried. Please speak to Rhys and take a collateral history to help clarify what is causing his father's change.

Your job: Take a focused collateral history from Rhys in about ten minutes to clarify the cause of his father's change, explain your impression, and agree what happens next.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Fix when Gordon was last completely himself
  2. Establish acute onset over days and fluctuation, including drowsiness
  3. Ask about attention, orientation, sleep reversal, hallucinations, eating
  4. Hunt causes in order: infection, drugs, alcohol, falls, bowels, bladder, pain
  5. Confirm an independent baseline: driving, money, shopping, childcare
  6. Ask about bereavement, mood and family history
  7. Name delirium, give Rhys two jobs and check his understanding
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Rhys, Gordon's son?
RhysYes. What's going on with him? Nobody tells me anything.
YouI can see how worried you are. Your dad has agreed we can talk, and I'd really value your view of how he was before this.
YouCould you take me back to when you last saw him well?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, consent from Gordon, acknowledge worry, purpose)
  • 2. Timeline of change (last well, onset over days, fluctuation, drowsiness)
  • 3. Features of confusion (attention, orientation, sleep reversed, seeing things, eating)
  • 4. Physical causes (cough, fever, urine, new tablets, alcohol, falls, bowels)
  • 5. Baseline function (driving, shopping, money, grandchildren, memory before)
  • 6. Grief, mood, background (wife's death, self-care, past history, family dementia)
  • 7. Risk (leaving ward, pulling lines, falls, driving, living alone)
  • 8. Summary and close (delirium not dementia, cause, Rhys's role, questions)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Anchor: last completely himself

One date that everything hangs on

When did you last see him completely himself?
And how quickly did things change: hours, days or months?
Last Tuesday. He took my two kids to the park. Totally normal.
Within a couple of days. Then the neighbour found him outside, half dressed.
B
Get it done

Fluctuation and drowsiness

Better and worse times, sleepy spells, worse at night

Since he's been here, is he the same all day, or better at some times?
Is he drowsy, or hard to keep awake?
It's up and down. He's asleep at lunch, then wandering at night.
C
Get it done

Attention, orientation and perception

Drifting mid-sentence, wrong names, where he is, seeing things, eating

Can he follow a conversation, or does he drift off mid-sentence?
Does he know where he is? Has he seemed to see things that aren't there?
Is he eating and drinking?
He loses track mid-sentence. He keeps calling me Owen, my brother.
He asked if he was at work. And he picks at the air.
He didn't touch his lunch today.
D
Get it done

Hunt the cause in order

Infection, drugs, alcohol, falls, bowels, bladder, pain

Had he been unwell before this, with a cough, fever or waterworks trouble?
Any new tablets, or anything from the chemist? How much does he drink?
Any falls or bumps to the head? Any problems with his bowels or passing water?
A bad cough. Green phlegm. I told him to see the GP. I should have taken him.
Just his blood pressure and cholesterol ones. Two pints at the weekend.
I don't know about his bowels.
E
Get it done

Baseline function

Driving, money, shopping, finding new routes, memory before

Before this, was he driving, shopping and managing his money himself?
Any memory problems over the past year?
All of it. He pays everything online, drives everywhere, finds his way to new places.
None. He's sharper than me.
F
Get it done

Grief, mood and background

Wife's death, self-care slipping, past history, family history

How has he been since your mum died?
Has he ever seen a psychiatrist, or has anyone in the family had dementia?
It's been hard. He lives on ready meals. He's let himself go a bit. I should have been there more.
No and no.
G
Get it done

Risk on the ward and at home

Leaving, pulling lines, falls, driving, living alone

Has he tried to leave the ward or pull at his drip?
He lives alone and still drives, is that right?
He tried to leave last night. And yes, he drives.
H
Get it done

Answer the question, then give him a job

Delirium not dementia, not psychosis; visits, glasses, photos

Is this the dementia you're worried about?
Dementia creeps on over months. Your dad changed in days, and his alertness comes and goes.
Visit at regular times, bring his glasses and some family photos, and calmly remind him where he is.
Yes. Is it? The consultant said psychotic.
What can I actually do?
I
Law and safety net

Consent and sharing

Gordon agreed; best interests if capacity lost

Your dad has agreed we can talk.
I'll make sure the ward keeps you updated.
Why has nobody told me anything?
J
Law and safety net

If he tries to leave

Mental Capacity Act, best interests, DoLS

If he tries to leave again, the staff will gently bring him back and keep him safe.
We'll do that the least restrictive way we can.
He tried to leave last night.
K
Law and safety net

No Mental Health Act, no regular antipsychotic

Delirium is a physical illness; medicines only as a last resort

I don't think he has a psychotic illness. This is treated here on the medical ward.
We'll treat the cause first, not start a regular antipsychotic.
Will he need to go to a mental hospital?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Accepting 'psychotic'
Ask onset and fluctuation before any label
Missing hypoactive delirium
Ask about drowsiness and sleepy spells
Skipping bowels, bladder, medicines
Go through the cause list in a fixed order
No baseline
Ask driving, money, shopping, memory over the past year
Regular antipsychotics
Treat the cause; short-term low dose only as last resort
Finish

Close it well

YouThis looks like delirium: sudden confusion from a physical illness, probably the chest infection. Most people get better.
YouThe ward will treat the infection and check his bloods, bowels and bladder. No driving until he's better and his GP has seen him.
YouWhat's your main takeaway? I'll speak to the ward consultant now. What questions do you have?
