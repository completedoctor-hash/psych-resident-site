Going home without carers: capacity to refuse a care package

Friday 10:30 · Acute medical ward, side room

The task

Miss Dorothy Pennington, an 82-year-old retired bookkeeper, has been on a general medical ward for several weeks with a stubborn urinary tract infection and is now medically ready to go home. She walks with a stick and fell once during the admission. The occupational therapist recommends carers twice daily to help her wash, dress and prepare meals, and the consultant agrees, but Dorothy is declining. Her recent MMSE was 29/30. She has type 2 diabetes on tablets, hypertension, cataracts in both eyes and osteoarthritis, and takes amlodipine, metformin and simvastatin. Please assess her capacity to refuse carers and explain your conclusion to her.

Your job: Assess her capacity to refuse carers after discharge, tell her the outcome, and agree a safe discharge plan either way.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Name the specific decision: refusing twice-daily carers on discharge
  2. Give the relevant information in plain words first
  3. Test understand, retain, use and weigh, and communicate with open questions
  4. Screen for delirium, mood, dizziness, nutrition and loneliness
  5. Tell her the conclusion plainly and that the decision is hers
  6. Agree a safety plan she accepts and keep the offer of carers open
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Is it Miss Pennington?
DorothyI am. Dorothy. Are you here about these carers?
YouI am, Dorothy. Your team asked me to hear your side properly before anything is decided.
YouCan you tell me what you've been told about the carers, and how you feel about it?
DorothyAnother doctor? I've told everyone. I don't need strangers washing me.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, purpose, her right to decide, her view first)
  • 2. The decision and the information (carers twice daily, why, glasses and hearing)
  • 3. Her reasons (independence, strangers, leaving hospital)
  • 4. Impairment of mind? (MMSE 29/30, delirium cleared, mood)
  • 5. Understand, retain, use and weigh, communicate (open questions, repeat back later)
  • 6. Risks at home (falls, dizziness, diabetes, meals, loneliness)
  • 7. Conclusion explained (has capacity, unwise not incapable, hers to decide)
  • 8. Safety plan and close (OT, physio, alarm, BP check, GP, carers any time)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Name the decision, out loud

Refusing twice-daily carers on discharge, not going home; anger acknowledged

I'm sorry you've had to keep saying it. That must be tiring.
I'm not here to make you have carers. I want to understand your decision and make sure it's properly recorded.
The decision we're looking at is whether to have carers twice a day when you go home.
Another doctor? I've told everyone. I don't need strangers washing me.
Well. Go on, then.
B
Get it done

Give the information first

Glasses on, hearing checked; carers twice daily for washing, dressing, meals; the reasons

Have you got your glasses on, and can you hear me all right?
The team suggested carers twice a day, morning and evening, to help with washing, dressing and meals for a while.
That's because you fell on the ward, your walking is slower after being so poorly, and your eyesight isn't at its best until the cataract operation.
Yes, I can see you. Just about.
I know all that. I want my own house.
C
Get it done

Hear her reasons

Independence; strangers at set times; thirty years alone; fear of hospital

What do you see as the good and bad sides of having carers?
What matters most to you about going home?
Good: someone checks on me. Bad: strangers in my house at set times, telling me when to get up. I've lived alone thirty years.
If I stay here any longer, this place will kill me.
D
Get it done

Any impairment of mind?

MMSE 29/30; confusion with the infection now cleared?; mood; no psychosis

When you first came in with the infection, were you muddled at all? How's your thinking now?
How's your mood been? Any low spells, or losing interest in things?
No, I'm not down. It does get lonely since Arthur died.
E
Get it done

Understand: in her own words

Open questions, not 'do you understand?'; what carers do and why

Can you tell me in your words what the carers would do, and why they were suggested?
And what do you think has changed since you came in?
Help me wash and dress, and do my meals. Because I fell and I'm a bit wobbly.
I'm weaker. That infection nearly finished me.
F
Get it done

Use and weigh: what if, how would you

What if you go home alone; washing and meals; a fall at night

What might happen if you go home without them?
How would you manage washing and meals in the first week?
And if you fell at night, what would you do?
I could fall again. I know that. I'm not daft.
Strip wash at the sink, sitting down. The Patels next door have offered meals.
I roll over and crawl to the phone. I'd rather risk that than stay here.
G
Get it done

Retain and communicate

Ask again later in the talk; a clear, consistent choice

Just so I know I explained it well, can you remind me what the carers would do and why?
Not at all. Everyone starts from the position that they can make their own decisions. My job is to check that properly and write it down.
Wash, dress, meals, twice a day, because of the fall and being wobbly. So are you saying I'm not all there?
H
Get it done

The risks at home, looked at properly

Dizziness on standing; amlodipine; metformin and eating; eyes; loneliness

Do you get dizzy when you stand up?
How have you been eating, and have your sugars been all right?
A bit. First thing in the morning.
Mostly. The Patels do my shopping already.
I
Law and safety net

The conclusion and the Mental Capacity Act

Presume capacity; unwise is not incapable; record decision and each ability; best interests only if she lacks it

You've understood and remembered the information, weighed it up and told me clearly.
You have the capacity to make this decision, and it's yours to make, even if some people would choose differently.
Thank you. Finally someone's listened.
J
Law and safety net

A safety plan she accepts

OT home visit, physio, pendant alarm, BP check, GP in 2 weeks; reablement; carers any time

Can I suggest some things that might make home safer, without daily carers?
An occupational therapist could fit a stool for the sink and grab rails, a physio could help your balance, and a pendant alarm means you press a button rather than crawl.
If things get harder, you can ask for carers at any time.
The alarm's a good idea. And the OT. Not carers. Not yet.
I'll have the OT come round, but not carers every day.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Unwise equals incapable
Judge the process of deciding, not the outcome
Assessing the wrong decision
Name it: refusing carers, not going home
Closed questions
Ask 'tell me in your words', 'what might happen if', 'how would you manage'
Capacity, then walking away
Agree OT, physio, alarm, BP check, GP review; carers open
Finish

Close it well

YouBefore you go we'll check your blood pressure lying down and then standing, as your tablet might be causing the dizziness. The GP will see you in two weeks.
YouIf things get harder, you can ask for carers at any time; here's the number. If you fall and can't get up, are hurt, or feel muddled again, press the alarm or ring 999.
YouCan you tell me what we've agreed?
DorothyHome without carers. The OT, the physio, an alarm, blood pressure check, GP in two weeks. And I can change my mind.
