Mum's memory, whisky and driving: results and an unsafe driver

Wednesday 15:30, memory clinic · Memory clinic, older adults' community mental health team (phone call)

The task

Mrs Margaret Doyle is an 81-year-old widow referred to the memory clinic by her GP with short-term memory problems. A specialist nurse found she scored 50/100 on the ACE-III, mainly losing points in memory and visuospatial tasks; she looked unkempt and smelled of stale alcohol. She was told not to drive but refused. Her GP then found she has been drinking half a bottle or more of whisky daily for months, with raised ALT and GGT. She again refused to stop driving or cut down. Her son, Mr Stephen Doyle, has phoned the clinic because she is furious and he wants to know what has gone on. She has agreed that you may discuss this with him. Please explain the findings so far, address his concerns, and discuss immediate and longer-term management of her driving risk.

Your job: Confirm consent, hear the son's concerns, explain the test, the alcohol findings and what they may mean, then agree immediate and longer-term action on her driving.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm who you are speaking to and the consent before any result
  2. Draw out his observations: home, driving incidents, drinking
  3. Explain ACE-III 50/100, the whisky, ALT and GGT, and possible causes
  4. Say she must stop driving now; explain her DVLA duty and the clinic's next step
  5. Agree longer-term steps: safe alcohol treatment, tests, transport, LPA, carer support
  6. Check understanding with teach-back and safety-net
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors at the memory clinic. Am I speaking with Stephen Doyle?
StephenYes. What's going on with Mum?
YouThank you for calling, Stephen. Your mum has agreed I can talk with you. I can hear this has been upsetting.
StephenShe's livid. Says you're taking her car.
YouLet me explain what we've found and why, then we'll plan together.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (identity, consent, anger acknowledged, plan for the call)
  • 2. His concerns (home, self-care, driving incidents, drinking)
  • 3. What we found (ACE-III 50/100, memory and visuospatial, unkempt)
  • 4. The alcohol picture (half a bottle daily, ALT, GGT, never stop suddenly)
  • 5. What it might mean (dementia, alcohol damage or both, more tests)
  • 6. Driving now (unsafe, stop now, DVLA duty, our next step)
  • 7. Longer term (transport, alcohol help, social care, LPA)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, meeting with consultant, safety net)

What has he seen?

Bills, fridge, clothes; scrapes; getting lost; where she drives; bottles

First, what have you been told, and what's worrying you most?
How has she been managing at home?
How has her driving been? Where does she drive now?
Bills missed, fridge empty, same clothes. I thought it was grief since Dad died.
She scraped the car twice this spring. And once she got lost driving to mine.
Shops, church. And I'd no idea about the whisky until a neighbour mentioned the bottles.
C
Get it done

The score, in plain words

ACE-III 50/100; usual cut-offs 82–88; memory and visuospatial; unkempt, smell of alcohol

The nurse did a memory and thinking test called the ACE-III. It's scored out of 100, and your mum scored 50.
Most people without memory problems score above 82 to 88. Her weakest areas were memory and judging space and shapes, which matters for driving.
Fifty. That's half.
God. I should have noticed. I thought it was just her getting older.
D
Get it done

The whisky and the liver

Half a bottle a day = 14 units daily; GGT heavy drinking; ALT liver strain; no sudden stop; thiamine

Her GP found she gets through half a bottle of whisky or more each day. That's about 14 units, a whole week's limit, every day.
GGT shows heavy drinking. ALT shows her liver is under strain.
She mustn't stop suddenly, as that can cause fits.
And the blood tests?
E
Get it done

What it might mean

Dementia, alcohol-related brain damage, or both; MRI, bloods, repeat ACE-III after abstinence

It suggests dementia, probably moderate, or damage from alcohol, or both.
A brain scan, more bloods, and repeating the test after a couple of months of cutting down. Some may improve; we'll know more then.
Surely it's just the whisky, then? If she stops, she'll be back to normal?
How do you find out which?
F
Get it done

Say it plainly: she isn't safe to drive

Stop now; memory, spatial skills, drink, scrapes; no driving test as a stopgap

I'm sorry, Stephen. From what we know, it isn't safe for her to drive, and she needs to stop now.
Her memory, her sense of space, the drinking, and those scrapes all point the same way.
With these results, she isn't safe to drive while waiting for a driving test.
Is she safe to drive?
Can't she just do a driving test to prove she's fine?
G
Law and safety net

Her DVLA duty and the GMC order

Her legal duty to tell DVLA; persuade once more; tell her we'll inform; inform promptly; write to her

By law, she has to tell the DVLA about her memory problems and the drinking. We'll ask her once more to stop and to tell them herself.
Then we tell her we're informing the DVLA, we do it, and we write to her.
So what happens with her licence?
And if she won't?
H
Law and safety net

If she drives anyway

Driving after revocation is an offence; police last resort; keys only in immediate danger

Let us know straight away.
Keeping the keys is only for when she's about to drive and it's clearly dangerous.
What if she ignores them and drives anyway?
I
Get it done

Replace what driving gives her

Freedom, church, friends; lifts, taxi account, bus pass, community transport; sell the car

What does driving mean to her?
Let's plan what replaces it: lifts from family, a taxi account, a bus pass, community transport.
Freedom. And getting to church and her friends.
J
Get it done

Support for her, and for him

Older adults' alcohol worker; social care assessment; carer's assessment; joint meeting with consultant

An older adults' alcohol worker can help her cut down safely, with her GP.
We'll also ask social care to assess help at home, with her agreement, and offer you a carer's assessment.
Our consultant can meet her with you this week, so she hears it from us, not just you.
She'll never forgive me if I'm the one who stops her.
I didn't know I could get anything.
K
Law and safety net

Lasting Power of Attorney, while she can

LPA for health and welfare, and for property and finance, only while she has capacity

While she can still decide, it's worth talking to her about Lasting Power of Attorney, covering both her health and her money.
If she agrees, her GP or a solicitor can help, or you can do it together online.
How does that work?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Results before consent
Confirm identity and her consent first
Blaming it all on whisky
Say it may be dementia, alcohol or both; retest after cutting down
A driving test as a stopgap
She stops now; a test is not a way to keep driving
Leading with police or keys
Her duty, one more try, DVLA, then last resorts
'Stop drinking today'
Never stop suddenly; GP-led reduction and thiamine
Finish

Close it well

YouSo: the test suggests dementia, the drinking may add to it, and she must stop driving now. If she won't, we'll inform the DVLA.
YouIn your words, how will you put this to her this week?
StephenThat she can't drive now, that you'll tell the DVLA if she won't, and that we'll sort lifts and help with the drink.
YouCall 999 if she's driving and anyone is at risk. If she gets shaky, sweaty or confused cutting down, call her GP or 111. Here's our number if anything changes.
