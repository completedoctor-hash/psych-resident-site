Old Age Psychiatry · Mood and psychosis in later life

Low mood in an older carer: suicide risk and risk to his wife

Tuesday 14:00, home visit · Older adults community mental health team, the patient's home

The task

Mr Desmond Clarke is an 83-year-old retired bus driver who lives with his wife, Margaret. Margaret was diagnosed with vascular dementia twelve months ago and Mr Clarke looks after her at home with little outside help. His GP has referred him to the older adults community mental health team because of worsening low mood; routine blood tests came back normal. He has osteoarthritis in his knees and hands. You are visiting him at home. Please take a history of his current symptoms and carry out a thorough risk assessment.

Your job: Take a history of his current symptoms, including the strain of caring, physical health and substances; screen for psychotic features; and carry out a thorough assessment of suicide risk and of risk to Margaret.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Count depressive symptoms with numbers and a duration
  2. Walk through a day and a night of caring; find out who helps
  3. Cover pain, falls, senses, memory, alcohol and every tablet in the house
  4. Exclude psychotic features in one minute
  5. Ask about suicidal thoughts, plans, preparations and what keeps him going
  6. Ask plainly about joint-death thoughts and harm or neglect towards Margaret
  7. Agree same-day safety steps: tablets out, Karen called, review in 2 days
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors from the older adults team. Are you Mr Desmond Clarke?
DesI am. Des, please.
YouThank you for letting me visit, Des. Your GP mentioned things have felt heavy lately.
DesIt's been a long year.
YouIs there somewhere we can talk privately for a while, and is Margaret settled?
DesShe's watching telly, she's fine. We can sit in the kitchen.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, privacy, Margaret settled, time agreed)
  • 2. Mood symptoms (duration, sleep, weight, interest, energy, concentration, guilt, hope)
  • 3. Caring role (day and night, wandering, help, breaks, guilt, frustration)
  • 4. Physical health (pain, painkillers, falls, hearing, sight, memory)
  • 5. Psychotic screen (guilt, nihilism, poverty, voices)
  • 6. Alcohol and medicines (units, saved tablets, his and hers)
  • 7. Suicide risk (thoughts, plan, preparation, protective factors)
  • 8. Risk to Margaret (joint death, harm, neglect, safeguarding)
  • 9. Summary and close (depression named, help for both, means removed, crisis numbers)

Duration; waking time; weight lost; interest; energy; concentration; hope

The last three months it's been much worse.

Suicide: thoughts, plans, protection

Thoughts, method, plan, final acts, protective factors

Des, with everything you've told me, I need to ask: have things felt so bad that you've thought of ending your life?
Have you made a plan, chosen a time, or done anything to prepare, like writing a note or sorting your affairs?
What keeps you going?
I've looked at that tin and thought, that'd do it.
No plan. I couldn't do it to the grandchildren, Ellie and Sam.

Ask the joint-death question

Both better off gone; ways for both to die; harm; neglect

Some people caring for someone they love start to think it would be kinder if they both went. Has anything like that crossed your mind?
Have you ever looked into ways for you both to die, or felt like harming her, even for a moment?
Has she gone without meals or medicines?
Some nights I wonder if we'd both be better off gone. I'd never hurt her.
I shouted at her once at three in the morning. She cried. I felt like dirt.

Law and safety net

Safeguarding Margaret

Discuss with senior today; safeguarding concern to adult social care if harm or neglect risk

I need to talk to my consultant today about how we keep you both safe.
Has anyone in the family raised concerns about how she's being looked after?
Will they take Margaret off me?
No. I do everything for her.

Law and safety net

Help for both: the Care Act

Carer's assessment for Des; needs assessment for Margaret; night support; no sleeping tablets

You've been carrying this alone. I'd like to get help in for both of you.
Would you agree to a carer's assessment, and to someone looking at what Margaret needs at night?
Nobody's lifted a finger for us in a year.
Can't you just give me something to make me sleep?
K
Law and safety net

Karen, consent and his status

Consent to call Karen; GMC risk-to-life exception; informal; MHA only if risk rises

Could I ring Karen today, with your permission, so she can take the tin away?
Is there anyone else you'd like me to speak to?
Karen can have the tablets. Ring her if you like.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling it understandable
Agree he is worn out, then count symptoms: 4 am waking, half a stone, allotment dropped
Missing the tin and the whisky
Ask what is saved, his and hers, not just what is prescribed
Never asking about both of them
Normalise, then ask the joint-death question plainly
A plan for him alone
Carer's assessment, needs assessment for Margaret, safeguarding discussion
Sleeping tablets as the answer
Explain falls risk; treat the depression; cut the whisky
Finish

Close it well

YouLet me check I've got this right. For three months you've been waking early, losing weight, lost interest, and feeling guilty and hopeless. You're caring alone through the nights, drinking more, and you've had thoughts of dying, and of you both going, but no plan. Is that fair?
YouThis sounds like depression, Des, and it's treatable.
YouToday I'd like that tin out of the house and to ring Karen. I'll talk to my consultant, and someone from our team will see you again within two days.
YouIf things feel worse before then, ring Samaritans, free, on 116 123 or 111 option 2, or 999 in an emergency. I'll write these down.
