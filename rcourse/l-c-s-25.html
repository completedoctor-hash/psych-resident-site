Explaining vascular dementia to her son

Friday 14:00 · Older adult community mental health team (memory service)

The task

Patricia Wojcik is a 78-year-old woman recently assessed by a colleague in the older adult community team after her GP raised concerns about memory and behaviour. Her husband noticed she had grown irritable and recently found her wandering outside, unsure where she was heading. Screening bloods and urine were normal. She has type 2 diabetes, hypertension, raised cholesterol and a previous stroke with residual right arm weakness. ACE-III was 68/100; HADS depression 2/21, anxiety 5/21. MRI showed extensive periventricular and deep white matter changes consistent with marked small vessel disease and moderate generalised volume loss. She lives with her husband, manages her own daily tasks, still drives and goes to a pensioners' club twice weekly. Please explain the diagnosis to her son, Daniel Wojcik, and answer his questions about management.

Your job: Explain the diagnosis of vascular dementia to her son, including how the scan and tests support it, the likely course, risk-factor control, support, and driving.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Mrs Wojcik's consent; ask what he knows and fears
  2. Explain dementia as an umbrella and vascular dementia in plain words
  3. Test his infection theory aloud with the evidence: course, urine, bloods
  4. Explain HADS 2/21, ACE-III 68/100 and the MRI in plain words
  5. Explain stepwise course, risk-factor control, no donepezil, not directly inherited
  6. Raise DVLA and wandering; offer CST, dementia adviser, carer's assessment, LPA
  7. Check understanding with teach-back
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the older adults team. Are you Daniel Wojcik?
DanielYes. Dad said Mum's got dementia, and frankly I don't buy it.
YouThank you for being honest, Daniel. It must have been a shock. Your mum has agreed we can talk. Could you tell me what you've noticed yourself?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, her consent, what he knows, his doubt and fears)
  • 2. How we reached it (husband's account, normal bloods and urine, HADS, ACE-III 68)
  • 3. Scan and diagnosis (small vessel damage, old stroke, risk factors, vascular dementia)
  • 4. Course (stepwise or gradual, patchy, varies between people)
  • 5. Treatment (BP, sugar, statin, clopidogrel; no donepezil for pure vascular)
  • 6. Lifestyle and support (club, activity, CST, dementia adviser, carer's assessment)
  • 7. Risk and practical issues (driving and DVLA, wandering, LPA)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflet, number, review in six weeks)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

His starting point and his fears

What he's heard, what he's seen, what worries him most

What have you heard so far?
What have you noticed yourself?
What worries you most about it?
She's fine. She still drives and goes to her club. I don't buy it.
Honestly? I think it's a water infection. Old people get confused with those all the time.
B
Get it done

Dementia is an umbrella

Umbrella term; Alzheimer's is one type; hers is vascular

Dementia isn't one disease. It's an umbrella term for brain changes that steadily affect memory, thinking and daily life.
Alzheimer's is one type. Your mum has a type called vascular dementia. Does that make sense?
I thought dementia was Alzheimer's.
C
Get it done

Test the infection theory aloud

Months, not days; urine clear; bloods normal

Your dad noticed changes over months. An infection usually comes on over hours or days and makes people muddled from one hour to the next.
Her urine test was clear, and her blood tests were all normal. Does that answer it?
But did you actually test for infection?
D
Get it done

Mood and the memory score

HADS depression 2/21 = not depression; ACE-III 68/100, below the usual cut-off of 82

She did a mood questionnaire and scored low, so depression isn't behind this.
On the memory test she scored 68 out of 100. A score under 82 usually means a real problem with thinking.
What about depression? She's been grumpy.
E
Get it done

The scan and what vascular dementia is

Damage to tiny deep vessels plus the old stroke; diabetes, BP, cholesterol; absorb guilt

The scan shows a lot of damage to the tiny blood vessels deep in the brain, plus the old stroke.
Her diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol have all harmed those vessels. Does that fit with what you know?
It does. Mum's always been terrible at taking her tablets. I should have nagged her more.

Get it done

The course

Often stepwise; can be stable for long periods; pace varies

It usually progresses, but often in steps: stable for a while, then a drop, sometimes after another small stroke.
Some people stay at one level for a long time.
What happens now, then? Does it get worse?
G
Get it done

What slows it, and why not donepezil

Letter to GP: tighten BP, diabetes, cholesterol; check clopidogrel adherence; no memory tablets for pure vascular

We can lower the chance of further steps by controlling her blood pressure, sugar and cholesterol, and taking her blood thinner.
Those tablets help in Alzheimer's, but not in vascular dementia alone.
Can she have donepezil, like my mate's dad?
H
Get it done

Will I get it?

Not usually directly inherited; risk factors run in families; NHS Health Check

Vascular dementia isn't usually passed down directly.
Diabetes and blood pressure can run in families, so keeping yours in check lowers your risk. Your GP can do an NHS Health Check.
Will I get it? Will my kids?
I
Get it done

Support for her and her husband

Keep the club; group cognitive stimulation therapy; Alzheimer's Society dementia adviser; leaflet and team number

Keep her going to her club; routine and company help.
We'll offer her group cognitive stimulation therapy, and a dementia adviser from the Alzheimer's Society.
Dad's struggling already. What help is there?
J
Law and safety net

Driving and wandering

She must notify the DVLA; advise stopping until DVLA decides; GMC disclosure rule; GPS tracker, Herbert Protocol

By law she has to tell the DVLA about the diagnosis.
Because she got lost recently, our advice is that she stops driving until the DVLA decides.
You're taking her car? That's all she's got left.
K
Law and safety net

Consent, attorney and the carer

Share only with her consent and record it; LPA while she has capacity (MCA 2005); carer's assessment (Care Act 2014)

Now is a good time for her to set up a Lasting Power of Attorney, while she can.
Your dad can ask the council for a carer's assessment.
That's a lot to take in.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Explaining before asking what he knows
Ask what he's heard and fears first.
Dismissing his infection theory
Test it aloud: course, urine, bloods.
Jargon: 'white matter hyperintensities', 'ACE-III'
Tiny blood vessels; memory test scored 68 out of 100.
Offering donepezil for pure vascular dementia
Explain why risk-factor control matters more.
One long speech, or forgetting driving
Chunk and check; always raise DVLA.
Finish

Close it well

YouIt is a lot. Can you tell me in your words what you'll tell your dad tonight?
DanielIt's vascular dementia, from her blood vessels and the stroke, not an infection. No memory tablets, but her blood pressure and sugar matter. She stops driving and tells the DVLA. And there's help for Dad.
YouThat's exactly it. Here's a leaflet on vascular dementia and our team's number. We'll see your mum again in six weeks, and you're welcome to come. If you'd ever like a second opinion, just ask.
