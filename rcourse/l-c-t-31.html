Galantamine for Dad: answering his son's questions

Tuesday 14:20, memory clinic · Memory clinic, community mental health centre

The task

Mr Harold Whitaker is a 79-year-old retired joiner diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease after assessment in the memory clinic. At today's appointment the team have decided to start galantamine. His son, Mr Neil Whitaker, a 50-year-old teacher, has asked to speak with you about the medicine; his father has given consent for this. Please explain to Neil the benefits and side effects of galantamine and answer his concerns. A history is not needed.

Your job: Explain to Neil, Harold's son, the benefits and side effects of galantamine, how it is started and monitored, and answer his concerns. A history is not needed.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm Harold's consent; ask what Neil knows and fears
  2. Recap: Alzheimer's, mild to moderate; not a cure
  3. Honest benefit; judge at 3 months
  4. Dose: 8 mg XL with breakfast, 16 mg after 4 weeks
  5. Side effects with actions: stop and call if faint, falls, pulse <50, bad rash
  6. Name the heart issue: bisoprolol, ECG first, GP pulse checks
  7. Beyond the tablet: CST, carer's assessment, LPA, DVLA, leaflet, contact number
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the memory clinic. Are you Neil Whitaker?
NeilYes, Harold's son.
YouThanks for staying, Neil. Your dad has said he's happy for us to talk about his treatment. What do you already know about galantamine, and what's on your mind?
NeilMainly whether it actually works. Everything I read says these drugs don't do much.
YouThat's a really fair question, and I'll be honest with you. Is there anything else worrying you, so I can cover it all?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Harold's consent, Neil's knowledge and worries)
  • 2. Diagnosis recap (Alzheimer's, mild to moderate)
  • 3. How it works (acetylcholine; not a cure)
  • 4. Benefits (modest; some improve, some steady, some no change; 3 months)
  • 5. Dosing (8 mg XL with breakfast, 16 mg at 4 weeks, missed doses)
  • 6. Side effects and the heart (nausea, weight, dizziness; bisoprolol, ECG, pulse)
  • 7. His other questions (liver, addiction, cost, daffodils)
  • 8. Beyond the tablet (capacity, LPA, DVLA, CST, carer's assessment, close)

Recap the diagnosis

Alzheimer's, mild to moderate; memory and planning affected

Your dad has Alzheimer's at a mild to moderate stage.
Memory and planning are affected, but he still does a lot himself. Does that fit with what you see?
He's got lost driving home twice, and he asks me the same thing five times.

How galantamine works

Slows breakdown of acetylcholine; helps symptoms; not a cure

In Alzheimer's, a brain chemical called acetylcholine runs low. It helps nerve cells pass messages for memory.
Galantamine slows its breakdown, so more of it is around.
It doesn't cure Alzheimer's or stop it.
So it fixes his memory?
D
Get it done

Honest benefits and the 3-month judgement

Some improve, many steadier for months, some no change; review at 3 months

Some people improve a bit in memory, daily tasks or mood. Many stay steadier for some months. Some notice no change.
We'll judge it at about three months. If it's helping and suits him, he carries on. If not, we stop it.
So it's a lottery.
OK. That's more honest than what I read.

Dose and missed doses

8 mg XL with breakfast x4 weeks, then 16 mg; maybe 24 mg; restart low if several days missed; pill box

It's one long-acting capsule each morning with breakfast. Food helps stop sickness.
He starts on 8 milligrams for four weeks, then 16 if he's fine.
If he misses more than a few days in a row, ring us. He'd need to restart on the low dose.
He forgets tablets sometimes.
I can sort his tablets on a Sunday.

Side effects linked to action

Nausea, poor appetite, loose stools, weight loss, dizziness, vivid dreams; usually settle

The common ones are feeling sick, poor appetite, loose stools, some weight loss, dizziness, and sometimes vivid dreams.
They often settle within a week or two of each step.
If sickness stops him eating for more than a couple of days, ring us.
That's a lot to remember.
G
Law and safety net

The heart: bisoprolol and slow pulse

ECG before starting; GP pulse checks; stop and same-day review if faint, falls, pulse <50, severe rash

You were right to raise his heart. Galantamine can slow the heart a little, and bisoprolol already does.
Before he starts, we'll do a heart tracing, an ECG, and his GP will check his pulse as the dose goes up.
If he faints, falls, feels very dizzy, or his pulse drops below 50, stop the capsule and get him seen that day.
What if it goes too slow?
Fainting, falls, pulse under 50, a bad rash.

The surprise questions

Liver; addiction; cost; daffodil extract

It isn't known to damage the liver.
No, it isn't addictive. If it's stopped, any benefit fades over a few weeks.
It's on the NHS, and at his age prescriptions are free.
Supplements from bulbs aren't tested, the dose is unknown, and parts of the bulb are poisonous.
That it wrecks the liver.
Is it addictive?
A colleague said daffodil extract does the same thing.

Law and safety net

Dad decides: capacity and consent

MCA 2005: presume capacity; Neil supports, doesn't decide; record consent scope

Your dad has agreed to start it. It's his decision, and you're there to support him.
If he changes his mind, he can tell us at any time.
What if he refuses to take it?

Law and safety net

Driving and attorney

Dementia must be reported to DVLA; driving assessment; LPAs while he can

With this diagnosis, your dad must tell the DVLA.
We can help arrange a driving assessment if he wants to keep driving.
It's a good time for him to set up Lasting Powers of Attorney.
He'll hate that. But after getting lost, I think he knows.

Beyond the tablet, and Neil himself

CST groups, carer's assessment, dementia adviser, Alzheimer's Society

Apart from the tablet, there are memory groups called cognitive stimulation, twice a week.
You can have a carers' assessment for support.
I'll pass your details to our dementia adviser today.
Is that it, though? Apart from the tablet, what else can we do?
I'm already shattered. Work, then his place every evening.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

'Helps symptoms for a while; we judge at 3 months'

Taking a history
