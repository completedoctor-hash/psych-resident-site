Abnormal DaT scan: explaining Lewy body dementia to his daughter

Wednesday 15:30 · Older adult inpatient ward, family room

The task

Chandran Pillai is a 75-year-old man admitted two months ago to an older adult mental health ward with worsening disturbed behaviour and memory difficulties. He was first treated on a medical ward for presumed delirium due to constipation, but once that settled an underlying cognitive disorder was clear. On arrival he was seeing birds flying through the hospital. His ACE-III is 42/100. Last week's DaT scan showed symmetrically reduced striatal uptake, consistent with Lewy body dementia, though Parkinson's disease or a related condition can look similar. He has no obvious movement disorder. His daughter, Anjali Pillai, has asked to know more. Please help Anjali understand what Lewy body dementia is and how it will be managed.

Your job: Explain dementia with Lewy bodies to his daughter: the core features in his own symptoms, what the scan shows, the likely course, and the treatment, including antipsychotic sensitivity and falls.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Confirm his consent; find out what she knows and her main worry
  2. Ask about fluctuation, hallucinations, sleep acting out, falls and stiffness
  3. Give a warning shot, then name it: dementia with Lewy bodies
  4. Link each core feature and the DaT scan to his own story
  5. Give an honest course: progressive, shortens life, pace varies
  6. Explain rivastigmine and antipsychotic sensitivity; flag it on every record
  7. Agree a home-safety plan, teach-back and a family meeting
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the ward psychiatrists. Are you Anjali Pillai?
AnjaliYes. Chandran's daughter.
YouThank you for coming, Anjali. Your father is happy for us to talk. I know you'd like to hear about his scan and diagnosis. What have you been told so far?
AnjaliJust that it's some kind of dementia.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, his consent, what she knows, her main worry)
  • 2. Her story (good and bad days, the birds, sleep, falls, past medicines)
  • 3. What it is (warning shot, Lewy bodies, link each feature, DaT scan)
  • 4. Course (progressive, varies day to day, falls, swallowing later, no cure)
  • 5. Medication (rivastigmine or donepezil; pulse and ECG; avoid antipsychotics)
  • 6. Managing symptoms (hallucinations, lighting, constipation, falls, sleep, low BP standing)
  • 7. Discharge and support (OT, care package, carer's assessment, capacity, LPA, Lewy Body Society)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflet, family meeting)

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

Her starting point and main worry

What she's been told; what worries her most; absorb frustration

Before I explain, what's worrying you most?
Can I check a few things you've noticed at home first, so I can link it all together?
Nobody tells me anything properly.
Is it Parkinson's? And can he come home?

Get it done

Good days and bad days

Fluctuating attention and alertness: sharp one hour, muddled or drowsy the next

Over the past months, have there been good days and bad days, when he's sharp one hour and muddled or drowsy the next?
What time of day is he at his best? And his worst?
Yes! Some mornings he's himself, doing the crossword. By lunchtime he barely knows me. Then the next day he's fine again.

Get it done

What he sees

Well-formed visual hallucinations: the birds; how he reacts; is he frightened

The staff said he was seeing birds flying through the hospital. Has he seen things at home that weren't there?
How does he react to them? Is he frightened?
Birds, yes. He'd point at the ceiling. Mostly he wasn't bothered, but sometimes he'd shout at them.

Get it done

Sleep, movement and past medicines

Acting out dreams; falls, stiffness, slowness; any medicine for agitation and its effect

Does he ever shout, punch or kick in his sleep, as if acting out a dream?
Has he fallen, or seemed stiffer or slower?
On the medical ward, did anyone give him medicine for agitation? How did he react?
Mum used to say he fought in his sleep, years ago.
No, he walks fine. No falls.
E
Get it done

Warning shot, then name it

Prepare her; name dementia with Lewy bodies early; protein clumps; close cousin of Parkinson's

I'm afraid what I have to say isn't good news. Is it alright to go on?
Your dad has a type of dementia called dementia with Lewy bodies.
Tiny clumps of a protein, called Lewy bodies, build up inside brain cells and stop them working properly.
So it's Parkinson's? He doesn't shake.
F
Get it done

The scan in one line, honestly

Looks at dopamine in movement centres; his shows less than normal; fits, though Parkinson's can look similar

The scan looks at dopamine, a chemical messenger in the brain's movement centres. His shows less than normal.
On its own the scan can't tell Lewy body dementia from Parkinson's. Together with his symptoms, it makes this very likely.
So the scan proved it?
G
Get it done

The course, honestly

No cure; progressive; shortens life; pace varies; pause for her reaction

I'm sorry to say there's no cure. It usually gets worse over the years: thinking, movement and the hallucinations.
It does shorten life, but many people live several years, and good care makes a real difference.
How long has he got?
I promised Mum I'd look after him.
H
Get it done

Rivastigmine

Often helps alertness and hallucinations; pulse and ECG first; nausea and slow pulse; start low; won't stop progression

A tablet called rivastigmine often helps alertness and the hallucinations.
We check his pulse and a heart tracing first, because it can slow the heart.
Can't you just give him something to stop the birds?
I
Get it done

The big danger: antipsychotics

Severe sensitivity; flag on drug chart, electronic record and GP letter today; tell every doctor and paramedic

The usual medicines for hallucinations, called antipsychotics, can make people with Lewy bodies very stiff, drowsy, or dangerously ill.
We'll put a warning on his drug chart, his records and his GP letter today. Please tell any doctor or paramedic who sees him.
So nobody should give him those? Nobody told me.
J
Get it done

Managing symptoms at home

Don't argue with hallucinations; better lighting; keep bowels regular; falls and low BP on standing; OT home visit

When he sees the birds, stay calm, ask what he sees, reassure him he's safe, and switch on a brighter light.
If he's not frightened by them, you don't need to do anything.
When he sees things, should I tell him they're not real?
Can he still come home, then?
K
Law and safety net

Consent and the guideline

Record his consent and what was shared (GMC 2017); NICE NG97: donepezil or rivastigmine; antipsychotics only in severe distress, with great care

Your dad agreed to me talking with you, and I'll note what we discussed.
You noticed, and you got him help. The good days make this very hard to spot, even for doctors.
I kept telling myself he was just getting old. I should have pushed the GP.
L
Law and safety net

Capacity, attorney and carer support

Capacity for where to live assessed at discharge (MCA 2005); LPA while he can; carer's assessment (Care Act 2014); Lewy Body Society

When we plan discharge, we'll assess whether he can decide where to live himself.
She's able to ask for a carer's assessment from the council.
Who decides if he comes home?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Calling it 'just Parkinson's' or 'a bit of Alzheimer's'
Name it: dementia with Lewy bodies, a close cousin of Parkinson's.
A lecture on proteins and dopamine
Tie every feature to something she has seen.
Forgetting to flag antipsychotic sensitivity
Drug chart, electronic record and GP letter, today.
Promising rivastigmine will stop it
It may help symptoms; it doesn't stop progression.
Finish

Close it well

YouIt's a lot to take in. So I can check I've explained it clearly, what will you tell the rest of the family tonight?
AnjaliIt's Lewy body dementia, it'll get worse slowly, he can try rivastigmine, no antipsychotics ever, and the OT will check the house.
YouThat's exactly right. Let's meet as a family with his consultant next week. Here's a leaflet, and I'll ask our social worker about a carer's assessment for you. What else would you like to ask?
