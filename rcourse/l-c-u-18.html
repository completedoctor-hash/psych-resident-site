Dad's memory: a ten-minute collateral history from his daughter

Monday 10:00, new referrals clinic · Older adult community mental health team base

The task

Tariq Hussain is a 68-year-old retired bus driver referred by his GP to the older adult community mental health team because of problems with his memory. His daughter, Zainab Hussain, has come to the team base to help with the assessment, and Tariq has given his consent for you to speak with her. He lives with his wife, who is well. Please take a collateral history from Zainab about the changes she has seen in her father, keeping in mind the likely causes and any risks.

Your job: Take a focused collateral history from Zainab in about ten minutes: the timeline, the thinking skills affected, daily living, other causes and the risks, then share your impression and next steps.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask her main worry first
  2. Fix the onset, the course (gradual or stepwise) and any fluctuation, with a date
  3. Get one dated example each for memory, words, orientation and recognition
  4. Ask about at least three daily tasks: self-care, money, cooker
  5. Screen depression, Lewy body, frontal, vascular, alcohol and medicine causes
  6. Name an action for driving, cooker, scams and carer strain
  7. Summarise a likely picture and name bloods, memory testing and a scan
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the older adults team. Are you Zainab Hussain?
ZainabYes, Tariq's daughter.
YouThank you for coming, Zainab. Your dad has agreed that we can talk about him. Anything you share helps us understand what's going on.
ZainabI'm glad. We've been so worried.
YouCould you start by telling me what first made you notice something had changed?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, his consent confirmed, her main worry)
  • 2. Timeline (first change, when, gradual or stepwise, fluctuation)
  • 3. Thinking skills (memory, repetition, words, getting lost, recognising people, dates)
  • 4. Daily living (shaving, dressing, bills, cooker, hobbies)
  • 5. Other causes (mood, hallucinations, falls, tremor, personality, alcohol)
  • 6. Medical and family (stroke, heart, diabetes, head injury, medicines incl. OTC sleep aids, family history)
  • 7. Risks and carer (driving, cooker, scams, getting lost, Mum's strain)
  • 8. Summary and close (likely Alzheimer's type, bloods, memory tests, scan, leaflet, duty number)

Practicalities A to Z

Where the work is done: the exact questions, what you are likely to hear, and where it takes you next.

A
Get it done

Her worry, then her story

Main worry first; absorb guilt; let her talk for a minute

Before I ask my questions, what worries you most?
What first made you think something had changed?
That he'll get lost and something will happen to him.
I thought it was just old age. I feel awful we didn't notice sooner.
B
Get it done

Fix the timeline

First change and its date; gradual or stepwise; good and bad days

When was that, roughly?
Has it crept on gradually, or come in sudden steps?
Does it vary: good days, then very muddled days?
He started missing appointments. The GP, the dentist. About a year ago.
Gradually. Just slowly worse. He's the same every day.
C
Get it done

Memory and words

Recent memory, repeating himself, word-finding; one dated example

Does he forget recent things, like what you talked about, or repeat himself?
Can you give me an example from this week?
Does he struggle to find words, or call things by the wrong name?
All the time. He asks when I'm coming three times in one call.
He says 'the thing, the thing' a lot.
D
Get it done

Getting lost and recognising people

Lost somewhere familiar, when and where; knows people; knows the day

Has he ever got lost somewhere familiar? When, and where?
Does he still know who people are?
Does he know what day it is?
Last month he got lost driving to the mosque. He's been going there thirty years. He rang us from a garage forecourt.
He knows us all. Days, he gets mixed up.
E
Get it done

Daily living

Washing, shaving, dressing; bills and money; cooker; hobbies dropped

How is he with washing, shaving and dressing?
Who handles the bills and money now?
Does he still use the cooker? Has he given up any hobbies?
His beard's a mess, and he buttons his shirt wrong.
He used to do all the bills. There were red letters, so I took over.
He can't follow the cricket on the radio any more. He burnt a pan last month.
F
Get it done

Screen the other causes

Low mood, visual hallucinations, fluctuation, falls, tremor, personality change, alcohol

Has his mood been low, or has he lost interest in life?
Does he ever see things that aren't there? Any falls, shaking or stiffness?
Has his personality changed: ruder, more impulsive? Does he drink alcohol?
He's quieter. Not sad, I don't think.
No, nothing like that.
No, he's still Dad. He's never drunk.
G
Get it done

Medical background and medicines

Stroke, heart, diabetes, head injury; all tablets incl. chemist sleep remedies; family history

Has he had a stroke, heart trouble, diabetes or a head injury?
What tablets does he take, including anything from the chemist for sleep?
Has anyone in the family had memory problems?
Just a blood pressure tablet. Amlodipine.
Not that I know of.
H
Get it done

Turn each risk into an action

Cooker, getting lost, scams: a named action for each before she leaves

Has he left the cooker on, or been caught by a phone or doorstep scam?
Let's ask his bank about card limits and a trusted contact.
Our occupational therapist can look at the cooker.
He paid a man on the phone three hundred pounds for boiler cover we didn't need.
I
Get it done

How is his wife coping?

Ask about the carer; carer's assessment from adult social care; number today

And how is your mum coping with all this?
She's able to ask for a carer's assessment from adult social care. I'll give you the number today.
She's exhausted. She won't ask for help. She does everything.
J
Law and safety net

His consent and what you share

Record his consent; share only what he'd agree to; ask him before writing to family

Your dad has agreed we can talk. I'll check with him before I send anything to the family.
What you describe fits a memory illness like Alzheimer's, but we need bloods, memory tests and a scan before we can say for sure.
Is this dementia? Just tell me straight.
K
Law and safety net

Driving

Advise no driving until assessed; DVLA must be told if dementia diagnosed; GMC disclosure rule

Is he still driving? Any near misses?
I'll advise him not to drive until he's assessed, and I'll explain it to him myself.
To the shops, yes. He'll never stop. It's his whole life.
L
Law and safety net

Attorney and safeguarding

Lasting power of attorney while he has capacity; scams can be a safeguarding concern

Has your dad ever thought about a lasting power of attorney?
It's best set up while he can still make that decision himself.
We've never talked about it. Is it too late?
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Diagnosing Alzheimer's too early
Screen every other cause first; call it 'fits', not 'is'.
Forgetting anticholinergic medicines
Ask about chemist sleep remedies, bladder tablets, painkillers.
Missing Lewy body dementia
Always ask about visual hallucinations, fluctuation and falls.
Leaving driving until the diagnosis
Advise no driving until assessed, today.
Never asking how his wife is coping
Ask, then offer a carer's assessment.
Finish

Close it well

YouWhat you describe, a steady change over a year starting with memory and words, fits a memory illness such as Alzheimer's disease. We need tests before we can say for sure.
YouBlood tests, which he's having now, memory testing in clinic, and a brain scan. Those rule out other causes, like thyroid or vitamin problems.
YouCan you tell me the next steps, as you understand them?
ZainabTests, no driving for now, the form, and help for Mum.
YouExactly. Here's our duty number if things change before clinic. I'll write to the GP today.
