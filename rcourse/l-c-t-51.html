Lewy body dementia: Gerald's grandson asks what can be done

Tuesday 14:00, memory clinic · Older adults memory clinic, community mental health base

The task

Mr Gerald Okafor is an 82-year-old widower whose memory has worsened over the past 18 months. Within the last nine months he has developed parkinsonian stiffness and slowness and has been falling often. He reports vivid visual hallucinations: he sees his late wife and his own mother and chats with them as though they are present, and he also sees a cat, a fox and children's faces around the house. A DaT scan supports dementia with Lewy bodies. Rivastigmine was started and he now takes 3 mg twice a day. Lately he has become more unsettled at home, and relatives are growing anxious. His grandson, Ellis Okafor, has come to the memory clinic. Please speak with Ellis about his grandfather's behaviour and the further management options, exploring what he thinks is happening, his main fears, and his hopes for Gerald's care.

Your job: Speak with Ellis about his grandfather's behaviour, check for a new cause of the decline, explain dementia with Lewy bodies and the scan, go through the treatment options honestly, and address his ideas, concerns and expectations.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Ask what Ellis knows and hopes for before explaining
  2. Get the visions in his words: comforting or frightening
  3. Ask about fluctuation, sleep, falls, aggression
  4. Rule out delirium: infection, constipation, pain, new drugs, onset
  5. Explain DLB and the DaT scan; honest prognosis
  6. Rivastigmine up to 4.5 then 6 mg bd; warn: no antipsychotics
  7. Non-drug plan, OT/physio, carers' assessment, LPA, review
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors. Are you Ellis, Gerald's grandson?
EllisYes, that's me.
YouThanks for coming in. Gerald is happy for me to discuss his care with you, so we can speak openly.
EllisGood, because we're worried sick.
YouI can hear that. Could you tell me what's been happening at home, and then we'll talk about what can help? First, what do you already know, and what were you hoping for today?
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Gerald's consent, knowledge, hopes)
  • 2. What Ellis has seen (visions, swings, nights, falls, aggression)
  • 3. New causes (infection, constipation, pain, new drugs, onset)
  • 4. The illness (Lewy bodies, three core features, DaT scan, outlook)
  • 5. Medication (rivastigmine up, levodopa caution, no antipsychotics)
  • 6. Managing at home (don't argue, lighting, OT, falls physio, tablets)
  • 7. Carers and planning (carers' assessment, LPA, driving)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, safety net, memory nurse, 4 weeks)

Get it done

Explaining Lewy body dementia

Lewy bodies; visions, fluctuation, stiffness, sleep; DaT scan; vs Parkinson's dementia; progressive

Small protein deposits, called Lewy bodies, collect inside brain cells and upset the messengers for alertness, memory and movement.
His scan looks at the cells that use dopamine. They're working less than they should.
I'm sorry, it's a progressive illness. The tablets can help for a while, but they don't cure it.
But why isn't it just Parkinson's?
And will he get better with the tablets?
F
Get it done

Rivastigmine: raise the dose

3 mg bd to 4.5 mg bd, then 6 mg bd after 2 weeks; pulse and ECG; patch option

His rivastigmine often reduces the visions. I'd like to raise it to 4.5 milligrams twice a day, and to 6 in two weeks if he copes.
Side effects are feeling sick, loose stools, poor appetite and weight loss. Less often, a slow heart.
Any side effects?
G
Law and safety net

No antipsychotics; levodopa with care

Severe antipsychotic sensitivity; avoid haloperidol; quetiapine only by specialist; levodopa can worsen visions

People with this illness can react badly to antipsychotics, even small doses. It can be fatal. We avoid them.
Only in severe distress or danger would a specialist consider a tiny dose of quetiapine, with close checks.
Levodopa can ease stiffness, but can make the visions worse. Not for now.
I read antipsychotics could kill him. Is that true?
Thank God. I was terrified someone would give him one.

Managing at home

Don't argue; go along gently; reassure and move room; lighting; OT, falls physio; blister pack

Please don't argue with what he sees. If seeing Gran comforts him, go along gently.
If the children's faces scare him, reassure him and move him to another room.
Good lighting, night lights, fewer shadows, glasses on.
We've been telling him Gran's dead. He gets so upset.

The carers

Mum exhausted; Care Act 2014 carers' assessment; respite; care home fears

Driving and attorney

DVLA must be told; should not drive; LPAs registered with the OPG

While he can still take part, a lasting power of attorney lets him choose who decides for him later.
