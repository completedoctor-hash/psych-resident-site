Explaining Alzheimer's disease and its management to a daughter

Friday 14:30, older adult community team · Memory clinic, quiet interview room

The task

Margaret Oduya, aged 78, was seen recently in the older adult community team clinic. Her ACE-III score was 46/100 and HADS showed anxiety 4/21 and depression 1/21. MRI reported marked global atrophy for her age, with bilateral mesial temporal and hippocampal atrophy and clear parietal involvement. All bloods, including B12, folate, thyroid and syphilis serology, were normal. She has osteoporosis and diverticular disease and takes alendronic acid 70 mg weekly, calcium with colecalciferol, paracetamol 1 g four times daily and lactulose. She lives alone in her three-bedroom house, does not drink, has no carers and manages her own daily tasks. She has consented to you speaking with her daughter, Claire. Please explain the diagnosis and management of Alzheimer's disease to Claire.

Your job: Explain the results and the diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease in plain words, then the medication, non-drug help, safety at home and planning ahead, checking understanding throughout.
Tell yourself first

Keep it sharp

Given this task, I need to:

  1. Find out what Claire knows and fears before any result
  2. Give results in plain words, a bit at a time; pause if overwhelmed
  3. Name it: Alzheimer's disease, moderate stage, progressive, not her fault
  4. Donepezil: how it works, benefit, side effects, pulse check; memantine later
  5. Home safety: cooking, medicines, falls with osteoporosis, scams, driving
  6. LPA, social care, carer's assessment
  7. Teach-back, written info, follow-up in 4 weeks
The exact words

Your opening

YouHello, I'm Dr Chinonso, one of the psychiatry doctors in the older adults team. Are you Claire Oduya?
ClaireYes, Margaret's daughter.
YouThanks for coming, Claire. Your mum has said I can talk to you about her results. How has she been, and what do you know so far?
ClaireOnly that the scan's back. I'm nervous. She's been forgetting things for about a year.
YouIt's natural to feel nervous. What's worrying you most?
ClaireThat it's dementia. And that she's on her own.
Structure

What you have to cover

  • 1. Opening (name, Margaret's consent, what Claire knows, her fears)
  • 2. Results in plain words (score 46, scan shrinkage, normal bloods, not depression)
  • 3. Diagnosis (Alzheimer's, moderate, progressive, not her fault)
  • 4. Medication (donepezil, side effects, pulse, memantine later)
  • 5. Non-drug help (CST, dementia adviser, memory aids)
  • 6. Living alone and risk (cooking, medicines, falls, scams, driving)
  • 7. Planning ahead (LPA, care assessment, carer's assessment)
  • 8. Summary and close (teach-back, leaflets, safety net, 4-week review)

Results, one bit at a time

Low memory score; shrinkage in memory areas; normal bloods; not depression

Her memory score was low, well below what we'd expect for her age.
Her scan shows shrinkage in the parts of the brain for memory and finding her way around.
Her blood tests were normal, and it doesn't look like depression.
Please stop. It's too much. I can't take it all in.
Shrinkage? What does that mean?

Name it: Alzheimer's disease

Commonest dementia; moderate; proteins damage cells; progressive; not her fault

Putting it together, your mum has Alzheimer's disease.
Dementia is the umbrella term. Alzheimer's is the commonest type. Hers is at a moderate stage.
Shall I pause there?
I knew it. I think I knew it.
I should have noticed sooner.
How long has she got?

Get it done

Donepezil

Boosts acetylcholine; 5 mg at night, 10 mg after 4 weeks; GI upset, dreams, slow pulse; memantine later

There's a tablet called donepezil. It stops a memory chemical being broken down so quickly.
It doesn't cure it, but can help memory and daily life for a while.
We check her pulse first, start low at night, and increase after a month.
My neighbour's on a memory tablet. Can Mum have that?
Are there side effects?

Help that isn't a tablet

Cognitive stimulation therapy 2x/week for 7 weeks; dementia adviser; calendar clock

There's a group called cognitive stimulation therapy, twice a week for seven weeks.
A dementia adviser can help with routines, a calendar clock, labels and photos.
There's support for you too.
She'd like a group. She loves company.

Living alone: cooking and medicines

Burnt pan; missed bills; OT for cooker cut-off and alarm; blister pack; alendronic acid

You mentioned she's on her own. Any problems with cooking, medicines, bills or getting lost?
An occupational therapist can fit a cooker cut-off, a falls sensor and an alarm.
The pharmacy can help with a blister pack for her tablets.
She burnt a pan last month. She's missed bills.
She insists she's fine.

Falls and scams

Osteoporosis: fall means fracture; falls sensor; bank fraud protection; call blocker

Has she had any falls or trips?
With her osteoporosis, a fall could mean a broken hip, so I'd like the falls team involved.
Her bank can add fraud protection, and a call blocker stops cold callers.
She gave her bank details to someone on the phone.

Law and safety net

Driving

Must tell DVLA; not safe now; GMC: doctor may inform DVLA if she continues

Does she still drive?
With these results, it isn't safe, and she must tell the DVLA.
I'll talk to her about it myself.
To the shops. She'd hate to stop.
She'll never give up driving. It's her independence.

Lasting powers of attorney

MCA 2005: capacity is decision-specific; two LPAs while she can

While she can still make decisions, it's worth her making lasting powers of attorney.
One is for health and welfare, one is for money and property.
I didn't know about that.

Law and safety net

Care and carer's assessment

Care Act 2014: needs assessment for Margaret; carer's assessment for Claire

How much can you realistically do alongside work?
Social services can assess your mum for help at home as things change.
You can ask for a carer's assessment yourself.
I work full time, though. I'm ten minutes away.
Watch out

Traps to avoid

Jargon
'Shrinkage in the memory areas', not 'hippocampal atrophy'
Avoiding the word Alzheimer's
Name it clearly, then pause
Promising the tablet stops it
'It doesn't cure it, but can help for a while'
Forgetting driving, scams and falls
Ask about each; act on each
No written information or date
Leaflets, contact number, review in 4 weeks
Finish

YouThat's spot on. Here are leaflets from the Alzheimer's Society and our number. If she gets suddenly more confused, falls, or is found lost, call us or her GP.
YouI'll see your mum with you in four weeks to start the plan. What questions do you have?
