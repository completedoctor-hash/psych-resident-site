Challenge 3 · 13.8 Intention to treat, missing data and meta-analysis · 3.2.25
A reviewer wants to assess whether a meta-analysis of 20 trials of a new antidepressant may be affected by publication bias. Which plot is used for this purpose?
  1. Kaplan-Meier plot
  2. Scatter plot
  3. Funnel plot
  4. Dot plot
  5. ROC plot
Show the working
Answer: C Funnel plot

Small studies produce scattered estimates and large studies produce precise ones, so a plot of effect size (horizontal) against precision or sample size (vertical) should look like an inverted funnel, wide at the bottom and narrow at the top, symmetrical about the pooled estimate. If small studies with unimpressive results have gone unpublished, the bottom corner on the null side is empty and the funnel is lopsided. Egger's regression test and trim-and-fill are the statistical companions.

For the 20 antidepressant trials, the reviewer would plot each trial's effect against its standard error. A symmetrical funnel is reassuring; a gap of small trials on the null side suggests that negative small studies are missing.

The exam often adds that asymmetry can have other causes, such as true small-study effects or heterogeneity, so a lopsided funnel is suggestive rather than proof. Funnel plots also need around ten studies to be interpretable.

Rule: Publication bias = funnel plot; asymmetry with missing small null studies is the warning sign.
Trap: Confusing the funnel plot (publication bias) with the Galbraith or L'Abbe plot (heterogeneity).

Galbraith plot of 8 trials of a nurse-led relapse prevention programme in bipolar disorder (odds ratio of staying well at 12 months)
The Galbraith (radial) plot shows eight trials of a nurse-led relapse prevention programme in bipolar disorder. Each trial's standardised effect (log odds ratio divided by its standard error) is plotted against its precision (1 divided by standard error), with a regression line through the origin and dashed lines two units above and below it. Which trial contributes most to the heterogeneity of this meta-analysis?
  1. Haddad 2011
  2. Ali 2021
  3. Varga 2015
  4. Schmidt 2013
  5. Nakamura 2019
Show the working
Answer: C Varga 2015

A Galbraith plot rescales each trial so that its vertical position is the effect divided by its standard error (a z statistic) and its horizontal position is precision. A line through the origin with slope equal to the pooled effect passes through the trials if they are homogeneous, and 95% of them should fall within 2 units above or below it. A trial outside the band is contributing more than its share of heterogeneity, and the plot makes it easy to spot even among many studies.

The fitted slope here is about 0.47 (log OR). Varga 2015 has precision 1/0.25 = 4, so its expected z is about 1.9, but its observed z is 1.40/0.25 = 5.6, roughly 3.7 units above the line. Every other trial sits within the band: Haddad 2011, the most precise, at precision 10 has z 4.2 against an expected 4.7, half a unit below the line.

The exam may ask which study to remove in a sensitivity analysis or which study explains a high I2; the Galbraith outlier is the answer. Know too that the horizontal distance from the origin tells you precision, so the far-right point is the largest trial.

Rule: On a Galbraith plot the trial outside the plus or minus 2 band is the source of heterogeneity.
Trap: Picking the far-left (least precise) or far-right (most precise) point rather than the one outside the band.

Answer: C Heterogeneity cannot be assessed within the milnacipran subgroup because it contains a single trial

Cochran's Q sums the weighted squared deviations of each study from the subgroup pooled estimate and is tested on k minus 1 degrees of freedom. Challenge 14 · 13.8 Intention to treat, missing data and meta-analysis · 3.2.23
A meta-analysis combines trials of a psychological therapy for anxiety in which some trials measured outcome with the Beck Anxiety Inventory, some with the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale and some with the GAD-7. Which summary measure allows these continuous outcomes to be pooled?
  1. Weighted mean difference
  2. Standardised mean difference
  3. Pooled odds ratio
  4. Pooled relative risk
  5. Weighted kappa
Show the working
Answer: B Standardised mean difference

When trials measure the same thing on different instruments, a difference of 4 points means something different on each, so raw mean differences cannot be averaged. The standardised mean difference (Cohen's d or Hedges' g) rescales each trial's effect in standard deviation units: mean difference divided by the pooled within-group standard deviation. A d of 0.5 means the groups differ by half a standard deviation, whatever the scale, so the trials become comparable.

Here the BAI, HAM-A and GAD-7 all measure anxiety severity but have different ranges and item counts. Converting each trial's result to an SMD lets the reviewer pool them. The cost is interpretability: an SMD of 0.4 must be translated back into scale points for clinicians, and the usual benchmarks are 0.2 small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

The exam pairs this with the same-scale version, where the weighted mean difference is correct. The deciding word is whether the scales are 'different' or 'the same'.

Rule: Different scales measuring the same construct = standardised mean difference.

A funnel plot places each trial's effect estimate on the horizontal axis and its precision (usually the standard error, inverted so that large trials sit at the top) on the vertical axis.
