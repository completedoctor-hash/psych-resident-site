3.2.9Prevalence and incidence

Big idea. Incidence counts the people arriving, prevalence counts the people present, and the gap between them is how long the illness lasts.
Picture it as a bath with a tap and a plughole.

Syllabus: Point, period and lifetime prevalence; cumulative incidence and incidence rate

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 30 · Incidence, prevalence, ratios and rates →

The story in plain words. Picture a bath. The tap is incidence, the new cases arriving. The plughole is recovery and death, the cases leaving. The level in the bath is prevalence, everyone who has the condition right now. Prevalence comes in three forms. Point prevalence is the level at one instant: existing cases divided by the population at that moment. Period prevalence counts anyone who had the condition at any time in a stated period, new or old. Lifetime prevalence counts anyone who has ever had it. Incidence comes in two. Cumulative incidence, also called risk, is new cases over a period divided by the people at risk at the start, and it is meaningless without its time frame. The incidence rate divides new cases by total person-time at risk, as in cases per 1,000 person-years, which copes with people followed for different lengths of time. ((? Can someone who already has the disease be in the denominator? ?)) No. Only those free of it at the start are at risk.

Prevalence is the level in the bath; incidence is the tap; recovery and death are the plug. A longer illness raises prevalence even when incidence is unchanged

The people and the evidence. Kirkwood and Sterne set out the relationship the exam returns to: for a stable, reasonably uncommon condition, prevalence is approximately incidence multiplied by average duration. That one line explains most of what confuses candidates. A treatment that keeps people alive without curing them narrows the plughole, so prevalence rises while incidence is untouched. A cure, or a rapidly fatal course, empties the bath faster and lowers prevalence with the tap running exactly as before.

From everyday life to the clinic. The two measures answer different questions and are commissioned for different reasons. Incidence is the measure for studying causes, because it counts the act of becoming ill. Prevalence is the measure for planning services, because it counts the people who need a bed, a clinic slot or a care coordinator this year. So a service whose prevalence is climbing is not necessarily failing. Schizophrenia has a low incidence and a high prevalence precisely because it lasts, and better long-term care raises prevalence by design.

Who is in the clinic in June (the dashed line)? A, B and E are the point prevalence; B, D and E are the year's new cases (incidence); C has recovered and is counted in neither. Long illnesses like A's keep prevalence high

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is the stem that reports a rising prevalence and invites you to conclude that more people are falling ill. It may equally mean people are living longer with the illness. The second is cumulative incidence given without a time frame, which makes it uninterpretable. The third is the person-time denominator: an incidence rate per 1,000 person-years is not the same as a risk per 1,000 people, and the exam will offer you both.

3.2.10Survival analysis

Big idea. Time-to-event data are always incomplete, because the study ends before everyone has had the event, and survival analysis exists so that incomplete follow-up still counts for something.
Picture it as a race where the clock stops before everyone finishes.

Syllabus: Censoring, Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank test, hazard ratios, Cox regression, relative survival

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 96 · Survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier →

The story in plain words. Imagine timing a race where the stopwatch stops at an hour. Some runners have finished and you know their times. Others were still going, and all you know is that they lasted at least an hour. Throwing those runners away would bias the result; pretending they finished at an hour would be worse. That is censoring, and it is the whole problem. A censored person contributes their time at risk up to the moment they left, whether the study closed, they were lost to follow-up, or they died of something else. They are not discarded and they are not counted as events. Ordinary t tests and chi-square cannot do this, which is why survival analysis is a separate discipline. The Kaplan-Meier curve plots the proportion still event-free against time as a series of steps. Each step down is an event; the small ticks are censored people leaving. Median survival is where the curve crosses 50%, and if it never crosses, the median is not reached and should be reported as such.

Reading a Kaplan-Meier plot: steps are events, ticks are censoring, the numbers at risk shrink with time, and the median is read where each curve crosses 50%

The people and the evidence. Bewick and colleagues (2004) set out the method clinicians actually need. The log-rank test compares two or more curves on a single factor, using every time point and every censored observation, which is why it beats comparing survival at one arbitrary moment. Spruance and colleagues (2004) wrote the paper on the hazard ratio that the exam quietly leans on. The hazard is the instantaneous event rate among those still at risk. The hazard ratio compares hazards between groups and assumes they stay in proportion over time. The null value is 1.

From everyday life to the clinic. Cox proportional hazards regression is the workhorse, because it models time to event while adjusting for several covariates at once and reports adjusted hazard ratios. It is how a trial accounts for age, sex and baseline severity without splitting the sample. Relative survival is the cancer registry's answer to a practical problem: observed survival divided by the survival expected in a matched general population. It estimates survival as if the disease were the only cause of death, without needing cause-of-death data at all. ((? A five-year relative survival of 80 per cent means what? ?)) Patients reached 80 per cent of the survival expected for people of their age and sex.

Six people, six different follow-up lengths: Ben and Eve relapsed; Chen moved away; Amira, Dev and Femi were still well when the study closed. The Kaplan-Meier method uses every month each person contributed

Where the exam tries to catch you. A hazard ratio of 0.6 means a 40% lower rate of the event at any given moment. It does not mean 60% fewer events, and it does not mean patients live 40% longer. The second trap is the tail of a Kaplan-Meier curve. Late steps are large because few people remain at risk, so a dramatic late separation may rest on three patients. The third is forgetting that proportional hazards is an assumption: if the curves cross, the hazard ratio is not describing anything real.

3.2.11Mortality statistics

Big idea. Two populations cannot be compared on deaths alone, because one may simply be older, and standardisation is how that difference is taken out before anyone draws a conclusion.
Picture it as comparing two towns, one a seaside retirement town.

Syllabus: Crude and cause-specific death rates, case fatality, standardisation and the standardised mortality ratio

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 37 · Mortality statistics and standardisation →

The story in plain words. The crude death rate is all deaths divided by the population, usually per 1,000 a year. It is honest and almost useless for comparison, because a seaside retirement town will always bury more people per thousand than a university city at exactly the same underlying risk. Three relatives are worth separating. The cause-specific death rate is deaths from one cause over the whole population. Case fatality is deaths from a disease divided by the people who had it, so it measures how lethal the illness is rather than how much of it there is. Proportional mortality is the share of all deaths due to one cause, and it rises when other causes fall. Direct standardisation fixes the age problem by applying each population's age-specific rates to one standard population, so you compare adjusted rates. ((? Crude rates differ but adjusted rates match. What explains the difference? ?)) Age structure, and nothing else.

The standardised mortality ratio is observed over expected: here 60 / 34 = 1.76, or an SMR of 176, so this group died at 1.76 times the standard rate after allowing for its age structure

The people and the evidence. Direct standardisation needs reliable age-specific rates, which means it suits large populations where each age band has enough deaths to be stable. For a small population those rates bounce around and the method fails. Indirect standardisation inverts the arithmetic. Apply the standard population's age-specific rates to your population's age structure, and you get the deaths you would have expected. Compare that with what actually happened.

From everyday life to the clinic. That comparison is the standardised mortality ratio: observed deaths divided by expected deaths, written as a ratio around 1.0 or as a percentage around 100. An SMR of 2.5, or 250, is two and a half times the expected mortality. This is the figure behind the single most quoted fact in psychiatry. Severe mental illness carries an SMR of about 2 to 3, and because it is standardised, that excess is not explained by the age of the population. It is the argument for every physical health check on every care plan.

The seaside town has a higher crude death rate simply because it is older. Standardisation asks: how many deaths would each town have if both had the same age mix?

Where the exam tries to catch you. Case fatality and mortality rate are different animals and the stem will swap them. Case fatality has the diseased as its denominator; the mortality rate has the whole population. The second trap is proportional mortality, which can rise simply because another cause of death has been conquered. The third is reading an SMR of 250 as 250% more deaths. It is 250 per cent of expected, which is 150% more.

Sources
  1. Bland JM, Altman DG (1998). Survival probabilities (the Kaplan-Meier method). BMJ, 317: 1572.

Answer: A Kaplan-Meier curves plotted as cumulative incidence

Kaplan-Meier curves can be drawn either way up. The conventional form starts at 1 and steps down, showing the proportion still free of the event; the cumulative incidence form is simply 1 minus that, starting at 0 and rising as events occur. Both are the same estimator and both handle censoring. The rising form is often chosen when the event is the thing of interest, such as transition to psychosis, rather than survival from it.

In this figure the horizontal axis is days since recruitment and the vertical axis is the proportion who had made the transition, with one curve per study rising from zero. That is a set of Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence curves.

Every survival analysis needs a time origin. For cohort follow-ups it is usually the date of recruitment or the date a defining criterion was met.
