3.2.12.6Number needed to treat and to harm

Big idea. The number needed to treat turns a percentage into a number of real people, which is the only form of the result a patient can picture.
Picture it as how many people in the waiting room before one is helped.

Syllabus: Number needed to treat, number needed to harm, and their interpretation

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 19 · NNT and NNH →

The story in plain words. Fill the waiting room and ask how many you would have to treat before one of them benefits who would not have benefited anyway. That is the number needed to treat, and it is simply 1 divided by the absolute risk reduction, always rounded up to a whole person. An ARR of 20% gives an NNT of 5. An ARR of 15% gives 7. An ARR of 1% gives 100. A lower NNT means a more effective treatment, an NNT of 1 would be perfect, and the null value is infinity, because when the treatment does nothing you could treat forever. The mirror image is the number needed to harm, which is 1 divided by the absolute risk increase. Weight gain in 15% on the drug against 5% on placebo gives an NNH of 10.

NNT is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction: ARR 20% gives NNT 5, ARR 1% gives NNT 100, and ARR 0 gives an infinite NNT. Always round NNT up

The people and the evidence. Altman (1998) set out how to put a confidence interval on an NNT, and the method is the exam's favourite piece of awkwardness. Invert the limits of the confidence interval for the ARR. The awkward case follows directly. If the ARR interval crosses 0, the NNT interval runs through infinity, because somewhere inside that range the treatment does nothing at all. An NNT quoted without an interval is hiding that possibility.

From everyday life to the clinic. An NNT is meaningless without three companions: the comparator, the outcome and the time frame. An NNT of 5 for preventing relapse over one year and an NNT of 50 for preventing a death over five years can both be well worth having, and neither can be judged against the other. The use at the bedside is to set NNT against NNH for the same drug. ((? NNT 10 for response and NNH 10 for significant weight gain. What does that tell you? ?)) That benefit and harm are equally likely, and the conversation now depends on how the patient weighs the two.

NNT 5: treat five people and one of them is spared a relapse they would otherwise have had. The other four take the drug without that benefit

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is arithmetic: the ARR must be a decimal. An ARR of 20% is 0.2, so the NNT is 5, not 0.05. The second is rounding. NNT is rounded up, because you cannot treat four and a half people. The third is the null: infinity, not 0 and not 1, which is why a non-significant trial produces an NNT range that includes it.

Power is the probability of detecting a true difference of a stated size. It depends on sample size, the minimum important difference, the variability of the outcome and alpha. Response rates speak to non-response bias, a separate problem. Without a sample size calculation, power cannot be stated.

Challenge 3 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.6.6
For which question would a crossover design be LEAST appropriate?
  1. Melatonin versus placebo for sleep onset in chronic insomnia
  2. Nicotine patch versus placebo for craving in daily smokers
  3. Two short-acting analgesics for chronic back pain
  4. Methylphenidate versus placebo for stable adult ADHD
  5. CBT versus waiting list for a first depressive episode
Show the working
Answer: E CBT versus waiting list for a first depressive episode

Crossover trials need conditions that return to baseline between periods. Psychological therapies teach skills that persist, and first episodes may remit naturally. Both break the assumption that each period is comparable.

A design that needs a return to baseline cannot test treatments with lasting effects. Skills learned in therapy do not wash out.

Rule: Crossover is unsuitable for curative, lasting or progressive conditions.
Trap: Forgetting that lasting treatment effects make crossover invalid.

Challenge 4 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.6.5
Researchers want to test a collaborative care model for depression in primary care. They worry that GPs trained in the model would use it with every patient in their practice. What design best avoids this problem?
  1. Individually randomised trial with blinding
  2. Crossover trial
  3. Cluster randomised trial with practices as the unit of randomisation
  4. n of 1 trial
  5. Factorial trial
Show the working
Answer: C Cluster randomised trial with practices as the unit of randomisation

When an intervention is delivered through staff or services, individual randomisation lets it leak into the control arm. Cluster randomisation solves this but patients within a cluster are alike, so the sample size must be inflated by the design effect.

Contamination arises when the intervention spreads to control patients through shared clinicians. Randomising the practice keeps the arms apart.

Rule: Intervention delivered at practice or ward level, risk of contamination = cluster randomisation.
Trap: Thinking blinding prevents contamination.

Source: Campbell MK, Piaggio G, Elbourne DR, Altman DG (2012). CONSORT 2010 statement: extension to cluster randomised trials. BMJ, 345: e5661.

Challenge 6 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.6.3
A trial of three doses of a new antidepressant prespecifies that, at an interim analysis, any arm with clearly poor response will be dropped and more new participants will be allocated to the better-performing arms. What is this design?
  1. Adaptive trial
  2. Factorial trial
  3. Crossover trial
  4. Trial stopped early for benefit
  5. Per-protocol trial
Show the working
Answer: A Adaptive trial

Adaptive designs can drop arms, re-estimate sample size or change allocation ratios (response-adaptive randomisation). The changes must be planned in the protocol, with control of the type I error, or the trial loses its integrity.

The key word is prespecified: changes driven by interim data are planned in the protocol. Unplanned changes would threaten the type I error.

Rule: Pre-specified modifications using interim data = adaptive design.
Trap: Thinking any mid-trial change counts as adaptive.

Source: Pallmann P, Bedding AW, Choodari-Oskooei B et al. (2018). Adaptive designs in clinical trials: why use them, and how to run and report them. BMC Med, 16: 29.

Challenge 9 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.5
Appraising a pragmatic trial of a community crisis service against usual care, which feature should NOT be counted as a weakness?
  1. Allocation by alternate referral to the two services
  2. Primary outcome missing for a third of participants at 12 months
  3. Outcome assessors aware of allocation, using a bespoke unvalidated scale
  4. Broad inclusion criteria that admit people with comorbid substance use

Answer: D Broad inclusion criteria that admit people with comorbid substance use

Pragmatic trials deliberately accept heterogeneous patients and usual care to answer whether an intervention works in routine practice. Challenge 10 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.6.7
A crossover trial of a short-acting hypnotic reports results from both periods combined. Which appraisal question is most specific to this design?

In a crossover trial each participant receives both treatments. The design is valid only if the condition is stable and the first treatment no longer acts when the second begins. Appraisal checks washout length, tests for carry-over and period effects, and whether the analysis is paired.

Challenge 11 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.1.4
Which of the following is NOT a proper method of random allocation?
  1. A computer-generated random number list
  2. A random list prepared and held by an independent trials unit
  3. Permuted blocks of variable size
  4. Random allocation of whole wards
  5. Allocation by odd or even date of birth
Show the working
Answer: E Allocation by odd or even date of birth

Quasi-random methods follow a predictable rule. Anyone recruiting can know the next allocation and may enrol or exclude patients accordingly. Such trials overestimate treatment effects compared with properly randomised ones.

Proper methods leave the next allocation unpredictable. Date of birth, alternation and record number do not.

Rule: Birth date, hospital number or alternation = quasi-randomisation.
Trap: Accepting systematic methods because they look unbiased.

Source: Schulz KF, Grimes DA (2002). Generation of allocation sequences in randomised trials: chance, not choice. Lancet, 359: 515-519.

Challenge 12 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.1.4
Which allocation method is LEAST adequate for a randomised trial?
  1. Tossing a coin for each participant
  2. Minimisation by a central computer
  3. Permuted block randomisation
  4. Allocating alternate patients as they attend clinic
  5. Computer-generated random numbers
Show the working
Answer: D Allocating alternate patients as they attend clinic

A coin toss is crude but genuinely random. Alternation, odd or even dates and record numbers are not random at all. Because the next allocation can be predicted, recruiters can steer patients towards an arm.

Look for the method where a recruiter could predict the next arm. Alternation is the most transparent of these.

Rule: Alternation is the weakest: predictable and unconcealable.
Trap: Rejecting the coin toss as least adequate.

Challenge 13 · 13.3 Experimental studies and trials · 13.3.1.4
In a trial of a new antipsychotic, the recruiting psychiatrist telephones a trials unit in another city after consenting each patient, and only then is told which arm the patient is in. What does this procedure protect?

  2. Allocation concealment

Answer: B Allocation concealment

Allocation concealment protects the random sequence until the moment of assignment. Central telephone or web randomisation is the most secure method; sealed opaque envelopes are weaker. Inadequate concealment exaggerates treatment effects.

Allocation revealed by a remote third party only after consent is the gold standard for concealment. It protects against selection bias.

What does allocation concealment refer to?

Concealment and blinding are often confused. Concealment is always possible, even in surgery or psychotherapy trials. Blinding operates after allocation and is not always possible.

Concealment is about predicting the next allocation before it happens. Blinding is about knowledge after it happens.

Per-protocol analysis can be biased because adherent patients differ from non-adherent ones. It is usually reported alongside ITT. In non-inferiority trials it matters more, because ITT can make treatments look similar.
