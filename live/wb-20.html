13.7.1Qualitative methodologies

Big idea. Each qualitative tradition is defined by the question it asks: explain a process, describe an experience, read a culture or change practice.
Picture it as four tools in a drawer, each shaped for one job.

Syllabus: Qualitative research methodologies including grounded theory, phenomenological, ethnographic and other methodologies

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 115 · Qualitative approaches →

The story in plain words. Methodology is the thinking behind the method. Interviews can serve any tradition; what differs is what the researcher is trying to produce. Starks and Trinidad (2007) put it simply: choose the approach by the kind of answer you want. Grounded theory builds a theory of a social process from the data. Phenomenology describes what an experience is like for the person living it. Ethnography studies a culture through long immersion. Action research changes practice and studies the change in the same cycles. In descriptive phenomenology the researcher tries to set aside prior assumptions, called bracketing or epoché. ((? How does bracketing differ from reflexivity? ?)) Bracketing tries to suspend the researcher's assumptions; reflexivity openly examines and declares them, accepting that they cannot be switched off.

The people and the evidence. Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss published their method in 1967, after studying how hospital staff managed dying patients. Their tools were constant comparison and theoretical sampling, continued until theoretical saturation. Jonathan Smith later developed IPA, interpretative phenomenological analysis, with small, alike samples, usually fewer than 10 people, analysed case by case. Erving Goffman spent a year at St Elizabeths Hospital in Washington and wrote Asylums (1961), an ethnography of the total institution that still shapes how wards are understood. Julienne Meyer (2000) described action research in health care as cycles of plan, act, observe and reflect, with participants as co-researchers.

From everyday life to the clinic. Picture four visitors to a new town. One works out how the bus system came to run the way it does: grounded theory. One asks a resident what it feels like to live there: phenomenology. One moves in for a year and learns the unwritten rules: ethnography. One joins the residents' committee to fix the buses: action research. On a secure ward, a researcher who volunteers for nine months and writes field notes about the culture around restraint is doing ethnography. Service users, nurses and a researcher who redesign ward rounds over several cycles are doing participatory action research.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Glaser and Strauss, constant comparison or theoretical sampling in the stem means grounded theory. Lived experience with a small sample analysed case by case means IPA, not grounded theory just because there are interviews. Field notes plus months of immersion means ethnography, even if staff experiences are described. A cycle of changing practice with blurred roles is action research, not audit, because it builds new knowledge rather than measuring against a standard.

13.7.2Sampling in qualitative research

Big idea. Qualitative samples are chosen for what people can tell you, and they stop growing when the story stops changing.
Picture it as a sponge that soaks until nothing more goes in.

Syllabus: Sampling techniques, including convenience sampling, purposive sampling, quota sampling, snowball sampling, chain referral sampling, theoretical sampling, data saturation and other techniques

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 121 · Qualitative sampling and saturation →

The story in plain words. Quantitative samples aim to represent a population, so they are drawn at random and sized by a power calculation. Qualitative samples aim for depth and range, so participants are chosen on purpose: purposive sampling. Maximum variation sampling deliberately picks people who differ, to see what holds across difference. Homogeneous sampling, used in IPA, picks people who are alike, to see one experience closely. In grounded theory, theoretical sampling lets the emerging analysis decide who comes next. Recruitment stops at saturation, when new interviews add no new themes, which is why collection and analysis must overlap. ((? A reviewer criticises a qualitative study for having no power calculation. Is that fair? ?)) No. Qualitative sample size is set by saturation and by how rich the data are, not by power.

The people and the evidence. Guest, Bunce and Johnson (2006) coded 60 interviews and found that saturation had largely occurred within the first 12, with the basic elements of the main themes present by 6. Malterud and colleagues (2016) later argued for information power: the more focused the aim and the richer the dialogue, the fewer participants are needed. Leo Goodman described snowball sampling in 1961. Participants refer others, which reaches hidden groups with no sampling frame, such as people who use novel psychoactive substances. The cost is a sample drawn from one network, so it may lack diversity.

From everyday life to the clinic. A food critic reviewing a city's curry houses does not pick restaurants at random. She chooses a few cheap and a few expensive, some old and some new, and stops when another meal tells her nothing she did not already know. That is maximum variation sampling, finished at saturation. Quota sampling fills preset cells, say ten men and ten women in each of three age bands, with whoever is available. Stratified random sampling uses the same cells but draws at random within each. Convenience sampling takes whoever turns up and is the weakest.

Where the exam tries to catch you. When a stem says the next recruit was chosen to test an emerging idea, the answer is theoretical sampling, not purposive sampling in general. Do not confuse theoretical sampling, the process, with theoretical saturation, the stopping point. Quota against stratified is a favourite: both have cells, only stratified draws lots within them. And a deliberately diverse sample is maximum variation purposive sampling, not random sampling, however varied it looks.

13.7.3Collecting qualitative data

Big idea. The way you collect words shapes the words you get: structure buys comparability, freedom buys depth, and groups add interaction.
Picture it as a map that is fully drawn, half drawn or blank.

Syllabus: Data collection techniques, including semi structured interviews, in depth interviews, focus group, observation, diary methods, ethnographic interviews, role play and simulation, case study, voice recordings and other techniques

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 121 · Qualitative sampling and saturation →

The story in plain words. Interviews sit on a spectrum. A structured interview fixes the wording and order, which makes answers comparable. A semi-structured interview uses a topic guide with probes. An unstructured interview, also called in-depth, starts from one broad theme and follows the participant. Semi-structured interviews dominate health research because they cover the key topics within limited time while leaving room for the unexpected. Each interview is audio-recorded and turned into a verbatim transcript; field notes record what the recording cannot, such as setting and mood. Observation varies on two switches: whether people know they are watched, overt or covert, and whether the researcher joins in, participant or non-participant. ((? A researcher sits in on handover, everyone knows why she is there, and she says nothing. What is this? ?)) Overt non-participant observation.

The people and the evidence. Nicky Britten's paper for clinicians (1995) set out the interview spectrum and reminded readers that the interviewer is part of the data, which is why reflexivity matters at the analysis stage. Jenny Kitzinger (1995) described focus groups as discussions in which interaction between participants is itself the data. Kitzinger suggested groups of about 4 to 8, and most texts accept up to about 10: small enough for everyone to speak. Several groups are run because one may be dominated by a single voice.

From everyday life to the clinic. Asking about a holiday shows the difference. A form with tick boxes gets dates and costs. A chat over coffee with a few prompts gets the highlights. Letting a friend talk for an hour gets the story you would never have thought to ask about. Put six friends together and they start correcting each other: a focus group. In the clinic, a solicited diary, such as adults with bipolar disorder writing each evening for six weeks about mood and sleep, catches experience close to the event and reduces recall bias, though it depends on literacy and motivation.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Stems about meaning shaped through discussion, or comparing patients, relatives and staff, point to focus groups. An interviewer with no fixed order who follows the respondent's lead is running an unstructured interview, not a semi-structured one. Covert observation raises consent problems and is rarely justified. Bigger focus groups do not mean more data. A transcript is the verbatim record of speech, while field notes are the researcher's own record of what happened.

13.7.4Analysing qualitative data and showing rigour

Big idea. Qualitative analysis turns transcripts into themes through repeated, recorded judgement, and its rigour is shown by making that judgement visible.
Picture it as sorting a box of family photographs into albums, then asking the family to check them.

Syllabus: Data interpretation and analysis: thematic, content and discourse analysis, constant comparison, triangulation, reflexivity, bracketing and member checking

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 127 · Qualitative sampling and saturation →

The story in plain words. Braun and Clarke (2006) gave thematic analysis 6 phases: familiarise, generate initial codes, search for themes, review themes, define and name themes, and produce the report. The phases loop back; reviewing themes often sends the analyst back to recode. Content analysis, as set out by Hsieh and Shannon (2005), codes text into categories and counts them, so 400 free-text survey comments sorted into categories such as staff attitude and waiting time is content analysis. Discourse analysis treats language as action and asks what talk does, such as how insight is built and contested on a ward round. Constant comparison, the engine of grounded theory, compares each new piece of data with the emerging categories. Framework analysis charts data into a matrix of preset themes. ((? Why is framework analysis the wrong answer for a Glaser and Strauss study? ?)) It starts from preset themes, while grounded theory builds categories from the data.

The people and the evidence. Lincoln and Guba (1985) replaced validity and reliability with four criteria of trustworthiness: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. Mays and Pope (2000) set out practical checks for clinicians: triangulation, member checking (respondent validation), a clear account of methods, reflexivity, attention to deviant case analysis and fair dealing. Rosaline Barbour (2001) discussed multiple coding by more than one researcher, and warned that ticking these techniques off a checklist is not rigour in itself. Each one earns its place only when it is used to test the analysis, not to decorate it.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of sorting a box of family photographs. You look through them all, label each, group them into albums, check the albums still make sense, give each a title and then show the family. A cousin who sorts the same box separately is double coding; showing the albums to the people in them is member checking. In a crisis team study, comparing patient interviews with ward observation and case notes is triangulation. Two researchers coding carers' focus groups and resolving differences improves reliability. The interviewer who notes how her clinical role shapes the data is being reflexive.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Any option offering a p value for a theme is wrong in a rigour question. Triangulation does not need exactly three sources; it needs more than one angle on the same question. Reflexivity examines the researcher's influence; bracketing tries to suspend it. Member checking supports credibility, but participant agreement does not prove the analysis right. Cases that do not fit are investigated, never discarded as outliers.

13.7.5Turning attitudes into numbers: scales

Big idea. Attitude scales turn opinions into numbers, and each type does it by a different rule: sum, weigh, climb or measure.
Picture it as four ways to weigh a feeling: a tally, a set of weights, a ladder and a ruler.

Syllabus: Conversion of qualitative data to quantitative data: Thurstone, Likert, visual analogue and Guttman scales

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 14 · Types of data and scales →

The story in plain words. A Likert scale asks for agreement with statements, usually on 5 or 7 points from strongly disagree to strongly agree, and adds the item scores. The responses are ordinal, though totals are often treated as interval. A Thurstone scale does the weighing in advance. Judges sort many statements into 11 piles from least to most favourable, giving each a scale value, and the respondent's score is the median value of the statements they endorse. A Guttman scale is cumulative, so agreeing with a strong item implies agreeing with every weaker one. A visual analogue scale is a 100 mm line anchored at each end, and the score is the distance in millimetres from the left. ((? In a perfect Guttman scale, what does the total score tell you? ?)) Exactly which items were endorsed, because the items form a ladder.

The people and the evidence. Louis Thurstone argued in 1928 that attitudes can be measured, and built scales with equal-appearing intervals. Rensis Likert showed in 1932 that simply summing agreement ratings worked about as well for far less labour, which is why Likert scales took over. Emory Bogardus's social distance scale (1925) asked how close people would accept members of a group, from visitor to the country through to relative by marriage. Louis Guttman formalised the cumulative idea in 1944; how well real data fit is checked with the coefficient of reproducibility.

From everyday life to the clinic. A hotel feedback card that asks you to agree or disagree with five statements and adds them up is a Likert scale. A pain line in a recovery room, where you mark a cross between no pain and worst pain imaginable, is a visual analogue scale. A ladder where reaching rung four means you passed rungs one to three is Guttman. Choose the format for the person. A clinician rating change from very much improved to minimally worse is using a verbally anchored Likert-type scale. Adults with moderate intellectual disability do better with a pictorial faces scale, because abstract lines and agree-disagree items invite confusion and acquiescence.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Watch for statements with judge-assigned weights: that is Thurstone, even though respondents agree with statements as in a Likert scale. A single index of social closeness with cumulative items is Guttman, even though it gives a total. A visual analogue scale is a continuous line, not a row of boxes; a 0 to 10 numeric rating scale is a different instrument. Ordered word anchors on every point make a Likert-type scale, not a visual analogue scale.
