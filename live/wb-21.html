13.7.7Appraising a qualitative study: the linked case

Big idea. Judge a qualitative study by qualitative standards: a fitting question, a purposeful sample, a visible analysis and conclusions that stay close to the data.
Picture it as a food inspector with the right checklist for the kitchen in front of her.

Syllabus: Critically appraise various qualitative studies

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 134 · Qualitative sampling and saturation →Evidence-Based Medicine, page 35 · Appraising a therapy paper →

The story in plain words. Appraisal starts with design. A study of people on community treatment orders using semi-structured interviews and focus groups, analysed by constant comparison, is a qualitative study. Two qualitative methods do not make it mixed methods. Then follow the path in the figure. Does the question suit qualitative methods? Was the sample purposive and justified? Is data collection described, including what did not go to plan? Is rigour shown through reflexivity, triangulation and deviant cases? Are the conclusions tentative? In the linked case, 22 service users were chosen to vary by sex, age, ethnicity and time on the order, and 14 care coordinators took part, though only 2 of 4 focus groups ran. ((? What kind of triangulation does hearing from both service users and coordinators give? ?)) Data source triangulation, because two groups see the same order from different positions.

The people and the evidence. Kuper, Reeves and Levinson (2008) wrote a clinician's guide to reading qualitative papers, asking whether the findings are credible, whether the researcher's position is declared and whether the findings transfer to the reader's own setting. Tong, Sainsbury and Craig (2007) produced COREQ, a 32-item reporting checklist for interviews and focus groups in three domains: research team and reflexivity, study design, and analysis and findings. O'Brien and colleagues (2014) produced SRQR, 21 items covering all qualitative approaches.

From everyday life to the clinic. A food inspector does not fault a sushi bar for having no oven. Criticising a qualitative study for a small sample, non-random selection or no power calculation is the same mistake: those are features of the design, not flaws. The fair criticism is transferability. Findings from one trust and a purposive sample may not travel, and readers judge that by how closely their own service resembles the study's, which is why rich description of the setting matters.

Where the exam tries to catch you. A good qualitative aim says explore, understand or describe. Options that test a hypothesis, estimate a proportion or prove an effect are mismatched. The interviewer's memo about her clinical role is reflexivity; the guardedness she worries about is the possible bias, not the process. The right conclusion is tentative: the order may feel like both a safety net and a leash, which merits study in other settings. Report the study with COREQ, not STROBE, CONSORT or PRISMA.

13.11.1Audit, service evaluation and clinical governance

Big idea. Audit asks whether we are doing what we agreed we should do, service evaluation asks what a service achieves without a standard, and research asks what we should be doing, while clinical governance makes the organisation answerable for all three.
Picture it as a baker weighing the loaves.

Syllabus: Audit: the steps of the audit cycle and the difference between audit and research; service evaluation; implications of clinical governance

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 38 · Clinical audit, research and service evaluation →

The story in plain words. A bakery promises that every loaf weighs 800 g. Once a month the manager pulls a tray, weighs each loaf and asks one question: are we doing what we said we would do? That is clinical audit. The weight checked is the criterion, the share of loaves that must pass is the standard, and the exercise only means something if the manager weighs again after fixing the oven. The audit cycle has six steps. Choose a topic that is high risk, high volume or high cost. Set criteria and standards, for example an ECG within one week of starting an antipsychotic in 95% of patients. Measure current practice. Compare it with the standard. Implement change. Then re-audit against the same standard, which is the step that closes the loop. Two neighbours are easily confused with audit. A service evaluation asks what a service achieves, with no standard to measure against. Research asks what should be done and aims at new knowledge that generalises beyond one site. ((? A team randomises inpatients to two discharge letter formats to see which gets better GP follow-up. Audit or research? ?)) Research, because random allocation always makes it research, however small and local the project.

The people and the evidence. Clinical governance grew out of failure. The concerns about children's heart surgery at the Bristol Royal Infirmary, later examined by Ian Kennedy's public inquiry (2001), showed what happens when outcomes go unmeasured for years. A First Class Service (1998) made NHS organisations accountable for clinical quality, and Scally and Donaldson defined clinical governance as the framework through which they continuously improve quality and safeguard high standards. The usual seven pillars are clinical effectiveness and research, clinical audit, risk management, education and training, patient and public involvement, use of information, and staffing and staff management. The Health Research Authority decision tool then sorts local projects: audit and service evaluation use usual care and normally need no REC review, while research does.

From everyday life to the clinic. A community team checks lithium monitoring against the NICE expectation of a level every 3 months in the first year. It finds 71%, runs a teaching session and adds a reminder system. The audit is not finished. Only when the team measures again, against the same standard, will it know whether the reminders worked. The other half of the job is honesty about the standard. Lowering the target to 70% because the team scored 71% turns audit into self-congratulation. Standards are often set below 100% to allow for justified exceptions, but they are set before the data come in, not after.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is calling every data collection exercise an audit. Ask whether there is a predetermined standard. No standard means service evaluation; random allocation or a novel intervention means research. The second is vocabulary. The criterion says what is measured; the standard says how often it should happen. The third is the pillars: finance, income and budgets belong to corporate governance, never to the clinical pillars.

13.11.2Quality improvement

Big idea. Quality improvement makes known good care happen reliably here, by testing small changes, reading data plotted over time and scaling up only what works.
Picture it as a cook adjusting the seasoning one pinch at a time.

Syllabus: Quality improvement: the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle and the difference between quality improvement and research

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 43 · Quality improvement and PDSA cycle →

The story in plain words. A good cook does not change five things in a recipe at once. She adds a pinch of salt, tastes, adjusts and tastes again. Quality improvement works the same way: small, rapid tests of change, judged against data, each one building on the last. The engine is the Model for Improvement. It asks three questions: what are we trying to accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, and what change can we make? Changes are then tested in PDSA cycles: Plan, Do, Study, Act. The first cycle might involve 1 nurse for 1 week; only when it works does the team widen it to a bay, then the whole ward. QI differs from research in its question. Research asks whether something works; QI asks how to make something already known to work happen reliably here. ((? Practices use established evidence on an SSRI and NSAID interaction to change local prescribing, then re-measure. QI or research? ?)) QI. No new knowledge is generated; known evidence is being put into everyday practice.

The people and the evidence. Walter Shewhart described a cycle of specification, production and inspection in 1939, and W. Edwards Deming popularised it, later preferring Study to Check to stress learning over inspection: hence PDCA and PDSA. When Taylor and colleagues reviewed 73 published PDSA projects in 2014, only a minority had used genuinely small, iterative cycles with regular data. Data over time are read with two tools. On a run chart, Perla and colleagues describe a shift as 6 or more consecutive points on one side of the median and a trend as 5 or more consecutive points all rising or all falling. On an SPC chart, control limits sit 3 SD from the mean: a point outside is special cause variation, and wobble inside is common cause.

From everyday life to the clinic. A ward that keeps missing depot doses has a toolbox. A fishbone diagram lists possible causes by category. A Pareto chart counts and ranks them, because roughly 80% of problems come from 20% of causes. Lean strips out steps that add no value; Six Sigma reduces variation. When something has already gone wrong, root cause analysis looks backwards at the system that allowed it. Before a new process starts, FMEA looks forwards, scoring each possible failure for severity, likelihood and detectability. The finished project is written up with SQUIRE 2.0, not CONSORT.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest slip is confusing a shift with a trend. Seven points above the median that go up and down form a shift, not a trend. The second is direction in time: RCA looks back after harm, FMEA looks ahead before it. The third is tampering. Deming warned that reacting to one high month inside the control limits treats common cause as special cause and tends to make a process worse. Finally, hunting for the nurse who made the error is not QI; Reason's systems approach fixes the conditions, not the person.

13.12Reporting guidelines and national guidelines

Big idea. Reporting checklists make each study design tell its whole story, and national guidelines turn graded evidence into recommendations whose verbs reveal how confident the committee is.
Picture it as a pilot's pre-flight checklist.

Syllabus: Guidelines and reporting: CONSORT, QUOROM, STARD, PRISMA and SRQR; the process for developing national guidelines such as NICE; advantages and limitations of guidelines and protocols

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 124 · Reporting guidelines: CONSORT, STARD, PRISMA →

The story in plain words. A pilot runs the same checklist before every flight, not because she has forgotten how to fly but because one missed item costs too much. Reporting guidelines do this for papers. Each design has its own list, gathered by the EQUATOR Network, so that a reader can find every item needed to judge bias. Match the checklist to the design. CONSORT for randomised trials, with its flow diagram from enrolment to analysis. STROBE for cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies. STARD for diagnostic accuracy. PRISMA for systematic reviews, updated in 2020, whose precursor was QUOROM (1999). MOOSE for meta-analyses of observational studies. SRQR or COREQ for qualitative work, and SQUIRE for QI. Checklists report a study; they do not appraise a guideline. ((? A trust wants to compare the quality of three international guidelines on adult ADHD. Which tool? ?)) AGREE II, with 23 items in six domains, including editorial independence.

The people and the evidence. NICE builds a guideline in a fixed order: a scope (itself consulted on), a committee of professionals and lay members, review questions, systematic evidence and economic reviews, draft recommendations, stakeholder consultation, publication and then surveillance. Since April 2026 the committee judges cost effectiveness against £25,000 to £35,000 per QALY, with costs and benefits discounted at 3.5%. Certainty of evidence is rated with GRADE, set out by Guyatt and colleagues in 2008. Randomised trials start high and lose levels for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision or publication bias. Observational studies start low and can gain levels for a large effect, a dose-response gradient, or confounding that would have shrunk the effect. The older SIGN system ran from 1++ to 4, with grades A to D.

From everyday life to the clinic. NICE tells you how confident it is through its verbs. 'Must' marks a legal duty. 'Offer' marks a strong recommendation that most people would benefit from. 'Consider' marks a closer balance, where the conversation with the patient matters more. Read 'consider offering arts therapies' as an invitation to discuss, not as an absence of evidence. Guidelines raise standards and reduce unwarranted variation, but Woolf and colleagues warned that they lag behind evidence, can be bent by conflicts of interest and can be used as a cookbook. Hughes and colleagues showed the sharpest problem: written for one disease at a time, they fit poorly with multimorbidity and can add up to polypharmacy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. STROBE and STARD are the classic swap. A cross-sectional study of a screening tool against a reference standard is diagnostic accuracy, so STARD. A meta-analysis of cohort and case-control studies is MOOSE, not QUOROM. In GRADE, observational evidence starts at low, not very low, and can be upgraded. And the NICE step after drafting is consultation, which candidates forget because it feels administrative.

13.13Network analysis

Big idea. Network analysis treats a disorder as symptoms that switch each other on, so the questions become which symptoms connect, how strongly, and which ones sit on the routes between clusters.
Picture it as a railway map with one junction station.

Syllabus: Network analysis: nodes, edges, network graphs, degree, centrality, clusters and communities, paths, density, diameter, motifs, network types, assortativity, connectivity and resilience

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 145 · Multiple, multilevel and multivariate regression →Evidence-Based Medicine, page 74 · Network meta-analysis →

The story in plain words. Picture a railway map. Stations are nodes and the tracks between them are edges. In psychopathology networks each node is a symptom and each edge is an association between two symptoms. A disorder is not a hidden cause behind the symptoms; it is the pattern of symptoms keeping each other going, as when poor sleep feeds tiredness and tiredness feeds sad mood. Some numbers describe one station. Degree counts its tracks; strength adds up their weights. Betweenness counts how often a station lies on the shortest route between two others; closeness reflects how near it is to everyone else. Other numbers describe the whole map: density is edges present over edges possible, and diameter is the longest of all the shortest routes. In the figure, poor sleep has degree 4, only one more than sad mood, yet its betweenness is 9 against 2, because every shortest route between the low-mood cluster and the worry cluster runs through it. ((? Which station would cut the most routes if it closed? ?)) Poor sleep, the bridge, which is why bridge symptoms are candidate treatment targets.

The people and the evidence. Cramer and colleagues proposed in 2010 that comorbidity arises through bridge symptoms shared between disorders, such as sleep problems linking depression and anxiety, and Jones, Ma and McNally later formalised bridge centrality. Most psychological networks are Gaussian graphical models, whose edges are partial correlations shrunk by the graphical LASSO; binary data use the Ising model. Van Borkulo and colleagues found in 2015 that people whose depression persisted had more densely connected baseline networks than those who remitted, which fits simulations by Cramer and colleagues (2016) in which strongly connected networks stay stuck in a depressed state. Epskamp, Borsboom and Fried set the accuracy rules: bootstrap the edges and want a correlation stability coefficient of at least 0.25, preferably 0.5.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of a school where the popular children mostly befriend each other: that is assortativity, like linking to like. A triangle of three friends who all know each other is a motif, a small pattern that recurs more often than chance. Tightly knit groups found by algorithms such as walktrap are communities. The data shape the map. One questionnaire from 600 people gives an undirected, between-person network: a snapshot of association, not direction. Daily diaries from the same people allow a directed temporal network, with arrows showing which symptom predicts another the next day.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The favourite trap is choosing the node with the most edges when the question asks about betweenness. Count routes, not tracks. The second is reading an undirected cross-sectional edge as a causal arrow. For density, divide edges present by n(n minus 1) / 2, not by n squared or by the number of nodes: 10 edges among 7 nodes is 10/21 = 0.48. For diameter, find every shortest route and take the longest (4 here, loss of interest to on edge), not the longest wandering path.

13.14Research ethics and governance

Big idea. Research ethics protects participants before a study begins, governance makes someone accountable while it runs, and integrity rules keep the data honest once it ends.
Picture it as a referee who checks the pitch before kick-off.

Syllabus: Research ethics: good research practice, the research governance framework, ethics approval for studies, declarations of interest and funding, and research fraud

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 61 · Good research practice and ethics approval →

The story in plain words. A referee checks the pitch, the boots and the ball before kick-off, then watches the whole game. A research ethics committee is that independent referee for participants: lay and expert members ask whether risks are justified, consent is valid, confidentiality is protected and vulnerable people are safeguarded. Badly designed research is unethical too, so the science is checked as well. Around the committee sits governance. The UK Policy Framework for Health and Social Care Research (2017) sets out who is responsible for what; in England, HRA approval combines the REC opinion with checks of legal and governance compliance. Trials of medicines also need MHRA authorisation and staff trained in Good Clinical Practice, the international ICH standard revised as E6(R3) in 2025. Adults who lack capacity can take part, but the route depends on the study. ((? A non-drug study of a memory aid includes care home residents who lack capacity. Who is asked? ?)) A personal consultee under the Mental Capacity Act, who advises on the person's wishes and feelings. In a drug trial the Clinical Trials Regulations instead require a legal representative, who consents.

The people and the evidence. The Declaration of Helsinki, first adopted in 1964 and last revised by the World Medical Association in 2024, places the interests of participants above those of science and society. Its demands for informed consent and independent review are what UK committees apply every week. Integrity failures are summed up as FFP: fabrication, falsification and plagiarism. The Lancet fully retracted Andrew Wakefield's 1998 paper on MMR vaccine in 2010, the year he was struck off after findings that included dishonesty. The Concordat to Support Research Integrity commits UK institutions to investigate such cases, and ICMJE asks authors to declare relevant interests from the previous 36 months.

From everyday life to the clinic. A trainee short of recruits who writes questionnaire answers for 30 people never seen has committed fabrication: the data come from fresh air. A researcher who quietly drops 3 awkward outliers so that p falls below 0.05 has committed falsification: the data are real but fiddled. Declarations work like a referee's register of interests. Speaker fees from the maker of a depot antipsychotic two years ago are declared at submission. Declaring does not imply wrongdoing; it lets readers weigh the possibility of bias for themselves.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest swap is fabrication with falsification. Invented means fabrication; altered, omitted or selectively reported means falsification. The second is assuming relatives can consent to non-drug research for an adult lacking capacity: consultees advise, they do not consent. The third is applying the Mental Capacity Act to a drug trial. Sections 30 to 34 exclude clinical trials of medicinal products, which use the Clinical Trials Regulations 2004 and a legal representative. Finally, GCP does not replace ethics review; it requires it.

13.15Artificial intelligence in research

Big idea. AI can speed research, but it inherits the biases of its data, invents plausible falsehoods and cannot be held accountable, so people disclose it, check it and keep patient data out of it.
Picture it as a brilliant intern who never says 'I don't know'.

Syllabus: Artificial intelligence in research

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 73 · Artificial intelligence in research →

The story in plain words. Imagine a brilliant intern who works through the night, writes beautifully and never once says 'I don't know'. That is a large language model. It predicts plausible text rather than looking up facts, so it can produce hallucinations: confident references, statistics and quotes that do not exist. Every output needs checking against the original source. Three risks recur. Algorithmic bias: a model learns the gaps in its training data. Hallucination: fluent is not the same as true. Data protection: identifiable notes pasted into a public tool leave the approved, secure system and may be stored or reused, with no lawful basis under UK GDPR. Responsibility stays with people. ((? Can a chatbot that drafted half the manuscript be listed as an author? ?)) No. COPE and ICMJE agree that authorship requires accountability, which a tool cannot hold, so its use is disclosed in the methods or acknowledgements instead.

The people and the evidence. Obermeyer and colleagues (2019) studied a commercial algorithm used to target extra care in US health systems. It used past healthcare costs as a proxy for need; because less had been spent on Black patients at the same level of illness, they were scored as healthier than they were. Fixing the proxy would have raised the share of Black patients flagged for extra help from 17.7% to 46.5%. Reporting has caught up. TRIPOD+AI (2024) covers development and validation of prediction models built with regression or machine learning. SPIRIT-AI and CONSORT-AI (2020) extend trial protocols and reports when the intervention contains an AI component, asking for the algorithm version and how people used its output.

From everyday life to the clinic. A relapse model trained mostly on one ethnic group is like a shoe last made from one foot: it fits that foot well and pinches everyone else. External validation in each subgroup, and open reporting of where the training data came from, show whether it pinches. A researcher who wants to code 200 sets of identifiable clinical notes with a public chatbot should stop. Patient data need a lawful basis, approvals and a secure environment. Anonymise first or use an approved tool inside the organisation's systems, and say in the paper how AI was used.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is choosing CONSORT-AI for a model development study. Developing or validating a prediction model is TRIPOD+AI; a trial of the tool is CONSORT-AI; its protocol is SPIRIT-AI. The second is assuming algorithms are neutral, and the third is trusting a fluent reference list. Two invented citations out of five is not a mostly good list; submitting them unchecked could amount to misconduct.
