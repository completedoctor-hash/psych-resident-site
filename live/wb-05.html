13.2.2.3Case reports, case series and cross-sectional studies

Big idea. A case report or series counts only the people who caught someone's eye, while a cross-sectional study counts everyone in a defined sample once, which buys a denominator and prevalence but never the order of events.
Picture it as a photograph of a crowd, not a film.

Syllabus: Case reports, case series and cross-sectional studies including surveys: prevalence and critical appraisal

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 61 · Case reports and cross-sectional studies →

The story in plain words. A liaison psychiatrist notices that three patients have had seizures after stopping the same hypnotic, and she writes to a journal within the month. That letter is a case series; had she seen one patient it would be a case report. Both are medicine's fastest early warning, and both are only a numerator: 3 people, with no idea how many took the drug and stayed well. Without a comparison group or a denominator, a case report cannot say how often something happens or show that the drug caused it. It raises a hypothesis that a stronger design must test. That is why it sits at the foot of the observational ladder, and why it still matters. A cross-sectional study adds the missing denominator. It contacts a defined sample once and counts who has the condition at that time, which gives prevalence: point prevalence for a single date, period prevalence for a stated window. ((? A survey asks adults about lifetime diagnoses at one interview. Is it still cross-sectional? ?)) Yes. The questions look back, but the data come from one contact, so there is no follow-up and no incidence.

The people and the evidence. In 1961 William McBride, an Australian obstetrician, wrote a short letter to the Lancet describing babies with severe limb malformations born to mothers who had taken thalidomide. A handful of cases changed prescribing across the world. The letter proved nothing on its own; the studies that followed did. The Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey shows the cross-sectional design at full scale. The 2014 round interviewed a random household sample of about 7,500 adults in England, once each, and found that roughly one in six met criteria for a common mental disorder in the past week. That figure is prevalence, and it is used to plan services.

From everyday life to the clinic. A cross-sectional study is a photograph of a crowd, not a film. You can see who is wet and who holds an umbrella, but not who got wet first. A student survey that finds lonely students three times as likely to screen positive for depression cannot say whether loneliness came before the low mood or followed it. Diagnostic accuracy studies use the same frame. A new screen and the reference standard are applied in the same week, before the true state can change, and sensitivity and specificity come from the 2 x 2 table. STARD sets out how to report them; STROBE does the same for cross-sectional, case-control and cohort studies.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is calling a survey a cohort because it asked about the past. Count the contacts: one contact means cross-sectional. The second is claiming causation from a snapshot, which fails temporality, the one Bradford Hill consideration that cannot be waived. When appraising a survey, look at the sampling frame, the response rate and the instrument. If 312 of 1,200 trainees reply, the response is 26% and non-response bias could push the estimate either way. Case reports and series have no denominator, so they never give incidence or prevalence.

13.2.2.4Ecological studies and the ecological fallacy

Big idea. An ecological study compares groups, so it can show that areas with more exposure have more of the outcome but can never show that the exposed people are the ones who became ill.
Picture it as a school league table that describes the building, not the children.

Syllabus: Ecological and correlational studies and their critical appraisal

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 56 · Case reports and cross-sectional studies →

The story in plain words. An ecological study uses groups, not people, as its unit. Each point on its graph is a trust, a region or a year, with exposure and outcome known only as averages or rates. Routine data make it cheap and quick, and it is the natural design when the exposure itself acts on a whole population, such as a law, a price or a water supply. Its weakness is built in. Knowing that areas with more exposure have more of the outcome does not tell you that the exposed people are the ones who became ill. Drawing that individual conclusion from group data is the ecological fallacy. In the figure, eight trusts line up almost perfectly, with r = 0.98 between psychiatrists per head and suicide rate, and deprivation drives both. ((? Does the line show that contact with psychiatrists raises an individual's risk of suicide? ?)) No. It cannot even show that the people who died were ever seen; only individual-level data can answer that.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Robinson (1950) made the point with United States census data. Across states, the proportion born abroad correlated at about -0.53 with illiteracy, as if migrants were more literate; for individuals the correlation was about +0.12, the opposite sign. Migrants had simply settled in states with good schooling. Psychiatry has its own classic. Faris and Dunham (1939) mapped Chicago admissions and found schizophrenia concentrated in the poorest central districts, a pattern still argued over as social causation or social drift. More recently, Scotland's minimum unit price for alcohol was followed by a fall of about 13% in alcohol-attributable deaths (Wyper and colleagues, 2023): a group-level exposure studied with group-level data.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of a school league table. A school with high average grades does not mean every pupil there is clever, and the best pupil in the county may sit in the weakest school. Averages describe the building, not the children. Time-trend studies are ecological too, and they need denominators. Counting first admissions in an asylum register in the 1890s and in a service in the 1990s compares numerators only. Population size, diagnostic criteria and admission thresholds have all changed, so any claim about falling incidence is unsafe.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The exam will hand you a striking area-level correlation and an individual claim. Name the error as the ecological fallacy, and remember the remedy: confirm with cohort or case-control data on individuals. The reverse mistake, applying an individual finding to a whole group, is the atomistic fallacy. Ecological designs are not useless. They suit population-level exposures and generate hypotheses cheaply. When asked which question fits the design best, choose the one where the exposure is a policy or environment that applies to everyone in an area.

13.2.3.1Case-control studies

Big idea. A case-control study starts with the outcome and looks back for the exposure, which makes it fast and ideal for rare diseases but means it can only ever give an odds ratio.
Picture it as choosing a fair comparison crowd from the same town.

Syllabus: Case control study: sampling, strengths and limitations, biases, odds ratio, nested designs and critical appraisal

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 63 · Case-control studies →

The story in plain words. A case-control study runs research in reverse. It starts with people who already have the outcome, the cases, and people from the same source population who do not, the controls, then looks back to compare how often each group was exposed. Because the cases already exist, the design is quick and cheap, and it is the best choice for rare outcomes and diseases with long latency. The investigators decide how many controls to take, often up to 4 per case, so the design cannot give incidence or a relative risk. It gives the odds ratio. In the figure, the odds of exposure are 48/72 in cases and 40/200 in controls, so the odds ratio is 3.33. The odds ratio of exposure equals the odds ratio of disease, which is why the calculation is valid. ((? When does the odds ratio approximate the relative risk? ?)) When the outcome is rare, under about 10%, as first-episode psychosis is in the general population.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Doll and Hill's 1950 study of lung cancer is the model. They interviewed hospital patients with lung cancer and comparison patients with other illnesses about smoking, and the gap in heavy smoking between the groups was too large to dismiss. Their later cohort of British doctors confirmed it. Di Forti and colleagues (2015) used the same logic in south London, comparing people with first-episode psychosis with controls from the same area. Daily use of high-potency cannabis carried roughly five times the odds of psychosis, a finding that rested on choosing controls from the population that produced the cases.

From everyday life to the clinic. Choosing controls is like choosing a fair comparison crowd: they should come from the same town that would have sent them to the clinic had they become ill. Population controls do this best. Hospital controls are convenient but bring Berkson's bias, and siblings or friends share so much diet, income and lifestyle that they cause overmatching and pull the odds ratio towards 1. A nested case-control study borrows a cohort. Stored blood from 18,000 employees sits frozen, and only the 140 who became depressed and 560 colleagues who did not are assayed. Exposure was recorded before the outcome, so recall bias disappears, at a fraction of the cost.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Know the biases that belong here: recall bias, Berkson's bias in hospital samples, and Neyman bias when prevalent rather than incident cases are used. Verification bias belongs to diagnostic accuracy studies and is the usual distractor. Watch for multiple testing. Twenty exposures tested at p < 0.05 will yield about one false positive by chance, so three marginal hits are hypothesis generating only. And never accept a relative risk or an incidence figure from a case-control table.

13.2.3.2Cohort studies

Big idea. A cohort records exposure while people are well and follows them forward, so it is the only observational design that counts new cases directly and gives a true relative risk.
Picture it as a class photograph retaken every year against the first-day roll.

Syllabus: Cohort study: types, sampling, strengths and limitations, biases, relative risk and critical appraisal

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 69 · Cohort studies →

The story in plain words. A cohort study moves in the same direction as cause. It selects people by exposure, records that exposure while they are well, and follows them forward to count new cases. That order gives it temporality and lets it measure incidence directly. In the figure, 60 of 400 adolescents with childhood adversity develop depression within five years, against 40 of 800 without. The risks are 15% and 5%, so the relative risk is 3.0 and the risk difference is 10 per 100. Cohorts come in several shapes. Prospective cohorts follow people from now; retrospective (historical) cohorts define exposure from old records and trace outcomes to the present; ambidirectional cohorts do both. ((? Does a retrospective cohort need a smaller sample than a prospective one? ?)) No. The logic and the numbers are the same; only the calendar has moved.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Andréasson and colleagues (1987) linked the records of about 45,000 Swedish military conscripts, who had reported their cannabis use at around 18, to later psychiatric admissions. Those who had used cannabis more than 50 times had about six times the risk of schizophrenia over 15 years. Exposure was recorded long before illness, so recall could not distort it. The Dunedin study is the classic birth cohort: about 1,000 children born in one New Zealand city in 1972 to 1973, followed for more than forty years. A fixed membership like this is a closed cohort; a catchment population that people join and leave is a dynamic one, analysed per person-time.

From everyday life to the clinic. A cohort is a class photograph taken on the first day of school and retaken every year. You know who was there at the start, so every later change can be counted against the original roll. Lose too many pupils and the later photographs mislead. That is the cost. Cohorts are slow and expensive, poor for rare outcomes, and vulnerable to attrition bias: if 35% are lost, more often from the high-adversity group, the relative risk can move in either direction. Kaplan-Meier and Cox analyses need follow-up data of this kind, from a cohort or a trial, because they track time to the event.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Do not call a historical cohort a case-control study because it uses old records. Ask which way the reasoning runs: exposure groups first, then outcomes, is a cohort, however old the files. Graded exposure, such as none, low and high combat, also lets a cohort show dose-response. Know your null values. The relative risk has a null of 1 and can never be negative; the risk difference has a null of 0 and can be. The odds ratio from the same table is 3.35, a little larger than the relative risk, because the outcome is not rare in the exposed.

13.2.3.3Self-controlled case series

Big idea. The self-controlled case series uses only people who had the event and compares each with themselves, so every fixed confounder cancels out and only time-varying ones remain.
Picture it as checking your own nights after an evening coffee against your other nights.

Syllabus: Self-controlled case series (SCCS): methodology, analysis, strengths and critical appraisal

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 75 · Self-controlled case series →

The story in plain words. A self-controlled case series includes only people who had the event, and compares each person with themselves. Their observation time is split into a risk window after an exposure starts, such as the 30 days after a first antipsychotic prescription, and baseline time, everything else they were observed. If the event happens more often in risk windows than in baseline time, the exposure is linked to it. The output is an incidence rate ratio, estimated by conditional Poisson regression. In the figure, 53 strokes fall in 172.5 person-years of risk window and 2,047 in 16,627.5 person-years of baseline, giving an IRR of 2.5. Because each person is their own control, anything that does not change over the observation period cancels out, measured or not. ((? Which confounder does the SCCS not remove automatically? ?)) Anything that varies over time, above all age, which is added to the model in bands.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Farrington developed the method in the 1990s to study vaccine safety, and used it to show that one mumps vaccine strain was linked to aseptic meningitis. Only the children with the event were needed, which made the work cheap and fast with routine hospital data. Douglas and Smeeth (2008) applied it to antipsychotics and stroke using UK primary care records of nearly 7,000 people. The stroke rate was about 1.7 times higher during antipsychotic exposure, and about 3.5 times higher in people with dementia, a result that shaped prescribing in dementia care.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of checking whether coffee keeps you awake by comparing your own nights after an evening cup with your other nights. Your age, genes and bedroom stay the same, so they cannot explain the difference, and a friend who sleeps badly every night adds nothing to the comparison. That is why lifelong exposures cannot be studied: genetics or childhood deprivation are present throughout, so there is no unexposed time. The exposure must switch on within individuals, as drug starts and vaccinations do. Clozapine and venous thromboembolism is a good fit.

Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Learn the assumptions. The event must not change the chance of later exposure: if prescribers avoid benzodiazepines after a hip fracture, post-event time looks falsely unexposed. Recurrent events must be independent, or only the first is used for a rare event. The observation period must not depend on the event. A fatal outcome such as suicide ends observation, so the standard design is biased and an extended method is needed. Remember too that only cases are sampled, yet the IRR still compares rates, not odds.

13.2.1.1Selection bias: who gets in and who stays

Big idea. Selection bias is a fault in who enters or stays in a study, so the people analysed stop resembling the people the answer is meant for, and recruiting more people through the same door never fixes it.
Picture it as a restaurant judging itself from the comment cards left on the tables.

Syllabus: Sources of bias and strategies to overcome them: selection, non-response and attrition biases

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 45 · Bias: selection, information →

The story in plain words. A restaurant that judges itself from the comment cards left on its tables will always hear good news, because the diners who hated the meal walked out without writing anything. That is selection bias: a systematic difference between the people who end up in the analysis and the people the result is meant to describe. An inpatient unit posts a satisfaction questionnaire to 400 discharged patients. 260 reply and 85% of them are satisfied, which is 221 people. The 140 who stayed silent are not a random slice. If none of them was satisfied, true satisfaction is about 55%; if all of them were, about 90%; if half, about 73%. The figure shows the gap. Selection bias comes in through two doors. At the front door the wrong people are recruited: gym members to measure depression in a city, or hospital patients to stand for the community. At the back door the right people leave: non-response, attrition and loss to follow-up. ((? A survey doubles its sample but still recruits through the same gyms. Does the bias shrink? ?)) No. Systematic error just becomes more precisely wrong.

The people and the evidence. In 1936 the Literary Digest received about 2.4 million replies to a mailed poll and predicted that Landon would beat Roosevelt. Roosevelt won by a landslide. Squire (1988) showed that who chose to reply mattered as much as who was mailed. Size cannot rescue a crooked sample. Berkson (1946) showed that two conditions which each raise the chance of hospital admission can appear linked in a hospital sample even when they are unrelated in the community, because admission depends on the combination, now called Berkson bias. Neyman (1955) described a parallel problem with existing cases: a clinic sees survivors and slow recoverers, not those who died early or got better quickly, which is Neyman bias, or prevalence-incidence bias. Sackett (1979) catalogued many more.

From everyday life to the clinic. Ask who was invited, who agreed and who was analysed. A drug-use survey of junior doctors with a 31% response cannot be fully rescued by statistics such as weighting, but it can be honest: compare responders with everyone invited on age, grade and specialty. A Cochrane review by Edwards and colleagues shows response rises with shorter questionnaires, reminders and incentives. In cohorts the back door matters most. A 20-year follow-up of 500 women treated for anorexia nervosa that traces only 400 will undercount deaths, because the untraced are more likely to have died. In trials, a completers-only analysis of smoking cessation counts those who stayed, often those who succeeded; intention to treat counts drop-outs as still smoking.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is blaming power or sample size when the problem is who was sampled. The second is assuming non-responders would have answered like responders; in satisfaction surveys non-response usually flatters the result. Name the variant. Hospital sample with two conditions that both lead to admission: Berkson. Existing cases from a specialist clinic: Neyman, avoided by recruiting incident cases. Ward controls admitted because of the exposure: association pulled towards 1. Untraced members of a mortality cohort: attrition, with deaths undercounted.

13.2.3.1.3Information bias: measuring the wrong way

Big idea. Information bias is error in how exposure or outcome is measured; when the error is the same in every group it usually blurs the effect towards the null, and when it differs between groups it can push the answer either way.
Picture it as a bathroom scale that reads light for some people and not for others.

Syllabus: Biases in case-control and cohort studies: information, recall, observer and participant biases

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 44 · Bias: selection, information →

The story in plain words. Weigh a class on a scale that reads 2 kg light for everyone and the gap between two people survives; use one that reads light for only one group and every comparison is spoiled. That is non-differential misclassification against differential misclassification, the two shapes of information bias. With a yes-or-no exposure, even equal error mixes the groups and makes them look alike. Take an illustrative case-control study of 200 cases and 200 controls, with 80 and 40 truly exposed. The true odds ratio is 2.67. If 15% of exposed people in both groups are wrongly recorded as unexposed, it falls to 2.52: the groups have been made more alike, so the estimate moves towards 1. If only the controls under-report, as happens when cases search their memories harder, it rises to 3.25. Its many names share one question: was the measurement made the same way in every group? Recall bias is cases remembering more. Interviewer bias is the interviewer probing cases harder. Social desirability is people under-reporting alcohol or drug use face to face. Surveillance or detection bias is one group being tested more often. ((? Equal error in both groups: does that mean no bias? ?)) No. It usually biases towards the null and can hide a real effect.

The people and the evidence. Copeland and colleagues (1977) showed through the arithmetic why equal error dilutes a relative risk. There are exceptions with more than two exposure levels, but towards the null is the safe default; differential error has none. Some biases come from being studied. At the Hawthorne works in the 1920s, output seemed to rise with almost any change because workers knew they were watched; the 2014 review by McCambridge and colleagues found the Hawthorne effect real but smaller and more varied than legend says. Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) told teachers that randomly chosen pupils were about to bloom, and those pupils gained more: the Pygmalion effect. Thorndike (1920) found that one good trait spilled into ratings of every other: the halo effect.

From everyday life to the clinic. In the clinic, recall bias is the mother of a child with autism going back over every tablet she took in pregnancy while a control mother remembers vaguely. Prescribing records made before the outcome remove it. Depot patients seen fortnightly with regular bloods will have more diabetes found than those on oral treatment seen every few months, so equalise the testing schedule before blaming the drug. In trials, performance bias is unequal care between arms, reduced by blinding the clinicians, and detection bias is unequal assessment of outcomes, reduced by blinding the assessors. Schulz and Grimes (2002) set out who can be blinded in a trial, and why.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The traps are mostly names. Hawthorne is being watched; Pygmalion is being expected of; halo is one trait colouring every rating. Recall bias belongs to the participant; interviewer bias belongs to the researcher. The second trap is direction. Non-differential error usually pulls towards 1; differential error can go either way. And no measurement bias, however small, is cured by a larger sample.

13.2.1.2Confounding: the third factor and how design controls it

Big idea. A confounder is a third factor linked to both exposure and outcome but not caused by the exposure; it can create, hide or distort an association, and the strongest defences are built in before the first participant is recruited.
Picture it as ice-cream sales and drownings, both following the summer sun.

Syllabus: Confounding and strategies to reduce risk of confounding

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 50 · Confounding and DAGs →

The story in plain words. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise and fall together, yet nobody bans cornets. The summer sun drives both. That is confounding: a third factor tied to the exposure and, independently, to the outcome, which makes the exposure look guilty or innocent when it is neither. A confounder meets three conditions: it is associated with the exposure, it is a risk factor for the outcome in its own right, and it is not on the causal pathway. A cohort finding that heavy midlife smoking doubles dementia risk without recording alcohol is exposed to confounding, because smokers drink more and alcohol harms the brain on its own. The figure contrasts that shape with a mediator, which sits on the pathway itself. Childhood trauma raises adult depression partly through insomnia: trauma disturbs sleep and poor sleep feeds depression. Insomnia is a mediator, and adjusting for it removes part of the real effect, which is overadjustment. ((? If the trauma effect vanishes entirely once insomnia is in the model, what is that called? ?)) Complete mediation. If it shrinks but persists, partial mediation.

The people and the evidence. For years observational studies suggested that hormone replacement therapy protected women from heart disease. The Women's Health Initiative trial (2002) randomised more than 16,000 women and found no protection, with a small rise in coronary events. Women who had chosen HRT were healthier, wealthier and more active: confounders that no questionnaire fully captured. That is why randomisation is the gold standard. It removes confounders from nobody; it spreads known and unknown confounders evenly between arms on average, as Altman and Bland (1999) explain, and a confounder can only distort a comparison when it is unevenly spread. Baron and Kenny (1986) gave the classic account of mediation, and of moderators, which are a different idea again.

From everyday life to the clinic. At the design stage there are four tools. Restriction admits only one level of the confounder, such as adults who use neither cannabis nor nicotine, at the cost of generalisability. Matching pairs each case with a control alike on chosen factors and suits small case-control studies of around 30 cases. Minimisation balances chosen factors in small trials. Randomisation covers everything, measured or not. Matching has costs. A matched factor can no longer be studied as a risk factor, the analysis must respect the pairs, and matching on something tied to the exposure that does not itself cause the outcome, overmatching, hides a real effect.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is calling every risk factor a confounder. Ask whether the exposure causes it: if it does, it is a mediator, and adjusting for it is wrong when the question is the total effect. The second trap is muddling stages and targets. Regression and stratification can only happen after data collection. Blinding controls bias, not confounding, so it is the odd one out in any list of confounding controls.

13.2.3.1.4Stratify, pool or split: confounding versus effect modification

Big idea. Stratification holds a third factor constant so you can compare crude with adjusted to detect confounding, and stratum with stratum to detect effect modification, which is a real finding to report rather than a bias to remove.
Picture it as sorting the laundry into piles before judging the stain remover.

Syllabus: Controlling confounding at analysis: stratification, Mantel-Haenszel, regression and effect modification

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 50 · Confounding and DAGs →

The story in plain words. Before judging a stain remover, sort the wash into whites and colours. If it works equally in both piles but the overall figure looked better, the piles were unevenly mixed: that is confounding. If it works brilliantly on whites and barely on colours, the effect genuinely depends on the pile: that is effect modification. Stratification is the sorting, and it is the one method that shows both. In an illustrative cohort, 156 of 1,300 benzodiazepine users and 55 of 1,100 non-users break a hip: a crude relative risk of 2.4. Within age bands it is 1.2 and 1.1, and the Mantel-Haenszel age-adjusted estimate is 1.15. The strata agree with each other but not with the crude figure, so age confounds: older people take more benzodiazepines and break more hips. Now stopping lithium and relapse of mania: crude 2.8, adjusted 2.7, but strata of 5.2, 2.6 and 1.3 from youngest to oldest. Crude and adjusted barely differ, so there is little confounding; the strata differ fourfold, so age modifies the effect. ((? Should the authors headline the adjusted 2.7? ?)) No. It describes no real age group, so report each stratum.

The people and the evidence. Mantel and Haenszel (1959) gave a weighted average of stratum-specific odds ratios that adjusts for the stratifying variable; a separate test of homogeneity, such as the Breslow-Day test, checks whether the strata really differ. Rothman, Greenland and Lash set out the modern reading: compare crude with adjusted for confounding, and strata with each other for modification. When several confounders must be handled together, strata thin out quickly and multivariable regression takes over. Least squares is how a linear model is fitted, not a method of adjustment in itself; adjustment comes from putting the confounders into the model. Only measured confounders can be adjusted for, so residual confounding always remains.

From everyday life to the clinic. A case-control study finds an odds ratio of 1.4 for antipsychotics and type 2 diabetes in people without a family history of diabetes, and 3.8 in those with one. That is not a nuisance to be averaged away. It tells a prescriber whose metabolic monitoring needs to be closest. Effect modification is clinical information; confounding is distortion. The first is reported stratum by stratum, the second is removed by adjustment. A single variable can do both at once, which is why both comparisons are always made.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Learn the two patterns. Crude far from adjusted, strata alike: confounding. Crude near adjusted, strata far apart: effect modification. Then decide what to report. Do not average the strata by hand: (5.2 + 2.6 + 1.3) / 3 is about 3.0 and applies to nobody. Do not add every stratum into one table, which simply rebuilds the crude estimate. And restriction cannot reveal effect modification, because only one stratum is studied.
