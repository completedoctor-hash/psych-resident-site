Survival and risk · Diagram 1

Survival and cumulative incidence curves

What it is. A Kaplan-Meier curve shows the proportion still free of the event over time; the cumulative incidence curve is its mirror image, the proportion who have had the event.

  1. 1Each step down is one or more relapses at that moment.
  2. 2A small vertical tick is a censored patient: followed up without relapse until they left the study.
  3. 3Median relapse-free time: read across from 0.5 to the curve, then down. Placebo about 8.7 months; lithium about 22.1 months.
  4. 4Numbers at risk: who is still being followed. Few at risk on the right makes the tail unreliable.
  5. 5Cumulative incidence = 1 − survival. At 12 months: 60% on placebo versus 29% on lithium have relapsed.
  6. 6The hazard ratio and log-rank test compare the whole curves, not one time point.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What proportion on lithium were relapse-free at 12 months?
About 71% (read up from 12 months to the blue curve). The cumulative incidence is therefore about 29%.
QUESTION 2
What does HR 0.41 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.55) mean?
At any moment, people on lithium relapse at about 41% of the rate on placebo. The interval excludes 1, so the difference is statistically significant.
QUESTION 3
Why should you not trust the far right of the placebo curve?
Only 9 placebo patients remain at risk at 24 months, so each event moves the curve a lot.
QUESTION 4
What test compares the two curves, and what model adjusts for covariates?
The log-rank test compares them; Cox proportional hazards regression adjusts for age, severity and other covariates.
QUESTION 5
Is censoring the same as relapse?
No. A censored patient left follow-up event-free; they count as at risk until they leave, then drop out without a step.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 2

Forest plot

What it is. A forest plot shows the result of each study in a meta-analysis, and the pooled result, on one chart.

  1. 1Line of no effect: OR = 1. A result crossing it is not statistically significant.
  2. 2Square: one study's point estimate. Bigger square = more weight (more precise, usually bigger study).
  3. 3Horizontal line: that study's 95% confidence interval.
  4. 4Diamond: the pooled result. Centre = pooled estimate, width = its 95% CI.
  5. 5Log scale on the x-axis: ratios of 0.5 and 2 sit the same distance from 1.
  6. 6Heterogeneity statistics (I², Q test): how much the studies disagree.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which study carries the most weight, and how can you tell?
Evans 2021 (32%): it has the largest square, because it is the most precise study.
QUESTION 2
Is the pooled result significant?
Yes. The diamond (0.63 to 0.87) does not touch the line of no effect at 1.
QUESTION 3
Which single studies are not significant on their own?
Brown 2016 and Davies 2019: their confidence intervals cross 1.
QUESTION 4
Why is the x-axis on a log scale?
Ratios are symmetrical on a log scale, so OR 0.5 and OR 2 are equally far from 1.
QUESTION 5
What does I² = 18% tell you?
Low heterogeneity: the studies broadly agree, so pooling is reasonable.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 3

Network meta-analysis map

What it is. A network map shows every treatment in a network meta-analysis and which pairs have been compared head to head, so all of them can be ranked together.

  1. 1A circle (node) is one treatment. Bigger circle = more patients randomised to it. Placebo is the hub.
  2. 2A line is direct trial evidence between two treatments. Thicker = more trials: placebo versus paroxetine has 41 trials.
  3. 3A closed loop (here placebo, fluoxetine and paroxetine) has direct and indirect evidence, so consistency can be checked.
  4. 4No line between agomelatine and bupropion: they have never been compared head to head, so their comparison is indirect only.
  5. 5A tiny node with thin lines (trazodone, 900 patients) gives an imprecise estimate and a fragile ranking.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which comparison has the most direct evidence?
Placebo versus paroxetine: the thickest line, 41 trials.
QUESTION 2
How can agomelatine be compared with bupropion when no trial compared them?
Indirectly, through common comparators such as placebo, assuming transitivity (the trials are similar enough in patients and design).
QUESTION 3
What is incoherence (inconsistency)?
Disagreement between direct and indirect estimates within a closed loop. It is tested statistically and undermines the network if present.
QUESTION 4
Why is a ranking (SUCRA) for trazodone unreliable here?
It rests on few patients and thin connections, so its estimates have wide intervals; a high rank may be chance.
QUESTION 5
What does node size usually represent?
The number of patients randomised to that treatment across all trials.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 4

Trial flow diagram (CONSORT)

What it is. A CONSORT flow diagram tracks every participant from screening to analysis, so you can judge selection, attrition and how the analysis was done.

  1. 1Enrolment: 412 assessed for eligibility.
  2. 2Exclusions before randomisation (172) affect who the results apply to (external validity), not internal validity.
  3. 3Randomisation (240): the point after which every loss threatens internal validity.
  4. 4Follow-up: losses and discontinuations in each arm. Unequal or reason-specific dropout suggests attrition bias.
  5. 5Analysis: intention to treat keeps everyone in the arm they were randomised to; per protocol keeps only those who completed as planned.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many were randomised, and how many were lost to follow-up overall?
240 randomised; 15 lost (6 sertraline, 9 placebo).
QUESTION 2
Why is the difference in reasons for discontinuation important?
Placebo patients stopped mostly for lack of effect, drug patients for side effects. Dropout is linked to outcome, so a per-protocol analysis would be biased.
QUESTION 3
Which analysis preserves randomisation?
Intention to treat: all 120 in each arm, analysed as allocated.
QUESTION 4
Do the 172 exclusions bias the comparison between arms?
No, they happened before randomisation. They limit generalisability, not the fairness of the comparison.
QUESTION 5
Per-protocol numbers are 106 and 98. Why is per protocol more optimistic?
It drops non-adherent patients, who tend to do worse, so the treatment looks better than it would in practice.
Diagnosis · Diagram 5

ROC curves

What it is. A receiver operating characteristic curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every possible cut-off of a test, showing the trade-off and the overall accuracy.

  1. 1Top-left corner: sensitivity 100% and specificity 100%, the perfect test.
  2. 2Diagonal: a useless test (AUC 0.5), no better than tossing a coin.
  3. 3Best cut-off by Youden's index (sensitivity + specificity − 1 largest): sensitivity 81%, specificity 81%.
  4. 4Area under the curve (AUC): the probability that a random case scores higher than a random non-case. Test A 0.89.
  5. 5Test A lies above Test B everywhere: it is better at every cut-off.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which test is more accurate and how do you know?
Test A: its curve is higher and nearer the top-left, AUC 0.89 versus 0.72.
QUESTION 2
What does AUC 0.89 mean in words?
Pick one person with the condition and one without: 89% of the time the test scores the person with the condition higher.
QUESTION 3
Moving the cut-off towards the bottom left does what?
Raises specificity and lowers sensitivity: fewer false positives, more missed cases.
QUESTION 4
Which point would you choose for a screening test that must not miss cases?
A point further up and right: higher sensitivity, accepting lower specificity.
QUESTION 5
What is Youden's index at the marked cut-off?
Sensitivity + specificity − 1 = 0.81 + 0.81 − 1 = 0.62.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 6

Funnel plot

What it is. A funnel plot shows each study's effect against its precision. Without bias, the points form a symmetrical upside-down funnel around the pooled effect.

  1. 1Vertical line: the pooled effect (OR 0.76).
  2. 2Dashed lines: the pseudo 95% limits. Small studies scatter widely; large studies sit near the top.
  3. 3The largest, most precise studies are at the top, close to the pooled line.
  4. 4The empty bottom-right corner: small studies showing no benefit are missing.
  5. 5Vertical line at 0: the line of no effect (log OR 0 = OR 1).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Is this funnel symmetrical?
No. Small studies cluster on the left (benefit) and the bottom-right is empty.
QUESTION 2
What is the most likely explanation?
Publication bias: small negative or null studies were not published. Other causes are small-study effects, poor quality and true heterogeneity.
QUESTION 3
What does Egger's test p = 0.016 tell you?
Significant asymmetry. The pooled OR of 0.76 probably overstates the benefit.
QUESTION 4
Why is the y-axis standard error, inverted?
So the most precise studies sit at the top and the funnel opens downwards as precision falls.
QUESTION 5
How many studies are needed before a funnel plot is meaningful?
About ten or more; with fewer, asymmetry can easily arise by chance.
Describing data · Diagram 7

Scatter and dot plots

What it is. A scatter plot shows the relationship between two continuous variables; a dot (strip) plot shows every individual value in each group, so you see the spread, not just the average.

  1. 1Regression line: predicted prolactin = 225 + 65 × dose (mIU/L per mg).
  2. 2Shaded band: the 95% confidence interval for the mean line, widest at the extremes.
  3. 3A point far from the line is a large residual: the model fits that patient poorly.
  4. 4Thick bar: the group median. Each dot is one person.
  5. 5The spread overlaps heavily between groups, even though the medians differ.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does r = 0.81 mean?
A strong positive linear association between dose and prolactin.
QUESTION 2
What proportion of the variation in prolactin is explained by dose?
R² = 0.66, so about 66%.
QUESTION 3
Predict prolactin at 10 mg.
225 + 65 × 10 = 875 mIU/L.
QUESTION 4
Does this prove dose causes the rise?
Not on its own. It is an association; causation needs design (for example dose randomisation) and other Bradford Hill criteria.
QUESTION 5
Why use a dot plot rather than a bar of means?
It shows every value, the skew and the overlap; a bar hides the spread and outliers.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 8

Galbraith (radial) plot

What it is. A Galbraith plot places each study by its precision (x) and its standardised effect (z = effect ÷ SE, y). It is used to spot the studies that cause heterogeneity.

  1. 1Regression line through the origin: its slope is the pooled effect (OR 0.66).
  2. 2Dashed lines at ±2: 95% of studies should fall between them if they share one true effect.
  3. 3A study outside the band (here Cole, Ito, Jones) is a source of heterogeneity.
  4. 4Points near the origin are small, imprecise studies.
  5. 5All lines start at the origin: zero precision carries no information.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which studies contribute most heterogeneity?
Cole, Ito, Jones: they lie outside the ±2 band.
QUESTION 2
How does this relate to I² = 71%?
Substantial heterogeneity; the outliers are the visible reason. Q = 38.1 on 11 df.
QUESTION 3
Where are the large studies on this plot?
Far to the right: high precision.
QUESTION 4
What would you do next?
Check those studies for differences in patients, dose or quality; use a random-effects model and a sensitivity analysis without them.
QUESTION 5
How does a Galbraith plot differ from a forest plot?
It plots precision against standardised effect, making outlying studies stand out; a forest plot shows each estimate and interval.
Describing data · Diagram 9

Bar chart with data table

What it is. A grouped bar chart compares a category-level summary (here, a percentage) between groups; the table underneath gives the exact values.

  1. 1The y-axis starts at zero, so bar heights are proportional to the percentages.
  2. 2Tallest bar: burnout in psychiatry trainees, 51.06%.
  3. 3Each pair compares the two groups for one outcome.
  4. 4The data table gives exact values, so you do not have to read them off the axis.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the difference in depression between the groups?
38.42% − 27.95% = 10.47 percentage points.
QUESTION 2
Express the anxiety difference as a relative risk.
44.17 ÷ 35.60 = 1.24: psychiatry trainees were 1.24 times as likely to screen positive.
QUESTION 3
Why must the y-axis start at zero?
Bar length encodes the value. A truncated axis exaggerates differences.
QUESTION 4
Why is a bar chart right here, not a histogram?
The x-axis holds separate categories. Histograms are for continuous data split into intervals.
QUESTION 5
What does the chart not show?
Uncertainty: no confidence intervals, and no adjustment for age, sex or workload.
Describing data · Diagram 10

Line graph

What it is. A line graph follows a mean over time for each group, with error bars showing the uncertainty at each visit.

  1. 1Both arms start at the same baseline (about 31.2): randomisation balanced them.
  2. 2Error bars here are 95% confidence intervals of the mean.
  3. 3At week 8 the gap is 5.5 MADRS points.
  4. 4Where intervals overlap heavily (weeks 0 to 2) the groups are not clearly different.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the drug-placebo difference at week 8?
21.1 − 15.6 = 5.5 points on the MADRS.
QUESTION 2
Is a 5.5-point difference clinically important?
It is near the 2 to 3 points often taken as minimal, so probably yes; judge alongside response and remission rates.
QUESTION 3
Why join the points with lines?
The x-axis is ordered time, so trends between visits are meaningful.
QUESTION 4
What would SD error bars show instead?
The spread of individual patients: much wider, and not a test of difference between means.
QUESTION 5
Why is placebo improving too?
Placebo response, regression to the mean and natural recovery.
Describing data · Diagram 11

Box plots

What it is. A box plot summarises a distribution in five numbers (minimum, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, maximum) and flags outliers.

  1. 1Line in the box: the median (acute adult 24 days).
  2. 2Top of the box: upper quartile (35 days).
  3. 3Bottom of the box: lower quartile (16 days). Box height = interquartile range.
  4. 4Whisker: the furthest value within 1.5 × IQR of the box.
  5. 5Open circle: an outlier beyond the whisker.
  6. 6Diamond: the mean. Above the median here, so the data are positively skewed.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the interquartile range for acute adult wards?
35 − 16 = 19 days.
QUESTION 2
Which ward has the most variable length of stay?
Rehabilitation: the tallest box and longest whiskers.
QUESTION 3
Why is the mean above the median?
A few very long stays pull the mean up: positive skew.
QUESTION 4
Which summary would you report for these data?
Median and IQR, because they resist skew and outliers.
QUESTION 5
Which test compares length of stay across the four wards?
Kruskal-Wallis, a non-parametric test for three or more independent groups.
Describing data · Diagram 12

Distribution plots

What it is. A distribution plot shows how values are spread: symmetrical (normal) or skewed. Where the mean, median and mode sit tells you which.

  1. 1Normal distribution: mean = median = mode, all at the peak.
  2. 2About 68% of values lie within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD, 99.7% within 3 SD.
  3. 3Positive skew: mode 8 < median 12 < mean 14. The long right tail drags the mean.
  4. 4The long right tail: a few people with very long sick leave.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
In a positively skewed distribution, which average is largest?
The mean; then the median; then the mode.
QUESTION 2
Which average best describes typical sick leave here?
The median (12 days), as it is not pulled by the tail.
QUESTION 3
What percentage of a normal distribution lies more than 2 SD above the mean?
About 2.5% (half of the 5% outside ±2 SD; strictly ±1.96 SD).
QUESTION 4
How might you analyse skewed data with a t test?
Log-transform it, or use a non-parametric test such as Mann-Whitney U.
QUESTION 5
Name a variable that is negatively skewed.
Age at death in a high-income country: most die old, a few young, so the tail is on the left.
Diagnosis · Diagram 13

Fagan nomogram

What it is. A Fagan nomogram turns a pre-test probability and a likelihood ratio into a post-test probability with a straight line, without calculation.

  1. 1Pre-test probability on the left: here 20%.
  2. 2Likelihood ratio in the middle: LR+ 10 for a positive result.
  3. 3Post-test probability on the right: 71% after a positive result.
  4. 4A negative result (LR− 0.1) drops it to 2.4%.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Pre-test 20%, LR+ 10. What is the post-test probability by calculation?
Pre-test odds 0.25 × 10 = 2.5; probability 2.5 ÷ 3.5 = 71%.
QUESTION 2
Why is the middle scale a different length?
Post-test log odds = pre-test log odds + log LR. With equally spaced scales the middle must be halved, so it is shorter.
QUESTION 3
Which LR values change probability a lot?
LR+ above 10 or LR− below 0.1 give large, often decisive shifts; LRs between 0.5 and 2 barely move it.
QUESTION 4
After a negative result the probability is 2.4%. What does that mean clinically?
The condition is now unlikely enough that you may stop testing, depending on the cost of a miss.
QUESTION 5
Where does the pre-test probability come from?
Prevalence in your setting, adjusted by the clinical picture.

Evidence synthesis · Diagram 14

L'Abbé plot

What it is. A L'Abbé plot shows each trial's event rate in the treatment group against the control group.

  1. 1Diagonal line of equality: treatment rate = control rate (no effect).
  2. 2Circle below the line: fewer events with treatment — benefit (when events are bad outcomes).
  3. 3Circle above or on the line: no benefit or harm.
  4. 4Spread of circles across the x-axis shows different baseline (control) risks between trials, a source of heterogeneity.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
A trial sits below the diagonal. What does it mean if events are relapses?
Fewer relapses with treatment — the treatment helped.
QUESTION 2
What does the diagonal represent?
No difference: treatment rate equals control rate.
QUESTION 3
Why does circle size matter?
Bigger circles are bigger trials and carry more weight.
QUESTION 4
What does a wide spread of points along the x-axis suggest?
Different baseline risks between trials — a source of clinical heterogeneity.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 15

PRISMA flow diagram

What it is. The PRISMA diagram shows how studies were found, screened and selected for a systematic review.

  1. 1Identification: databases searched — several databases plus grey literature reduce publication bias.
  2. 2Each box to the right subtracts records; numbers must add up.
  3. 3Reasons for exclusion at full text must be given.
  4. 4Included: number of studies in the review and in the meta-analysis.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many records were screened?
930 (1,240 identified minus 310 duplicates).
QUESTION 2
Why search several databases and grey literature?
To find all relevant studies and reduce publication bias.
QUESTION 3
How many studies entered the meta-analysis?
14 of the 18 included studies.
QUESTION 4
What must be reported at the full-text stage?
The reasons each study was excluded.
Survival and risk · Diagram 16

Calibration plot

What it is. A calibration plot checks whether a risk prediction model's predicted risks match the risks actually observed.

  1. 1Diagonal: perfect calibration (predicted = observed).
  2. 2Points above the line: the model under-predicts risk at higher levels.
  3. 3Calibration (agreement) is different from discrimination (ranking, measured by the C-statistic/AUC).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does perfect calibration look like?
All points on the diagonal: predicted risk equals observed risk.
QUESTION 2
Points lie above the diagonal at high risk. What does it mean?
The model under-predicts risk for high-risk patients.
QUESTION 3
How is calibration different from discrimination?
Calibration is agreement in absolute risk; discrimination is ranking cases above non-cases (C-statistic).
QUESTION 4
Why validate a model in a new population?
Performance usually falls outside the data it was built on (overfitting).
Diagnosis · Diagram 17

2×2 table and likelihood ratios

What it is. The 2×2 table compares a test result with the truth from a reference standard; every diagnostic statistic comes from it.

  1. 1Columns = truth (reference standard). Sensitivity and specificity read DOWN the columns.
  2. 2Rows = test result. Predictive values read ACROSS the rows.
  3. 3PPV and NPV change with prevalence; sensitivity, specificity and LRs do not.
  4. 4LR+ above 10 or LR− below 0.1 change probability a lot.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Calculate sensitivity.
a/(a+c) = 80/100 = 0.80.
QUESTION 2
Calculate LR+.
Sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity) = 0.80 ÷ 0.10 = 8.
QUESTION 3
Which statistics change with prevalence?
PPV and NPV. Sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios do not.
QUESTION 4
Which way do you read sensitivity and specificity?
Down the columns (the truth).
Diagnosis · Diagram 18

Leaf plot (probability-modifying plot)

What it is. A leaf plot shows how a positive or negative test result changes probability across every possible pre-test probability.

  1. 1Upper (green) curve: probability after a positive result.
  2. 2Lower (red) curve: probability after a negative result.
  3. 3Diagonal: no change — a test with LR = 1. The wider the 'leaf', the more useful the test.
  4. 4Read up from your pre-test probability (here 30%) to each curve: positive → 72%, negative → 6%.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Pre-test 30%: what are the post-test probabilities?
About 72% after a positive result and 6% after a negative result.
QUESTION 2
What does a wide leaf mean?
The test changes probability a lot — it is useful.
QUESTION 3
Where is a test least helpful?
At very low or very high pre-test probability, where the curves hug the diagonal at the ends.
QUESTION 4
Which curve uses LR+?
The upper (green) curve.
Diagnosis · Diagram 19

Frequency tree

What it is. A frequency tree splits a round number of people by prevalence, then by test result, to show true and false results.

  1. 1Start with a round number (1,000) — natural frequencies are easier than percentages.
  2. 2First split by disease using the prevalence.
  3. 3Then split the diseased by sensitivity (true positives vs false negatives).
  4. 4Split the healthy by specificity (true negatives vs false positives).
  5. 5Predictive values come from reading across the positive (or negative) boxes: PPV = TP / all positives.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Prevalence 10%, sensitivity 80%, specificity 90%. What is the PPV?
80 ÷ (80 + 90) = 47%.
QUESTION 2
How many false positives are there?
90 (10% of the 900 without depression).
QUESTION 3
Why does a good test have a low PPV here?
Low prevalence: false positives from the large healthy group outnumber true positives.
QUESTION 4
Why use natural frequencies?
They are easier to understand and communicate than conditional percentages.
Diagnosis · Diagram 20

Venn and nested-circle diagrams

What it is. Venn and nested-circle diagrams show the same 2×2 information as overlapping groups.

  1. 1The overlap is the true positives — they have the disease and test positive.
  2. 2Disease circle outside the overlap: missed cases (false negatives).
  3. 3Test circle outside the overlap: false alarms (false positives).
  4. 4Everything outside both circles: true negatives.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the overlap?
True positives: diseased and test positive.
QUESTION 2
What is the PPV from the diagram?
Overlap ÷ whole test-positive circle = 80/170 = 47%.
QUESTION 3
Where are the false negatives?
The part of the disease circle outside the test circle (20).
Communicating risk · Diagram 21

Cates plot (icon array)

What it is. A Cates plot shows 100 people as faces to display risk with and without treatment.

  1. 1Red faces: people who have the bad outcome anyway.
  2. 2Gold faces: people saved by treatment — the absolute risk reduction.
  3. 3Grey faces: well either way. Most people do not benefit, which is why the NNT is above 1.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Calculate the ARR and NNT.
40% − 25% = 15%; NNT = 100/15 = 6.7, rounded up to 7.
QUESTION 2
What do the grey faces show?
People who stay well with or without treatment.
QUESTION 3
Why are icon arrays good for patients?
They show absolute risks in natural frequencies, which people understand better than relative risks.
Describing data · Diagram 22

Stem-and-leaf plot

What it is. A stem-and-leaf plot keeps every value while showing the shape of the data.

  1. 1Stem: the leading digit(s) — here tens of years.
  2. 2Leaves: the final digit, one per person — so every original value is kept.
  3. 3The key tells you how to read a value: always find it first.
  4. 4The longest row shows the mode class; the shape on its side is a histogram.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many patients are there?
22 — count the leaves.
QUESTION 2
What is the mode?
34, which appears three times.
QUESTION 3
What is the median?
The average of the 11th and 12th values: 34.
QUESTION 4
What must you always read first?
The key (here 3 | 4 = 34).
Describing data · Diagram 23

Normal distribution and z-scores

What it is. The normal distribution is a symmetrical bell-shaped curve fixed by its mean and standard deviation.

  1. 1Symmetrical bell: mean = median = mode.
  2. 2About 68% lie within 1 SD of the mean.
  3. 395% lie within 1.96 SD — this is the 95% reference range.
  4. 4Each tail beyond 1.96 SD holds 2.5%.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What proportion lies within ±1.96 SD?
95%.
QUESTION 2
An IQ of 130 (mean 100, SD 15). What is the z-score?
(130 − 100)/15 = +2: about the top 2.5%.
QUESTION 3
What proportion lies beyond 1 SD above the mean?
About 16%.
QUESTION 4
How is a 95% reference range calculated?
Mean ± 1.96 SD.
Describing data · Diagram 24

Error bars: SD, SE and CI

What it is. Error bars show either the spread of individuals (SD) or the precision of a mean (SE or confidence interval).

  1. 1SD bars: how spread out individual patients are.
  2. 2SE bars: how precisely the mean is estimated (SD ÷ √n). Always narrower than SD bars.
  3. 395% CI bars: mean ± 1.96 SE. Non-overlapping 95% CIs mean a significant difference; overlapping ones do not prove no difference.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1

What it is. Bar charts show categories; histograms show continuous data.

What it is. A confidence interval plot shows whether a result could be no effect, by checking where the interval sits relative to the line of no effect.
