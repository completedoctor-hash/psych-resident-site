3.2.21.1Simple linear regression

Big idea. Regression draws the single straight line that comes closest to all the points, and the slope of that line is a prediction you can use on a patient who was never in the study.
Picture it as the line of best fit through a cloud.

Syllabus: The regression line, least squares, interpretation of the coefficient

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 120 · Simple linear regression →

The story in plain words. The line is y equals a plus bx. y is the dependent variable, the outcome. x is the independent variable, the predictor. a is the intercept, the value of y when x is zero. b is the slope, the change in y for each unit change in x. That slope is the regression coefficient, and it is not the correlation coefficient. Candidates conflate them constantly. The line is fitted by least squares, which chooses the line that minimises the squared vertical distances from the points to it. Prediction runs one way only: put in x, read off y. With y equals 1200 plus 0.4x, an x of 1000 predicts 1600. Reversing the line to predict x from y gives a different line altogether.

Simple linear regression: y = a + bx. Least squares chooses the line that makes the sum of the squared vertical gaps (residuals) as small as possible

The people and the evidence. The requirements are narrower than most candidates think. The outcome must be continuous with normally distributed residuals. The predictor does not need to be normal at all. The variables must be correlated for the exercise to mean anything, but causation and time order are not required by the arithmetic. Regression will happily fit a line through a nonsense pairing, which is why the clinical judgement sits with the reader.

From everyday life to the clinic. The output to read in a paper is the coefficient with its 95% confidence interval and p value, plus R squared for how well the line fits. One reading trick is worth having. ((? What does a straight line on a logarithmic scale mean? ?)) Exponential growth on the raw scale, which is how a graph makes an accelerating process look tame.

A taxi meter is a regression line: the intercept is the flag-fall you pay before moving (about 3 pounds), the slope is the charge per mile (about 2.10). Fare = 3 + 2.1 x miles

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is the intercept, which is the value of y when x is zero and is often clinically meaningless, as with a predicted weight at age zero. The second is swapping the coefficient for the correlation. The third is prediction outside the range of the data, where the line keeps going and the biology does not.

3.2.21.2Multiple, logistic and multivariate methods

Big idea. Once you have more than one predictor the model can hold the others still while it looks at one, which is the whole statistical answer to confounding.
Picture it as holding everything else constant.

Syllabus: Multiple linear regression, logistic regression, and related multivariate methods

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 153 · Multiple, multilevel and multivariate regression →

The story in plain words. Multiple linear regression takes one continuous outcome and two or more predictors: y equals a plus b1x1 plus b2x2 and so on. Each coefficient is the effect of its own predictor with the others held constant, and that phrase is how a paper adjusts for confounders. Predictors may be continuous or categorical, the latter entered as dummy variables. Variable selection may be forward, adding predictors, backward, removing them, or stepwise, doing both, and a statistically chosen model is not always a clinically sensible one. A pedantic point the exam does test. Multivariable strictly means many predictors and multivariate means many outcomes. Papers blur the two constantly.

The outcome picks the model. The predictors can be anything.
OutcomeModelWhat it reports
ContinuousLinear regressionCoefficients, R squared
Binary, yes or noLogistic regressionAdjusted odds ratios
Time to eventCox regressionAdjusted hazard ratios
A countPoisson regressionRate ratios

The people and the evidence. Logistic regression handles a binary outcome by modelling the log odds as a linear function of the predictors. There is no assumption that the outcome is normally distributed, because a yes or no cannot be. Its exponentiated coefficients are adjusted odds ratios with confidence intervals, which is why odds ratios dominate the psychiatric literature. Plotted against a single predictor the fitted probability is an S-shaped curve bounded by 0 and 1, and overall model significance is tested by a likelihood ratio chi-square.

From everyday life to the clinic. The outcome type picks the model and the predictors can be anything. Continuous outcome, linear. Binary, logistic. Time to event, Cox. A count, Poisson. Three more appear in psychiatric papers. Path analysis chains regressions into boxes and arrows, separating direct from indirect effects, and tests a model's fit without ever proving causation. Factor analysis reduces many correlated items to a few underlying factors, as in Liddle's three syndromes of schizophrenia. Cluster analysis groups people rather than variables. ((? What is the difference between a mediator and a moderator? ?)) A mediator is the route an exposure acts through; a moderator changes the strength of the effect.

An estate agent prices a house from several things at once. Multiple regression does the same: each arrow is a coefficient, the effect of that feature with the others held constant

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is the outcome type. A stem with a yes or no outcome and linear regression among the options is testing exactly this. The second is the claim that factor analysis groups patients. It groups variables; cluster analysis groups patients. The third is treating an adjusted odds ratio as though adjustment had removed confounding entirely, when it only removes what was measured.

Challenge 1 · 13.7 Correlation and regression · 3.2.21.1
Adjusted odds ratio for bullying from a multiple logistic regression in a birth cohort of 9,000 children
A birth cohort of 9,000 children is followed to age 25. Maternal depression, bullying, low birth weight and parental separation are entered together into a multiple logistic regression predicting whether a participant develops an anxiety disorder. For bullying the model reports the adjusted odds ratio shown in the figure, 1.8 (95% confidence interval 1.3 to 2.5). Which interpretation is correct?
  1. Children who were bullied are 1.8 times as likely to develop an anxiety disorder as those who were not
  2. The odds of anxiety disorder are 1.8 times higher in children who were bullied, after allowing for the other three factors in the model
  3. Bullying accounts for 80% of anxiety disorders in the cohort
  4. The association is not statistically significant because the interval is wide
  5. Bullying raises the absolute risk of anxiety disorder by 1.8 percentage points
Show the working
Answer: B The odds of anxiety disorder are 1.8 times higher in children who were bullied, after allowing for the other three factors in the model

Multiple logistic regression models the log odds of a binary outcome as a sum of predictor effects. Exponentiating a coefficient gives the odds ratio for that predictor, adjusted for every other variable in the model, with its confidence interval. Because the model works on odds, the result is a ratio of odds, which only approximates a ratio of risks when the outcome is uncommon.

Bullying: adjusted OR 1.8 (1.3 to 2.5). The odds of anxiety disorder in bullied children are 1.8 times those in non-bullied children with the same maternal depression, birth weight and parental separation status. The interval excludes 1, so the adjusted association is unlikely to be chance.

The exam's favourite misreading is 'times as likely'. Keep 'odds' in the sentence, keep 'adjusted for' in the sentence, and check the interval against 1 rather than judging it by its width.

Rule: Exponentiated logistic coefficient = adjusted odds ratio: odds, not risk, with the other predictors held constant.
Trap: Reading an odds ratio as a risk ratio ('1.8 times as likely') when the outcome is common.

  1. A confounder
  2. An effect modifier
  3. A mediator
  4. A source of selection bias
  5. A random error
Show the working
Answer: B An effect modifier

A third variable can affect an exposure-outcome relationship in two quite different ways. A confounder is linked to both exposure and outcome and creates or hides an association, so the crude estimate is misleading and the adjusted estimate is the truth. An effect modifier does not distort anything; the exposure genuinely has a different effect in different subgroups, so there is no single truth to adjust towards and the stratum-specific estimates should be reported.

Here the SSRI effect on hyponatraemia is strong in thiazide users (RR 4.5) and weak in non-users (RR 1.4). The effect varies by stratum, which is the signature of effect modification. Biologically it makes sense: both drugs lower sodium, and together they do more than either alone.

The exam tells you the estimate differs between subgroups and asks what the subgrouping variable is. Different-between-strata means effect modifier; different-from-crude means confounder. A variable can be both, but the stem will push you one way.

Rule: Different effect in different strata = effect modifier; crude estimate differs from adjusted estimate = confounder.
Trap: Calling any third variable a confounder; confounding is about distortion, effect modification is about genuine variation.

Daily clozapine dose and trough plasma level in 21 inpatients, with fitted line (plasma level = 67 + 0.74 x dose) and 95% confidence band
The scatter plot shows daily clozapine dose against trough plasma clozapine level in 21 inpatients on a rehabilitation ward. A least squares line, plasma level = 67 + 0.74 x dose, is fitted with its 95% confidence band, and one point is labelled P. What does the slope of 0.74 in the fitted equation mean?

  2. For each 1 mg/day increase in dose, the predicted plasma level rises by 0.74 ng/mL (74 ng/mL per 100 mg)
  3. The correlation between dose and plasma level is 0.74

  5. 74% of patients had a plasma level within the therapeutic range
Show the working
Answer: B For each 1 mg/day increase in dose, the predicted plasma level rises by 0.74 ng/mL (74 ng/mL per 100 mg)

A simple linear regression line is y = a + bx. The slope b is the average change in y for each one-unit increase in x, in the units of y per unit of x. The intercept a is the predicted y when x is zero, which is often outside the range of the data and then has no useful interpretation. The slope describes the relationship; r describes how tightly the points follow it.

The equation is plasma level = 67 + 0.74 x dose. So for each extra mg/day the predicted trough level rises by 0.74 ng/mL, or about 74 ng/mL for each 100 mg/day. A patient moving from 300 to 400 mg/day would be expected to rise from about 289 to about 363 ng/mL, other things being equal.

The exam deliberately makes the slope and r similar numbers, or offers r squared as 'percentage explained'. Keep three things separate: slope (units of y per unit x), r (no units, strength), r squared (proportion of variance explained).

Rule: The slope is the change in y per unit change in x; the intercept is y when x is zero.
Trap: Reading the slope as a correlation or as variance explained.
