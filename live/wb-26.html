Challenge 1 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.3
A guideline group wants to rate its overall confidence in the pooled evidence that clozapine reduces suicidal behaviour. Which approach is designed for this?
  1. RoB 2
  2. GRADE
  3. PRISMA 2020
  4. Egger's test
  5. I-squared
Show the working
Answer: B GRADE

GRADE starts RCT evidence at high and observational evidence at low. It downgrades for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias, and can upgrade for large effects, dose-response or when plausible confounding would reduce the effect.

GRADE rates certainty in a whole body of evidence for one outcome, which is what a guideline group needs. Single-study tools feed into it but do not replace it.

Rule: Certainty of a body of evidence: GRADE.
Trap: Using a single-study bias tool to rate overall certainty.

Source: Guyatt GH, Oxman AD, Vist GE et al. (2008). GRADE: an emerging consensus on rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations. BMJ, 336: 924-926.

Challenge 2 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.1
A study follows all children born in one city in a single week of 2001 throughout life, with no new members added. A second study follows everyone registered with a catchment-area mental health service, with people joining when they move in and leaving when they move out. How are these best described?
  1. Both are fixed cohorts
  2. The first is a fixed (closed) cohort; the second is a dynamic (open) population
  3. Both are dynamic populations
  4. The first is dynamic; the second is fixed
  5. Both are cross-sectional samples
Show the working
Answer: B The first is a fixed (closed) cohort; the second is a dynamic (open) population

Birth cohorts are classic fixed cohorts. Dynamic populations are analysed using incidence rates per person-time, since each person contributes a different length of time at risk.

In a closed cohort everyone is counted from the same start. In an open population people contribute different amounts of time, so rates per person-time are used.

Rule: Fixed (closed) = membership set by one event; dynamic (open) = members come and go, analysed by person-time.
Trap: Assuming all cohorts are closed.

Source: Rothman KJ, Greenland S, Lash TL (2008). Modern Epidemiology, 3rd ed. Philadelphia: Lippincott Williams and Wilkins.

Challenge 3 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.2
Which of the following is NOT itself a method of controlling confounding?
  1. Stratification
  2. Least squares estimation
  3. Restriction
  4. Mantel-Haenszel method
  5. Multiple regression
Show the working
Answer: B Least squares estimation

Least squares minimises the sum of squared residuals to estimate coefficients. It is used within regression but by itself does not adjust for anything; it is the inclusion of confounders as covariates that adjusts.

Adjustment comes from including confounders as covariates or strata. Least squares is the arithmetic engine of linear regression, not an adjustment.

Rule: Fitting method is not an adjustment method.
Trap: Choosing a familiar statistical term as an adjustment method.

Source: Rothman KJ, Greenland S, Lash TL (2008). Modern Epidemiology, 3rd ed. Philadelphia: Lippincott Williams and Wilkins.

Challenge 4 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.1.6
What is the main advantage of a nested case-control study over a conventional hospital-based case-control study?
  1. Exposure was recorded before the outcome, and controls come from the same cohort as the cases
  2. It measures incidence directly in exposed and unexposed groups
  3. It needs no matching of controls to cases
  4. It needs no stored baseline data or samples
  5. It removes all confounding, measured and unmeasured
Show the working
Answer: A Exposure was recorded before the outcome, and controls come from the same cohort as the cases

Because exposure was captured at cohort entry, information bias from recall is removed, and both cases and controls come from the same base population, reducing selection bias. It is cheaper than analysing every cohort member.

Sampling from within a defined cohort also means controls truly come from the same base as the cases, which ordinary hospital designs struggle to guarantee.

Rule: Nested case-control: prospective exposure data plus case-control efficiency.
Trap: Thinking nesting allows direct incidence calculation.

Source: Ernster VL (1994). Nested case-control studies. Prev Med, 23: 587-590.

Challenge 6 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.3
What is the main cause of publication bias?
  1. Journals refusing all small studies
  2. Reviewers searching only one database
  3. Studies being published in languages other than English
  4. Meta-analysts using a fixed-effect model
  5. Investigators not submitting studies with negative or null results
Show the working
Answer: E Investigators not submitting studies with negative or null results

Rosenthal coined the "file drawer problem". Evidence suggests non-submission by investigators, often because results seem uninteresting or unwelcome to sponsors, explains more unpublished studies than editorial rejection. Prospective trial registration helps detect them.

Most unpublished trials stay in the file drawer because investigators or sponsors do not submit them. Editorial rejection plays a smaller part.

Rule: File drawer: authors do not submit null results.
Trap: Blaming editors alone.

Source: Rosenthal R (1979). The file drawer problem and tolerance for null results. Psychological Bulletin, 86: 638-641.

Challenge 7 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.1.3
A team plans a case-control study of risk factors for anorexia nervosa. Why are incident (newly diagnosed) cases preferred over prevalent cases?
  1. Incident cases have better recall of childhood events
  2. Prevalent cases cannot give an odds ratio
  3. Incident cases make matching unnecessary
  4. Prevalent cases over-represent people with long illness, so exposures linked to survival or chronicity get mixed up with causes
  5. Prevalent cases are always hospital-based
Show the working
Answer: D Prevalent cases over-represent people with long illness, so exposures linked to survival or chronicity get mixed up with causes

People who die early or recover quickly are missing from a pool of prevalent cases. Factors associated with chronic illness can then look like causes. Incident cases also have a shorter gap between exposure and diagnosis, which helps exposure recall.

Neyman (prevalence-incidence) bias is the name for this survival effect. Incident cases capture people at the start of the illness, before survival filters them.

Rule: Use incident cases to avoid Neyman (prevalence-incidence) bias.
Trap: Using clinic caseloads as if they were new cases.

Source: Delgado-Rodriguez M, Llorca J (2004). Bias. J Epidemiol Community Health, 58: 635-641.

Challenge 8 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.6
A network meta-analysis reports a SUCRA value of 0.92 for one antipsychotic for a given outcome. What does this mean?
  1. It is 92% effective
  2. It reduces symptoms by 92% compared with placebo
  3. It has a high probability of ranking near the top among the treatments compared
  4. 92% of trials included it
  5. The difference from placebo is significant at p = 0.92
Show the working
Answer: C It has a high probability of ranking near the top among the treatments compared

Rankings can mislead when differences between treatments are small or uncertain. A drug can rank first with only a trivial, imprecise advantage, so SUCRA should be read alongside effect estimates and confidence in the evidence.

A SUCRA of 0.92 places the drug near the top of the ranking. It is not an effect size, so check how large and how certain its advantage actually is.

Rule: SUCRA ranks treatments from 0 to 1; always read with effect sizes and certainty.
Trap: Reading SUCRA as an effect size.

Source: Salanti G (2012). Indirect and mixed-treatment comparison, network, or multiple-treatments meta-analysis: many names, many benefits, many concerns for the next generation evidence synthesis tool. Research Synthesis Methods, 3: 80-97.

Challenge 9 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.3.1.4
In which situation is individually matching every participant on age, sex, smoking status and BMI most suitable?
  1. A cohort study of 20,000 people
  2. A large randomised trial
  3. An ecological study of regions
  4. A small case-control study with about 30 cases and 30 controls
  5. A cross-sectional survey of 5,000 adults
Show the working
Answer: D A small case-control study with about 30 cases and 30 controls

Matching increases efficiency in small case-control studies. It must be accounted for in the analysis (for example conditional logistic regression), and a matched factor can no longer be studied as a risk factor. Matching on too many factors makes controls hard to find.

In a large study, adjustment at analysis is cheaper and more flexible; in a small case-control study, matching keeps each comparison balanced. The analysis must keep the pairs.

Rule: Few cases, many confounders: match.
Trap: Matching on a factor linked to the exposure but not a cause of the outcome (overmatching), which hides a real effect.

Source: Grimes DA, Schulz KF (2002). Case-control studies: research in reverse. Lancet, 359: 431-434.

Challenge 10 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.3
A team wants to estimate the incidence of psychosis among recently arrived refugees over the next decade. Which design is most appropriate?
  1. Prospective cohort study
  2. Cross-sectional survey
  3. Case-control study
  4. Case series
  5. Ecological study
Show the working
Answer: A Prospective cohort study

Incidence rate = new cases / person-time at risk. Only a design that follows people free of the outcome can count new cases with an accurate denominator.

Incidence needs people free of the outcome at the start and a count of new cases over time. That is the definition of a cohort.

Rule: Incidence = new cases over time: cohort.
Trap: Choosing cross-sectional because the population is defined.

Challenge 11 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.1
Without screening, a man is diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease from symptoms at age 78 and dies at 84. With screening, the disease is detected at 75 and he still dies at 84. Which statement is correct?
  1. Survival rises by 3 years, all of it lead time
  2. Survival rises by 3 years, showing screening extends life
  3. Survival rises by 9 years
  4. Survival is unchanged at 6 years
  5. Survival rises by 6 years, half of it lead time
Show the working
Answer: A Survival rises by 3 years, all of it lead time

Survival without screening = 84 minus 78 = 6 years. With screening = 84 minus 75 = 9 years. The 3-year difference is lead time; lifespan is unchanged, so the true benefit is zero.

Lifespan is the test: he dies at 84 either way. All 3 extra years of survival from diagnosis are lead time.

Rule: Survival gain = lead time when death is unchanged.
Trap: Calling the 9-year survival a 50% improvement.

Source: Celentano DD, Szklo M (2019). Gordis Epidemiology, 6th ed. Philadelphia: Elsevier.

Challenge 12 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.3.4
A team plans to use a standard SCCS to study whether starting an antidepressant raises the short-term risk of death by suicide. Why is the standard design unsuitable?
  1. The event ends observation, so the observation period is not independent of the event
  2. Suicide is too common for SCCS
  3. SCCS cannot handle drug exposures
  4. Antidepressant use does not vary over time
  5. SCCS requires a separate control group
Show the working
Answer: A The event ends observation, so the observation period is not independent of the event

Standard SCCS needs observation periods fixed independently of event times. If the event is fatal, or greatly increases death risk, exposure after the event cannot be observed. Extensions exist for event-dependent observation periods, but the standard method would be biased.

Standard SCCS assumes the observation period would have been the same whatever happened. Death breaks that assumption.

Rule: Event that raises mortality (or is death): standard SCCS invalid; use an extended method or another design.
Trap: Ignoring censoring by death.

Source: Whitaker HJ, Farrington CP, Spiessens B, Musonda P (2006). Tutorial in biostatistics: the self-controlled case series method. Stat Med, 25: 1768-1797.

Challenge 13 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.2
Randomisation does not remove confounding factors from participants. Why does it nevertheless control confounding in a trial?
  1. It excludes participants with confounding factors
  2. Confounders only distort a comparison if unevenly distributed, and randomisation balances known and unknown confounders between arms on average
  3. It allows adjustment for confounders in the analysis
  4. It makes the sample representative of the population
  5. It guarantees perfect balance in small trials
Show the working
Answer: B Confounders only distort a comparison if unevenly distributed, and randomisation balances known and unknown confounders between arms on average

A confounder biases a comparison only when it is more common in one group. Random allocation makes each arm similar on all characteristics, measured or not, on average. Chance imbalance can still occur in small samples, which is why stratified randomisation or minimisation is sometimes used.

Randomisation works on average and improves with size. Small trials can still be unbalanced by chance, which stratified randomisation or minimisation reduces.

Rule: Randomisation balances confounders; it does not remove them.
Trap: Confusing random allocation with random sampling.

Source: Altman DG, Bland JM (1999). Treatment allocation in controlled trials: why randomise? BMJ, 318: 1209.

Challenge 14 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.1

Glass described primary analysis (original data), secondary analysis (reanalysis of data) and meta-analysis (statistical analysis of results from many studies). He applied it first to psychotherapy outcome research.

Challenge 15 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.1
Screening for a slowly progressive cognitive disorder detects a higher proportion of mild, slowly progressing cases than are seen in routine clinical practice. Screen-detected cases appear to do better. What is this artefact?
  1. Lead-time bias
  2. Neyman bias
  3. Recall bias
  4. Length-time bias
  5. Hawthorne effect
Show the working
Answer: D Length-time bias

Aggressive cases progress quickly between screens and present clinically, while indolent cases are caught by screening. Screen-detected cases therefore have a better prognosis regardless of treatment. Overdiagnosis is the extreme form.

Slow disease sits longer in the detectable phase, so screens find more of it. Screen-detected cases look better regardless of treatment.

Rule: Screening finds slow cases: length-time bias.
Trap: Concluding that screening improves prognosis.

Source: Celentano DD, Szklo M (2019). Gordis Epidemiology, 6th ed. Philadelphia: Elsevier.

Challenge 17 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.6
Appraising a prospective cohort study of childhood adversity and adult depression, which finding most threatens validity?
  1. Exposure was measured before the outcome occurred
  2. 35% of participants were lost to follow-up, more often in the high-adversity group
  3. The study reported relative risks
  4. Participants were not randomised
  5. Outcome assessors used a validated interview
Show the working
Answer: B 35% of participants were lost to follow-up, more often in the high-adversity group

Cohort studies are vulnerable to attrition bias because follow-up is long. When losses are large and differ between exposed and unexposed groups, the relative risk can be distorted in either direction. Appraisal also checks blinding of outcome assessment and adjustment for confounders.

Appraise a cohort by asking who was lost, from which group and why. Then check blinding of outcome assessment and adjustment for confounders.

Challenge 18 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.3
A researcher wants to analyse time to first relapse after discharge, using Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression. Which design provides the necessary data?
  1. Cohort study
  2. Case-control study
  3. Cross-sectional survey
  4. Ecological study
  5. Case report

Kaplan-Meier and Cox methods handle people followed for different lengths of time, censoring those lost or event-free at the end. This requires a defined starting point and follow-up, which a cohort (or an RCT, which is a cohort with allocation) provides.

Censoring lets people with different lengths of follow-up contribute. An RCT is also a cohort in this sense, with exposure allocated by chance.

Challenge 19 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.1
A service plans to enrol patients with high alcohol use scores into a brief intervention and measure change. Before starting, how can it reduce regression to the mean at the selection stage?
  1. Use a single, carefully timed baseline measurement
  2. Select only the very highest scorers
  3. Measure outcome at one week rather than three months
  4. Use a more sensitive scale
  5. Select patients using the mean of two or more baseline measurements
Show the working
Answer: E Select patients using the mean of two or more baseline measurements

Taking repeated measurements before selection gives a better estimate of each person's true level. Regression to the mean can also be estimated statistically, or controlled with a randomised comparison group.

The mean of repeated baselines is a better estimate of each person's usual level. Fewer people are then selected because of one bad day.

Trap: Selecting on a single extreme reading.

Challenge 21 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.5
Which statement about measures of association is correct?
  1. A relative risk below 1 is a negative relative risk
  2. A risk difference can be negative, but a relative risk cannot
  3. The odds ratio is calculated as (a x b) / (c x d)
  4. The null value for a risk difference is 1
  5. Relative risk can be calculated from any case-control study
Show the working
Answer: B A risk difference can be negative, but a relative risk cannot

If an exercise programme lowers depression risk from 0.20 to 0.12, the risk difference is minus 0.08 (an absolute risk reduction) and the RR is 0.6. A ratio can never fall below zero.

Difference measures run from minus 1 to plus 1 with a null of 0. Ratio measures run from 0 upwards with a null of 1.

Rule: Ratios: 0 to infinity, null 1. Differences: can be negative, null 0. OR = ad / bc.
Trap: Calling RR 0.6 a negative relative risk.

Source: Celentano DD, Szklo M (2019). Gordis Epidemiology, 6th ed. Philadelphia: Elsevier.

Challenge 22 · 13.2.1.1, 13.2.1.2 Bias and confounding · 13.2.1.2
Which method allows a researcher to examine both confounding and effect modification by the same variable?
  1. Randomisation
  2. Restriction
  3. Stratification
  4. Matching
  5. Blinding
Show the working
Answer: C Stratification

Within each stratum the confounder is held roughly constant. If stratum estimates differ, the variable modifies the effect. If they are similar to each other but different from the crude estimate, the variable confounds.

Restriction and matching fix the confounder at one level, so they cannot show whether effects differ. Stratification keeps every level in view.

Rule: Stratify to see both.
Trap: Thinking restriction can show effect modification.

Source: Rothman KJ, Greenland S, Lash TL (2008). Modern Epidemiology, 3rd ed. Philadelphia: Lippincott Williams and Wilkins.

Challenge 23 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.2.1
Which is NOT a feature of a retrospective (historical) cohort study compared with a prospective cohort study?
  1. It uses data that were recorded in the past
  2. It can be completed more quickly
  3. Data quality depends on what was originally recorded
  4. It requires a smaller sample size
  5. Exposure was recorded before the outcome occurred
Show the working
Answer: D It requires a smaller sample size

In a historical cohort the investigator starts at the present, looks back to define exposure from old records, then traces outcomes up to now. The cohort logic is unchanged, so the numbers needed are much the same. The main weakness is incomplete or inconsistent records.

Retrospective cohorts are quicker and cheaper because the follow-up has already happened. The price is record quality, not sample size.

Rule: Retrospective cohort: faster and cheaper, same sample size, data quality limited by records.
Trap: Assuming retrospective means small.

Source: Grimes DA, Schulz KF (2002). Cohort studies: marching towards outcomes. Lancet, 359: 341-345.

Challenge 24 · 13.2.2, 13.2.3 Observational designs: descriptive and analytical · 13.2.3.3.3
Which statement about confounding in a self-controlled case series is correct?
  1. All confounders, including age and season, are controlled automatically
  2. No confounders are controlled because there is no comparison group
  3. Confounders that do not change over the observation period, such as sex, genetics and deprivation, are controlled automatically; age needs to be modelled
  4. Only measured confounders can be controlled
  5. Confounding by indication is removed entirely
Show the working
Answer: C Confounders that do not change over the observation period, such as sex, genetics and deprivation, are controlled automatically; age needs to be modelled

Because comparisons are within individuals, stable characteristics, measured or not, cancel out. Age changes during observation and stroke risk rises with age, so age bands are added to the model. Other time-varying factors, such as a recent illness prompting treatment, can still bias the result.

Within-person comparison is the design's great strength, but it does not touch confounders that change over time, so age and season are modelled.

Rule: SCCS: fixed confounders cancel; time-varying confounders (age, season, illness course) need modelling.
Trap: Thinking SCCS removes all confounding.

Source: Petersen I, Douglas I, Whitaker H (2016). Self controlled case series methods: an alternative to standard epidemiological study designs. BMJ, 354: i4515.

Challenge 25 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.5
When is a random-effects model most clearly indicated in a meta-analysis?
  1. When all studies come from one country
  2. When there is statistical heterogeneity between study results
  3. When the studies are homogeneous
  4. When only one study is available
  5. When publication bias has been excluded
Show the working
Answer: B When there is statistical heterogeneity between study results

A fixed-effect model assumes every study estimates the same true effect. When results vary more than chance allows, a random-effects model assumes a distribution of true effects and estimates their mean, with a wider confidence interval.

Random effects is chosen when results differ more than chance would allow. It estimates the mean of a distribution of true effects rather than a single shared effect.

Rule: Heterogeneity present: random effects.
Trap: Believing random effects explains heterogeneity; it only accommodates it.

Source: DerSimonian R, Laird N (1986). Meta-analysis in clinical trials. Controlled Clinical Trials, 7: 177-188.

Challenge 26 · 13.4 Systematic review and meta-analysis · 13.4.5
Compared with a fixed-effect model, how does a random-effects model weight the studies in a meta-analysis that has heterogeneity?
  1. It gives all the weight to the largest study
  2. It gives relatively more weight to small studies
  3. It ignores sample size completely
  4. It weights by year of publication
  5. It gives relatively less weight to small studies
Show the working
Answer: B It gives relatively more weight to small studies

Random-effects weight = 1 / (within-study variance + tau-squared). Because tau-squared is the same for every study, the large studies lose proportionally more of their advantage. If small studies are biased, random effects can amplify that bias.

Because the same tau-squared is added to every study, large studies lose proportionally more weight than small ones. If small trials are biased, random effects can magnify that bias.

Rule: Random effects flattens the weights toward small studies.
Trap: Forgetting that small-study bias is magnified under random effects.

Source: Higgins JPT, Thomas J, Chandler J et al. (eds) (2024). Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.5. Cochrane.
