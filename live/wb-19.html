13.6.1Five ways to weigh cost against benefit

Big idea. Every economic evaluation counts costs in pounds; the five types differ only in the currency used to count what the money buys.
Picture it as five shoppers with the same purse and different shopping lists.

Syllabus: Cost minimisation, cost benefit, cost effectiveness, cost utility and cost consequence analysis

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 12 · Types of economic evaluation →

The story in plain words. Every economic evaluation compares at least two options and asks what each costs and what each achieves. The cost side is always counted in pounds. What changes from one type to the next is the currency used for the outcome, and that single choice gives the five names the exam expects you to know. If the outcomes have already been shown to be the same, only costs matter, and a cost-minimisation analysis simply picks the cheaper route. If there is one clinical outcome in its own natural units, such as relapses prevented or symptom-free days, the study is a cost-effectiveness analysis. Convert the outcome into QALYs and it becomes a cost-utility analysis, the form NICE uses as its reference case. Put both sides into money and you have a cost-benefit analysis, the only type that can say whether a programme is worth doing at all: net benefit above £0, or a benefit to cost ratio above 1. Lay the costs beside a list of separate outcomes without combining them and it is a cost-consequence analysis. ((? A report gives the cost per relapse prevented. Which type is it? ?)) Cost-effectiveness, because a relapse is a natural unit, not a utility and not money.

The people and the evidence. Ray Robinson set out the types in a short BMJ series in 1993. Drummond and colleagues, whose textbook is now in its fourth edition, insist that a cost-minimisation analysis is legitimate only when equivalence has been shown, not merely when a trial failed to find a difference. Cost-utility analysis grew because ratios in natural units cannot cross disease boundaries. A cost per bipolar relapse prevented cannot be ranked against a cost per suicide prevented. The QALY gives a common currency, which is why a single-budget health system leans on it.

From everyday life to the clinic. Picture five shoppers with the same purse. One buys the cheaper of two identical loaves. One compares price per slice. One compares price per unit of pleasure across bread, fruit and cheese. One asks whether the trip is worth the bus fare at all. One brings home the receipt and a list of everything bought and lets the family choose. A commissioning meeting makes the same choices. Two crisis teams with proven equivalent outcomes call for cost-minimisation. A perinatal service competing with a school anti-bullying programme needs a common currency, QALYs or pounds, because the outcomes share no natural unit; once earnings count too, only cost-benefit will do. A new early intervention model with symptom, readmission, carer and employment results is often reported first as cost-consequence.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is calling any cost per outcome study cost-utility. Look at the denominator: a QALY or DALY means cost-utility; anything counted in its own units means cost-effectiveness. The second trap is calling a cost-only comparison cost-minimisation when no one has shown that the outcomes are equal. The third is mixing up cost-benefit and cost-utility when programmes have different outcomes. Both can cope with that, but only cost-benefit puts the benefit in pounds. If the stem says willingness to pay, net benefit or benefit to cost ratio, you are in cost-benefit territory.

13.6.4Valuing health: QALYs, utilities and DALYs

Big idea. A QALY turns length and quality of life into one number by multiplying years lived by a utility between 0 and 1.
Picture it as a rectangle of carpet, its width the years and its height the quality.

Syllabus: Cost utility analysis: QALYs and DALYs, and eliciting utilities by time trade-off, standard gamble and willingness to pay

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 18 · QALYs, utilities and time trade-off →

The story in plain words. A QALY, or quality-adjusted life year, is one year lived in full health. A year in a worse state counts for less, weighted by a utility anchored at 0 for dead and 1 for full health. Some states, such as extreme pain with severe anxiety, are valued below zero. QALYs are years multiplied by utility, so they behave like area. Four years at a utility of 0.6 gives 2.4 QALYs. If a new treatment for resistant OCD lifts utility to 0.75 for those four years, the gain is 0.15 x 4 = 0.6 QALYs, the teal strip in the figure. The DALY runs the other way. It adds years of life lost to years lived with disability, using weights from 0 (full health) to 1 (death), so it measures health lost and fewer is better. ((? Is an intervention judged by DALYs gained? ?)) No, by DALYs averted: QALYs are gained, DALYs are averted.

The people and the evidence. Alan Williams used QALYs in 1985 to set coronary bypass grafting against other uses of NHS money, one of the first league tables. Torrance (1986) reviewed how utilities are measured. In the standard gamble a person chooses between their current state for certain and a treatment that cures with probability p or kills with probability 1 minus p; at indifference the utility equals p. It follows expected utility theory and so captures attitude to risk. In the time trade-off a person chooses between t years in the state and x years in full health, and utility is x / t. Dolan used time trade-off interviews with more than 3,000 members of the UK public to value EQ-5D states, and in 1994 Murray set out the technical basis of the DALY, built for the Global Burden of Disease study.

From everyday life to the clinic. Picture a rectangle of carpet. Its width is the years and its height is the quality. A long, thin strip can hold the same carpet as a short, tall one, which is both the point and the controversy of the QALY. On the ward, a man with chronic schizophrenia who would swap 10 years as he is for 7 in full health is telling you a utility of 0.7. Ask him how much he would pay to be well and you are using willingness to pay, which values health in money and so belongs to cost-benefit analysis.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Standard gamble has risk in it, time trade-off has years in it, and willingness to pay has money in it. If the stem mentions a chance of death, it is the gamble; if it mentions fewer years in full health, it is the trade-off. The calculation traps are simple. Multiply years by utility, do not add them. In a time trade-off put the healthy years on top: 7 / 10 is 0.7, while 10 / 7 is impossible as a utility.

  2. Robinson R (1993). Costs and cost-minimisation analysis. BMJ, 307: 726-728.
  3. Williams A (1985). Economics of coronary artery bypass grafting. BMJ, 291: 326-329.

13.6.6The ICER, the cost-effectiveness plane and acceptability curves

Big idea. The ICER is the extra cost of one extra unit of benefit, read as a slope from the origin and judged against what the NHS is willing to pay.
Picture it as the price of the upgrade, not the price of the car.

Syllabus: Incremental cost effectiveness ratio, with the cost-effectiveness plane and the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 21 · ICER and cost-effectiveness plane →

The story in plain words. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio compares a new option with the one it would replace: difference in cost divided by difference in effect, (C1 minus C0) / (E1 minus E0). It is always about differences. Dividing each drug's cost by its own effect gives average ratios, which answer a question nobody asked. On the cost-effectiveness plane the comparator sits at the origin, extra effect runs east and extra cost runs north. A new option is a point, and its ICER is the slope of the line from the origin to that point. The resistant OCD treatment costs £12,000 more and gains 0.6 QALYs, a slope of £20,000 per QALY, below the NICE band. NICE now judges cost-utility results against £25,000 to £35,000 per QALY, raised from £20,000 to £30,000 in April 2026. ((? Where on the plane is an option that is cheaper and better? ?)) South-east: it dominates and should be adopted whatever the threshold. North-west, dearer and worse, is dominated and rejected.

The people and the evidence. William Black drew the plane in 1990 to show that a ratio on its own hides the quadrant it came from. A negative ICER can mean cheaper and better or dearer and worse, which is why it must always be read with its quadrant. The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve was first proposed in the mid-1990s; Fenwick, Claxton and Sculpher (2001) set out how it summarises a probabilistic sensitivity analysis. For each willingness to pay on the x axis it shows the probability that each option is cost-effective on the y axis. For mutually exclusive options the curves sum to 1 at every point, and at £0 the cheapest option leads.

From everyday life to the clinic. Buying a car, the useful number is the price of the upgrade. An extra £2,000 for a better engine is judged by what the engine adds, not by the price of the whole car. The ICER is that upgrade price, per unit of health. In a commissioning paper, an intensive home treatment team for 150 young people at £4,000 each that prevents 12 admissions costs £600,000 / 12 = £50,000 per admission prevented. Whether that is good value depends on what an admission costs and what else the money could buy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Watch the axes. The plane has extra effect on x and extra cost on y; the acceptability curve has willingness to pay on x and probability on y. Examiners swap them. An acceptability curve shows how likely each option is to be the best value at a given price, not which option is most effective. The tallest curve at a threshold is the most likely good buy, and it may not be the strongest drug. Check the threshold too: answers built on £20,000 to £30,000 are out of date.

Sources
  1. Robinson R (1993). Cost-benefit analysis. BMJ, 307: 924-926.
  2. Fenwick E, Claxton K, Sculpher M (2001). Representing uncertainty: the role of cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Health Economics, 10: 779-787.
  3. Briggs A, Claxton K, Sculpher M (2006). Decision Modelling for Health Economic Evaluation. Oxford: Oxford University Press.
  4. Greenhalgh T (2019). How to Read a Paper: the Basics of Evidence-Based Medicine and Healthcare, 6th ed. Chichester: Wiley.

13.6.7Costs, opportunity cost and discounting

Big idea. What counts as a cost depends on whose pocket you look in, and any cost or benefit in the future is worth less today.
Picture it as a pound in the hand against a pound promised next year.

Syllabus: Economic concepts: direct, indirect, intangible and opportunity costs, discounting and discounting calculations

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 27 · Costs, perspective and discounting →

The story in plain words. Direct costs are the resources used to deliver care: drugs, staff time, beds, tests and the building. Patient travel and childcare to attend are direct non-medical costs. Indirect costs are productivity lost to illness or to treatment, such as days off work. Intangible costs are pain, grief, fear and stigma, real but hard to price. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up. With a fixed budget, every pound spent on a new ECT suite is a pound not spent on eating disorder outreach, so the true cost is the health forgone elsewhere. Discounting reflects time preference: people value costs and benefits now more than later. Present value = future value / (1 + r) to the power t. NICE discounts costs and health effects at 3.5% a year, so £10,000 due in two years is worth 10,000 / 1.0712 = £9,335 today. ((? Why does a higher discount rate hurt early intervention services? ?)) Their costs come early and their benefits late, and late values shrink most.

The people and the evidence. The NICE reference case counts costs to the NHS and personal social services and leaves productivity out. A societal perspective would bring indirect costs back in, which matters a great deal in mental illness, where much of the cost falls on work and on families. Claxton and colleagues (2015) estimated the health actually displaced when NHS spending rises and put it at about £13,000 per QALY, well below the threshold NICE uses. That is opportunity cost made concrete: money spent on one patient comes out of the care of another.

From everyday life to the clinic. A pound in the hand is worth more than a pound promised next year: you could spend it, save it or simply stop worrying about it. The same instinct, applied by a health system, is discounting. It is not inflation, which is removed separately by using prices from a single year. A doctor who chooses psychiatry over a better paid banking career has an opportunity cost, the salary she gave up. A patient with social anxiety who loses pay to attend therapy in working hours carries an indirect cost, which an NHS-perspective evaluation would not count.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Overheads are a direct cost even though accountants call them indirect. Suffering is intangible, not indirect. A carer's lost earnings are indirect, not direct. In calculations, divide by 1.035 for each year rather than subtracting 3.5% each year: two years gives £9,335, not £9,300. Discounting for one year only gives £9,662, a favourite distractor.

Sources
  1. Robinson R (1993). Cost-benefit analysis. BMJ, 307: 924-926.
  2. Fenwick E, Claxton K, Sculpher M (2001). Representing uncertainty: the role of cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Health Economics, 10: 779-787.
  3. Dolan P (1997). Modeling valuations for EuroQol health states. Medical Care, 35: 1095-1108.

13.6.8Appraising economic studies and testing them with sensitivity analysis

Big idea. An economic result is only as sound as the effectiveness evidence beneath it, and only as trustworthy as its sensitivity analysis shows.
Picture it as shaking a new shelf before you load it.

Syllabus: Critically appraise economic evaluation studies, and sensitivity analysis in the context of an economic evaluation

Read more in your textbookEconomic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 11 · Types of economic evaluation →Economic Evaluation, QI & Governance, page 33 · Sensitivity analysis and tornado diagrams →

The story in plain words. Appraising an economic evaluation starts with three questions: what is being compared, from whose perspective, and over what time horizon. The perspective decides which costs are counted. A study that counts only NHS bed costs takes the health service provider view and ignores social care, carers and work. Next, where did the effectiveness come from? Ideally from a randomised trial. A mirror-image study compares the same patients before and after a switch, for example to a long-acting injectable, and has no control group, so regression to the mean and falling bed numbers over time can pass for drug effect. Bed savings are gross until the cost of the drug itself is subtracted. Last, does the answer survive uncertainty? A one-way sensitivity analysis varies one input at a time and is drawn as a tornado diagram, widest bar on top. ((? In the figure, which input could change the decision? ?)) Relapse rate, because its bar runs through the band and past £35,000 per QALY while every other bar stays below £25,000.

The people and the evidence. Drummond's textbook offers a ten-point checklist for appraisal, and CHEERS 2022 (Husereau and colleagues) gives authors a 28-item reporting standard covering perspective, comparators, time horizon, discount rate and how uncertainty was handled. Briggs, Claxton and Sculpher (2006) set out probabilistic sensitivity analysis: every uncertain input is given a distribution and the model is run thousands of times, a Monte Carlo simulation. The cloud of results is plotted on the cost-effectiveness plane and summarised as an acceptability curve, and NICE expects it.

From everyday life to the clinic. Before you put books on a new shelf you give it a shake. If it wobbles at a gentle push, you do not trust it. Sensitivity analysis is that push applied to a model: change the uncertain numbers and see whether the recommendation falls over. In a business case for depot clinics, 60 patients falling from 52 to 16 bed days over two years is 18 days each a year, worth 60 x 18 x £480 = £518,400 a year in beds. Before celebrating, ask who started the depot just after a crisis and how many beds the trust closed in the same years.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The odd one out in a list of sensitivity analyses is usually a valuation method. Time trade-off values health; it does not test uncertainty. Threshold analysis finds the input value at which the decision changes, and scenario analysis uses best and worst cases. Do not confuse economic sensitivity analysis with the sensitivity of a diagnostic test. And when a stem gives a two-year mirror, annualise before you multiply.

Sources
  1. Fenwick E, Claxton K, Sculpher M (2001). Representing uncertainty: the role of cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Health Economics, 10: 779-787.
  2. Brooks R (1996). EuroQol: the current state of play. Health Policy, 37: 53-72.
  3. Murray CJL (1994). Quantifying the burden of disease: the technical basis for disability-adjusted life-years. Bulletin of the World Health Organization, 72: 429-445.

13.7When to use qualitative research, and mixed methods

Big idea. Qualitative research answers why, how and what it is like, and it earns its place beside trials rather than instead of them.
Picture it as a reporter who asks why, standing beside an accountant who counts.

Syllabus: Qualitative research: when to use it and when not to, and mixed methods designs (13.7.6)

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 115 · Qualitative approaches →

The story in plain words. A ward launches a daytime activity programme and almost nobody comes. A spreadsheet can tell you how many came; it cannot tell you why. For that someone has to sit on the ward, watch, listen and write it down. That is qualitative research: the data are words, observations and documents, gathered where life actually happens. The reasoning is inductive, working up from the data to concepts, so the work generates hypotheses rather than testing them. Participants are chosen on purpose for what they can tell you, purposive sampling, and recruitment stops at saturation, not at a number set by a power calculation. What qualitative work cannot do is just as examinable. It cannot estimate prevalence, and it cannot tell you whether a treatment works or by how much. ((? A trust asks whether a psychoeducation group cuts readmission and proposes focus groups with people who attended. What is wrong? ?)) There is no comparison group and no counted outcome, so the groups can explain the experience but cannot estimate the effect.

The people and the evidence. The Medical Research Council guidance on process evaluation (Moore and colleagues, 2015) put qualitative work at the heart of complex intervention trials. Interviews and observation show how an intervention was delivered and received, which explains why a trial did or did not work. Creswell and Plano Clark named the core mixed methods designs. Explanatory sequential runs numbers first, then words to explain them. Exploratory sequential runs words first, then builds a survey or instrument from the themes. Convergent collects both strands at the same time and merges them.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of a restaurant whose takings have dropped. The accountant tells you by how much; only a conversation with diners tells you the new chef oversalts everything. Each answers a different question, and a sensible owner wants both. On the ward, asking how people with a diagnosis of emotionally unstable personality disorder feel about the label is a qualitative question. Asking how many reject it is a survey question, and the survey items are better for being built from interviews first.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is a large census that asks about opinions. Closed tick-box questions are quantitative however many people answer them. The second is a case-control study slipped into a list of qualitative designs: if it produces an odds ratio, it is not qualitative. For mixed methods, read the order. A trial followed by interviews to explain a weak effect is explanatory. Focus groups whose themes become survey items is exploratory. Two qualitative methods together, such as interviews plus focus groups, are not mixed methods at all.

Sources
  1. Reeves S, Kuper A, Hodges BD (2008). Qualitative research methodologies: ethnography. BMJ, 337: a1020.
  2. Guest G, Bunce A, Johnson L (2006). How many interviews are enough? An experiment with data saturation and variability. Field Methods, 18: 59-82.
  3. O'Brien BC, Harris IB, Beckman TJ, Reed DA, Cook DA (2014). Standards for reporting qualitative research: a synthesis of recommendations. Academic Medicine, 89: 1245-1251.
  4. Glaser BG, Strauss AL (1967). The Discovery of Grounded Theory: Strategies for Qualitative Research. Chicago: Aldine.

Answer: A Explanatory sequential mixed methods

Mixed methods designs are named by the order and purpose of the strands. Explanatory sequential: quantitative then qualitative to explain.
