13.2.3.2.4Regression to the mean and screening artefacts

Big idea. Some apparent improvements are artefacts of when and how people were selected or diagnosed: extreme scores drift back towards average, and earlier diagnosis lengthens survival from diagnosis without adding a day of life.
Picture it as a golfer's best round, followed by an ordinary one.

Syllabus: Biases in follow-up comparisons: regression to the mean, lead-time and length-time bias

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 102 · Screening criteria and bias →

The story in plain words. A golfer who shoots his best round ever will probably play worse next week, not because he has lost skill but because the best round contained luck. Regression to the mean is that luck draining away. Anyone selected for an extreme score, high or low, tends to score nearer their usual level on retest, with or without treatment. Screening creates a different illusion. Without screening, a man is diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease from symptoms at 78 and dies at 84: survival from diagnosis is 6 years. With screening, detection comes at 75 and he still dies at 84: survival is 9 years. The extra 3 years, shown in grey in the figure, is lead time; his lifespan is unchanged. Length-time bias is subtler. Slowly progressing disease spends longer in the detectable window, so screening catches more mild cases, while aggressive cases surface between screens. Screen-detected cases look as if they do better whatever the treatment. ((? Median survival from dementia diagnosis rises from 5 to 7 years after screening starts, but age at death does not change. What explains it? ?)) Lead time of about 2 years.

The people and the evidence. Galton (1886) noticed that very tall parents had children who were tall but closer to average, and called it regression towards mediocrity. Bland and Altman (1994) and Barnett and colleagues (2005) explained why it haunts clinical research: every measurement contains random variation, and selecting people on one high reading selects some of that variation too. Morton and Torgerson (2003) showed how it misleads service decisions, such as interventions targeted at units after an unusually bad year that then appear to work. Gordis sets out the screening artefacts and the fair test of a screening programme: mortality in randomised screened and unscreened populations.

From everyday life to the clinic. A community team offers group therapy to everyone scoring above 25 on the BDI-II at one assessment, and mean scores fall by 8 points. Some of that fall would have happened anyway. A randomised control arm selected in the same way regresses equally, so the difference between arms is the treatment effect. Before starting, selecting on the mean of 2 or more baseline readings reduces the problem. For screening, ask about deaths per head of population, not survival from diagnosis. A programme that only moves the date of diagnosis will always look good on survival and do nothing for mortality.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The traps are treating an uncontrolled before-and-after fall in high scorers as proof of benefit, and treating longer survival from diagnosis as longer life. Keep the three apart. Regression to the mean comes from selecting on extreme values; lead time from moving diagnosis earlier; length time from screening preferentially finding slow disease. Overdiagnosis is length time at its extreme: disease that would never have caused harm in the person's lifetime.

13.2.1.1Bias, chance and the result that misleads

Big idea. Chance scatters results around the truth and shrinks as samples grow; bias shifts every result the same way and no sample size can fix it.
Picture it as two darts players, one shaky and one with a bent sight.

Syllabus: Sources of bias and strategies to overcome them

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 44 · Bias: selection, information →

The story in plain words. Picture two darts players aiming at the bullseye. The first has a shaky hand: darts land all around the centre, some high, some low, but on average they are right. The second has a steady hand and a bent sight: every dart lands in a tight cluster, confidently, in the wrong place. The first player shows random error, which we call chance. The second shows systematic error, which we call bias. More darts help the first player, because the misses cancel out and the average closes in on the bullseye. More darts do nothing for the second, because each one repeats the same mistake. ((? A study doubles its sample size. Which kind of error shrinks? ?)) Random error shrinks; bias stays exactly where it was, only now with a narrower confidence interval around the wrong answer. Bias enters at three doors. Selection bias comes from who gets into the study and who stays in it. Information bias comes from how exposure or outcome is measured, and its best known form in psychiatry is recall bias, where people with a disorder search their memories harder than healthy controls. Confounding comes from a third factor and has its own lesson.

Random error scatters estimates around the true value; bias moves them all the same way. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

The people and the evidence. David Sackett catalogued more than thirty named biases in analytic research in 1979, from the volunteer bias of keen participants to the recall bias of anxious parents. His point was practical: once a bias has a name you can look for it in the methods section. Altman and Bland added the companion warning about chance in 1995: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A small study that finds nothing may simply have been too small, which is a type II error, not proof that the exposure is harmless.

From everyday life to the clinic. Imagine a register study of bipolar disorder that takes obstetric complications from midwives' notes written at the time of birth, decades before anyone knew who would become ill. Recall bias is close to impossible here, because nobody was asked to remember anything. The design has closed that door. The same study finds that maternal pre-eclampsia is not significantly linked to bipolar disorder, yet only a handful of mothers had it. Before calling the earlier positive studies wrong, ask whether this one had the power to see an effect of that size.

Two bathroom scales: one wobbles around the truth (chance), one is set 2 kg heavy (bias). Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The exam tests whether you can tell the doors apart. Exposure taken from records made before the outcome removes recall bias, not confounding. A large sample removes chance, not bias. A rare exposure in a modest sample points to type II error. Watch for the option that blames the wrong error. If the study is careful about measurement and matching but small for the exposure in question, the answer is chance, not bias.

13.2.1.2Confounding: the third factor behind the link

Big idea. A confounder is a third factor tied to both exposure and outcome, so the link you see may belong to it rather than to the exposure.
Picture it as ice cream sales and drownings both rising in a heatwave.

Syllabus: Confounding and strategies to reduce risk of confounding

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 50 · Confounding and DAGs →

The story in plain words. On hot days ice cream sales rise and so do drownings. Nobody thinks ice cream drowns people. The heat drives both: more people buy ice cream and more people swim. The heat is a confounder, a third factor linked to the exposure and, independently, to the outcome. Three conditions make a confounder. It is associated with the exposure. It affects the outcome in its own right. And it is not a step on the causal path from exposure to outcome; a step on the path is a mediator, and adjusting for it hides real effects. ((? Patients who attend follow-up clinic are readmitted less often. What might confound this? ?)) Illness severity, insight and family support, which make people both more likely to attend and less likely to relapse. There are two moments to deal with confounding. At the design stage you can randomise, restrict entry or match cases to controls. At the analysis stage you can stratify or adjust in a regression model. Only randomisation balances confounders nobody has thought of.

A confounder opens a back-door path between exposure and outcome. Illustrative figures drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Nathan Mantel and William Haenszel gave epidemiology its standard stratified estimate in 1959: work out the association within each level of the confounder, then pool it. If the pooled odds ratio moves well away from the crude one, confounding was at work. Sander Greenland, Judea Pearl and James Robins popularised causal diagrams in 1999. Drawing exposure, outcome and the candidates as boxes and arrows shows which variables open a back-door path and must be adjusted, and which sit on the causal path and must be left alone.

From everyday life to the clinic. In a register study, neonatal unit admission is linked to later bipolar disorder before adjustment, still linked after adjustment for maternal age and social class, but no longer significant once the other birth complications are added. The admission was probably standing in for those complications rather than acting on its own. Multiple regression has its own trap. Put obesity, diabetes, triglycerides and waist size into one model and they compete, because they measure much the same thing. Each loses significance even when the model as a whole predicts well. That is collinearity, not proof that none of them matters.

Hot weather sells ice cream and fills the sea with swimmers: a confounder makes two things move together. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Questions describe an observational difference and a confident causal conclusion. The answer is nearly always that the groups may differ in other ways: confounding. Matching on age and sex does not cure it, because many other factors remain unmatched. Read adjusted tables column by column. An estimate that crosses 1 only after adjustment has been explained by the factors added in that column. And remember that confounding cannot be removed by a larger sample.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

13.2.1.3Attributable risk and the population view

Big idea. Attributable risk is the extra risk the exposure adds to each exposed person; scale it by how many people are exposed and you get the burden it adds to the whole population.
Picture it as a leaking roof: how wet each room gets against how much water the whole house takes in.

Syllabus: Interpreting basic epidemiology

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 72 · Cohort studies →

The story in plain words. Think of a house with a leaking roof over some rooms. How much wetter a leaky room is than a dry one is the attributable risk: the risk in the exposed minus the risk in the unexposed. How much extra water the whole house takes in depends also on how many rooms leak. That is the population attributable risk. In numbers: depression affects 0.15 of night-shift workers over five years and 0.05 of day workers. The attributable risk is 0.10. If a quarter of staff work nights, the population attributable risk is 0.10 x 0.25 = 0.025. ((? What share of all cases in the trust does that represent? ?)) The population risk is 0.05 plus 0.025, so 0.025 / 0.075, one third. That last figure is the population attributable fraction. Levin's formula gets it straight from the relative risk and the exposure prevalence: Pe(RR - 1) / [1 + Pe(RR - 1)]. A fraction of 100% would mean every case arose from this one exposure, which almost never happens.

Attributable risk in the exposed and its share of the whole population. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Morton Levin introduced the population fraction in 1953 to ask how much lung cancer smoking explained. Rockhill, Newman and Weinberg warned in 1998 that it is often misused: it assumes the link is causal and unconfounded, and fractions for different causes can add up to more than 100% because causes act together.

From everyday life to the clinic. A relative risk tells a patient how much an exposure multiplies their own risk. A population attributable fraction tells a commissioner how many cases a programme could prevent. The same exposure can have a large relative risk and a small population impact if few people are exposed. So when a public health plan claims that removing an exposure would halve a disorder, check the prevalence of the exposure and whether the association has been shown to be causal.

A leaking roof: the extra damp in the leaking room is the attributable risk; spread over the house it is the population figure. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The common slip is to stop one step early: quoting the attributable risk in the exposed when the question asks about the population, or forgetting to multiply by prevalence. The second is reading the attributable risk as a ratio. It is a difference, so its null value is 0, while relative risk has a null value of 1.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

13.2.2.3Cross-sectional studies: the snapshot

Big idea. A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome at the same moment, so it gives prevalence and odds but cannot say which came first.
Picture it as a single photograph of a busy street.

Syllabus: Cross sectional studies including survey

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 56 · Case reports and cross-sectional studies →

The story in plain words. A photograph of a busy street tells you how many people are on it at one moment. It cannot tell you who arrived first, or who stayed longest. A cross-sectional study is that photograph: exposure and outcome are measured together, once. It is quick and cheap and gives prevalence, the proportion who have the condition at that moment. From its 2x2 table you can also give the odds of a trait among people with and without the disorder. ((? A survey finds high perfectionism in people with generalised anxiety. Can it say perfectionism came first? ?)) No. Both were measured on the same day, so temporality cannot be established. The snapshot also favours long episodes. A short illness that starts and ends between surveys is missed, while a long one is almost certain to be caught. Prevalence studies therefore over-represent chronic cases.

A survey day catches the episodes that are running at that moment. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. The STROBE statement of 2007 set out what a cross-sectional report must show, including how the sample was drawn and how many were approached and responded. A survey of 300 people from a random household frame tells you far more than 300 volunteers from a clinic waiting room.

From everyday life to the clinic. Prevalence is what service planners need: how many people in the area live with the condition now. Incidence, the new cases over time, needs follow-up and comes from cohorts. The two are tied by duration. In a steady state, prevalence is roughly incidence x average duration. A disorder with few new cases but lifelong course, such as schizophrenia, can have a higher prevalence than a common but brief one, such as an adjustment reaction. A new treatment that keeps people alive longer without curing them raises prevalence even as incidence stays flat. A survey reporting that anxious adults are more perfectionistic is a reason to study the question in a cohort, not evidence that perfectionism causes anxiety.

A cross-sectional study is one photograph: it counts who has the condition now, not what came first. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Questions ask what cannot be established. The answer is the order of events. Prevalence, odds, odds ratios and confidence intervals can all be calculated. Read odds questions closely. The odds of a trait among people with the disorder is a single odds, not an odds ratio: high scorers divided by low scorers in that one column.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

  2. Wacholder S, McLaughlin JK, Silverman DT, Mandel JS (1992). Selection of controls in case-control studies. I. Principles. American Journal of Epidemiology, 135: 1019-1028.

13.2.3.1The case-control study: research in reverse

Big idea. A case-control study starts with the outcome and looks back for the exposure, which makes it fast and ideal for rare disorders but leaves it open to recall and selection bias.
Picture it as a detective starting at the crime scene and working backwards.

Syllabus: Case control study

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 63 · Case-control studies →

The story in plain words. A detective arrives after the event and works backwards from it. A case-control study does the same. It begins with people who already have the disorder, the cases, and people who do not, the controls, then looks back to compare how often each group was exposed. Its great strength is efficiency. A disorder that is rare even among exposed people would need an enormous cohort to produce enough cases; a case-control study simply gathers the cases that exist. It can examine many exposures at once. ((? Can it give a relative risk? ?)) No. The investigator chose how many cases and controls to include, so the risk of disease in the exposed cannot be calculated. It gives the odds ratio instead. Each group has an exposure rate: exposed cases over all cases, exposed controls over all controls. With 30/250 against 15/250, cases were twice as often exposed.

A case-control study begins with the outcome and looks back for the exposure. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill's 1950 study of lung cancer patients and hospital controls showed the power of the design: smoking stood out among dozens of exposures within months. A year later Jerome Cornfield showed that, when a disease is rare, the odds ratio from such a study closely approximates the relative risk.

From everyday life to the clinic. The hardest part is choosing controls. They should come from the same population that produced the cases, so that had they developed the disorder they would have been cases. Hospital controls are convenient but may share exposures with the cases, which pulls the odds ratio towards 1. If the exposure is very rare among controls, say under 1%, few people in either group are exposed and a much larger sample is needed to detect an association. Matching controls to cases on age and sex removes those two as confounders, but it has a price. Once a factor is matched it can no longer be studied, and matching on something linked to the exposure itself, called overmatching, hides real associations.

A case-control study is a detective at the scene, working backwards to the clues. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Recognise the design from its sampling: groups defined by outcome, odds ratios reported, exposure asked about or looked up. Then remember what it cannot give: incidence, relative risk, attributable risk. When the question asks for the most efficient design for a rare disorder, it is case-control. When it asks for a rare exposure, it is a cohort that selects people by exposure.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

13.2.3.1.5Calculating and reading the odds ratio

Big idea. The odds ratio is the odds of exposure in cases divided by the odds in controls, which the 2x2 table gives as ad over bc.
Picture it as a bookmaker's odds, set side by side for two crowds.

Syllabus: Calculation of odds ratio

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 25 · Odds ratios versus relative risks →

The story in plain words. A bookmaker never says three in ten will win; he says three to seven, winners against losers. That is odds: those with the feature divided by those without. Probability divides by everyone; odds divide only by the rest. Lay out the 2x2 table with cases and controls in the columns and exposed and unexposed in the rows. The odds of exposure in cases are a over c; in controls, b over d. Their ratio simplifies to ad / bc, the cross-product. ((? Forty of 100 cases and 20 of 100 controls were exposed. What is the odds ratio? ?)) (40 x 80) / (20 x 60) = 2.67. An odds ratio of 1 means no association, above 1 more exposure among cases, below 1 less. It cannot be negative; its floor is zero. If the 95% confidence interval excludes 1, the association is significant at the 5% level.

The odds ratio as a cross-product of the 2x2 table. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Cornfield's 1951 insight is why the odds ratio matters: for a rare disease it approximates the relative risk the case-control design cannot measure. Bland and Altman's 2000 BMJ note adds the caution that with a common outcome the odds ratio sits further from 1 than the risk ratio and overstates it.

From everyday life to the clinic. A study reports an odds ratio of 3 for preterm birth in women with anorexia nervosa. The correct sentence is that the odds of having been born preterm are three times higher in the women with anorexia nervosa. It is not that preterm birth triples the risk of anorexia, and not that three times as many were preterm. When smoking is common, say 60% of patients and 30% of relatives, the odds ratio is 3.5 while the ratio of proportions is only 2. Answer the question that was asked. A confidence interval of 1.2 to 3.0 around an odds ratio of 1.9 says the data fit anything from a modest to a threefold rise in odds, and that chance alone is an unlikely explanation. It does not say that exposure doubles anyone's risk of the disorder.

Probability divides by everyone; odds, like a bookmaker, divide by the rest. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The classic slips are dividing exposed by total instead of exposed by unexposed, putting the table the wrong way round and getting the reciprocal, and reading the odds ratio as a relative risk. Interpret in the direction of the design: in a case-control study the odds are of exposure, compared between people with and without the outcome.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

13.2.3.1.6The nested case-control study

Big idea. A nested case-control study draws its cases and controls from inside one cohort whose exposures were recorded before anyone fell ill.
Picture it as a family photo album: you pick the pages you need instead of reprinting the whole album.

Syllabus: Nested case control study

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 66 · Case-control studies →

The story in plain words. A family photo album already holds every year of your life. To compare the summers before two weddings you do not reprint the whole album; you pull out the pages you need. A nested case-control study works the same way inside a large, already-recorded cohort. Every member who develops the disorder becomes a case. For each case, controls are drawn from cohort members who were still being followed and still free of the disorder on the date the case was diagnosed. This is incidence density sampling, also called risk-set sampling, and it keeps cases and controls comparable in time. Because exposure was written into the record before the outcome happened, there is no recall bias and the order of events is clearer. ((? What problem remains? ?)) Confounding. It is still observational, so a third factor such as weight or deprivation may explain the link.

Risk-set sampling inside a cohort: each new case is matched to people still at risk on that date. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Virginia Ernster's 1994 review set out why the design spread through pharmacoepidemiology: large electronic health records hold prescriptions for hundreds of thousands of people, and analysing a few thousand cases with matched controls costs a fraction of analysing everyone while losing little precision. Matched sets are analysed with conditional logistic regression, which compares each case only with its own controls. The output is still an odds ratio, and with risk-set sampling it estimates the incidence rate ratio that the full cohort would have given.

From everyday life to the clinic. Think of a primary care record cohort of 250,000 adults followed for 12 years. Each new case of type 2 diabetes is matched to four people still at risk on the same date. Prior antipsychotic prescriptions, already on record, are compared using conditional logistic regression. Percentages matter more than counts here, because there are four controls for every case. If 3.0% of cases and 2.0% of controls had a second-generation antipsychotic, the exposure was more common among cases even though the control count is larger.

A nested case-control study pulls the pages it needs from an album already written. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Name the design from the giveaway phrase: controls drawn from the same cohort, at risk on the date of the case. Name the sampling: incidence density. Name the timing: retrospective analysis of data recorded prospectively. And name the main remaining threat: confounding, not recall or selection bias.

The trap on the left, the right reading on the right. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

13.2.3.2The cohort study and the relative risk

Big idea. A cohort study follows people forward from exposure to outcome, so it can count new cases and give the relative risk that a case-control study cannot.
Picture it as two groups of saplings planted in different soil and watched as they grow.

Syllabus: Cohort study

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 73 · Cohort studies →Evidence-Based Medicine, page 26 · Odds ratios versus relative risks →

The story in plain words. Plant two rows of saplings, one in rich soil and one in poor, and come back each spring to see which wilt. You chose them by soil before anything happened, then waited. A cohort study chooses people by exposure, free of the outcome, and follows them forward. Because you count the new cases as they appear, you have incidence in each group. Their ratio is the relative risk: risk in the exposed divided by risk in the unexposed. Their difference is the attributable risk. With dementia in 36/300 after head injury and 12/300 without, the relative risk is 3 and the attributable risk 0.08. ((? The relative risk is 1.0 or less. What does that mean? ?)) There is no evidence of a positive association; below 1 suggests a protective association. Cohorts can be prospective, recruiting now and waiting, or retrospective, starting from old records and tracing what happened since. Either way, exposure is measured before the outcome, which makes temporality clear.

A cohort study runs forwards from exposure to outcome. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Doll and Hill's study of British doctors, launched in 1951, is the textbook cohort: the smoking habits of some 40,000 doctors were recorded and deaths counted for fifty years. In psychiatry, the 1987 follow-up of Swedish conscripts linked heavy cannabis use at conscription to later schizophrenia, and gave the field its first dose-response evidence.

From everyday life to the clinic. Cohorts suit rare exposures, because you can deliberately recruit the exposed, and they can study several outcomes from one exposure. They suit rare outcomes badly, because you may follow thousands of people and see a handful of cases. Their weakness is time. People move, die or drop out, and if those lost differ between groups, attrition bias follows. Good cohort reports therefore state how many were lost from each group and why, and compare the baseline features of those who stayed and those who left. A loss of more than about 20%, or losses that differ between groups, should make you doubt the estimate.

A cohort chooses by soil first and counts the wilting later: risks, relative risk and attributable risk. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Questions test the null value (1 for relative risk), the meaning of relative risk below 1, and the step from risk to attributable risk. Calculate risks within rows, by exposure group, not down columns. 13.2.4From association to causation

Big idea. An association, however strong or well adjusted, only becomes a cause after weighing temporality, strength, consistency, dose-response and plausibility, and an observational table never settles it alone.
Picture it as a jury weighing several strands of evidence, none of them a confession.

Syllabus: Causation in epidemiological studies

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 25 · Cause and effect: Bradford Hill →

The story in plain words. A jury rarely has a confession. It weighs a motive, an opportunity, a witness and a fingerprint, and decides whether together they are beyond reasonable doubt. Deciding whether an association is causal works the same way. Bradford Hill offered nine viewpoints. Temporality, the cause coming before the effect, is the only one that must hold. Strength, consistency, biological gradient and plausibility add weight; specificity adds least in psychiatry, where most causes have many effects. ((? A table shows SSRIs with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.8 for hyponatraemia. Does it prove SSRIs cause hyponatraemia? ?)) No. It shows a strong, adjusted association; causation needs more than one observational table. When you read a table of adjusted odds ratios, the strongest independent predictor is the one with the largest odds ratio whose interval excludes 1. An odds ratio below 1 with an interval wholly below 1 marks lower odds; an interval that crosses 1 marks no significant association.

Bradford Hill's nine viewpoints on causation. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

The people and the evidence. Austin Bradford Hill set out the viewpoints in a 1965 lecture and insisted they were aids to judgement, not a checklist. Kenneth Rothman's 1976 causal pies added that most outcomes need several component causes acting together. John Snow's removal of the Broad Street pump handle in 1854 is the classic experiment viewpoint: take the exposure away and see whether cases fall.

From everyday life to the clinic. A table from a case-control study of older inpatients can inform prescribing: it flags who needs a sodium check. It cannot by itself dictate practice, because unmeasured confounding remains and the patients in front of you may differ from those studied. Shared decisions bring the patient's values in. Present the size of the association honestly, say what it does not prove, and agree a plan. The strongest evidence of causation in psychiatry usually comes from several designs pointing the same way: a cohort that shows exposure before onset, a dose-response gradient, consistent replication across countries, and a plausible mechanism. No single study, however large, closes the case.

Causation is a verdict reached by weighing several strands of evidence against the alternatives. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The exam asks which conclusion is not justified. It is the one that uses the word cause from an observational study. Statements about association, strength or lower odds are usually fine. It also asks for the next clinical step from such a table. Beware answers that turn an association into a universal screening rule.
