3.2.16.1Tests for categorical data

Big idea. Categorical tests all ask the same question, whether the counts you observed are further from what you expected than chance would explain, and they differ only in how they handle small numbers and paired data.
Picture it as counting heads into boxes.

Syllabus: Chi-square, Fisher's exact test, McNemar's test

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 91 · Categorical tests: chi-squared, Fisher →

The story in plain words. Chi-square compares the counts you observed in the cells of a table with the counts you would expect if there were no association. It also works on a single set of categories against an expected pattern, which is the goodness of fit version. Two rules are absolute. Use raw counts, never percentages, because the test is driven by how many people there were. And each person appears in one cell only, which is what makes the test invalid on paired data. Degrees of freedom are rows minus 1, times columns minus 1. A 2 x 2 table therefore has 1 degree of freedom and a 3 x 3 table has 4. The approximation needs expected counts of at least 5 in 80% of cells with none below 1, which is Cochran's rule.

Three tests for counts, and the one thing that separates them

Degrees of freedom: (rows minus 1) x (columns minus 1). A 2 x 2 has 1. Cochran's rule: expected counts of at least 5 in 80 per cent of cells, none below 1.

The people and the evidence. When expected counts fall below 5, Fisher's exact test replaces the approximation with the exact probability for a 2 x 2 table. It is valid at any sample size, which is why it is the small-numbers answer, and it becomes computationally heavy on larger tables. McNemar's test is for paired binary data: the same people classified twice, or matched pairs. It uses only the discordant pairs, the people whose status changed, because the people who stayed the same carry no information about change.

From everyday life to the clinic. Prevalence, response rate and relapse rate are all proportions, so comparing them between two groups is a chi-square. That single sentence answers a large share of the categorical questions in the paper. The paired case comes up more than candidates expect. ((? Fifty patients are assessed for insight before and after a programme. Which test? ?)) McNemar, because it is the same fifty people twice, and chi-square on paired data is wrong.

Counting heads in two wards: is 16 of 40 against 8 of 40 more than chance would produce? That is a chi-square question. If the wards were tiny, Fisher's exact test; if the same patients were counted twice, McNemar's

Where the exam tries to catch you. The classic trap gives you percentages and invites a chi-square. Ask for the counts; without them the test cannot be done. The second is small expected counts, where the stem is pointing you at Fisher without naming it. The third is degrees of freedom, where candidates reach for n minus 1 instead of the rows-by-columns rule.

3.2.16.4Tests for continuous and ordinal data

Big idea. Once you know the outcome type, the number of groups and whether they are the same people, there is only ever one right test, and the rest of the list is noise.
Picture it as a flowchart with three forks.

Syllabus: t tests, ANOVA, ANCOVA, non-parametric equivalents and time-to-event tests

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 109 · Choosing the right statistical test →

The story in plain words. Two groups and a continuous, roughly normal outcome: the unpaired t test compares the means. The same people measured twice, or matched pairs: the paired t test, which works on the within-pair differences rather than on the two sets of values. A subtlety the exam likes. Comparing change scores between two groups is an unpaired test of those differences, not a paired test, because the two groups are different people. Where the data are ordinal or stubbornly skewed, swap in Mann-Whitney U for two independent groups and Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired data.

Answer three questions (what is the outcome, how many groups, are they paired) and the test picks itself

The people and the evidence. One-way ANOVA handles three or more independent group means, assuming normality, independence and equal variances. A significant result says only that at least one group differs from the others; post hoc tests are what identify which. Factorial ANOVA adds a second factor, as in a 3 x 2 design, and tests main effects and interactions. An interaction means the effect of one factor depends on the level of another. Repeated-measures ANOVA handles the same people measured several times. The non-parametric equivalents are Kruskal-Wallis and Friedman.

From everyday life to the clinic. Vickers and Altman (2001) made the case for ANCOVA, which compares group means while adjusting for a continuous covariate, usually the baseline score. It corrects chance baseline imbalance and gains precision, and it is more powerful than analysing change scores. Time to event is its own country. ((? Why can a t test not compare survival between two arms? ?)) Because of censoring: patients who have not had the event cannot be given a time. Use the log-rank test for curves and Cox regression when covariates must be adjusted for.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is reading a significant ANOVA as telling you which groups differ. It does not; that is what post hoc testing is for. The second is the repeated-measures design presented as several independent groups. The third is survival data offered with a t test among the options, which is always wrong whatever else is on the list.

Category 213.7 · Correlation and regression

3.2.20Correlation

Big idea. Correlation measures how tightly two things move together and nothing else, and almost every mistake made with it is a claim about something else.
Picture it as two dancers in step.

Syllabus: Pearson's r, Spearman's rho, r squared, and the limits of correlation

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 114 · Correlation: Pearson, Spearman →

The story in plain words. r measures the strength and direction of a straight-line association between two variables, running from minus 1 to plus 1. Plus 1 is perfect positive, minus 1 perfect negative, and 0 means no linear association, though a clear curve can still sit underneath an r of zero. Pearson's r needs both variables continuous and roughly normal. Spearman's rho works on ranks and is the choice for ordinal data, skewed data or small samples. Significance depends on n as much as on r. A small correlation can be significant in a large sample and a large one non-significant in a tiny sample, which is why r and its p value must always be read together with the number of people.

Four scatter plots: r measures only the strength of a straight-line relation, so a curved relation can have r near zero

The people and the evidence. Four things r cannot do. It does not prove causation, in either direction. It does not predict one variable from another, which is regression's job. It is not transitive: A correlating with B and B with C says nothing definite about A with C. And it does not measure agreement. Bland and Altman (1986) made this point with an example the exam reuses: two raters who always differ by exactly 10 points correlate perfectly and agree not at all. Agreement needs kappa for categories and the Bland-Altman method for continuous measures.

From everyday life to the clinic. r squared is the useful figure for a clinician. It is the proportion of the variance in one variable explained by the other, so an r of 0.6 explains 36% and an r of 0.9 explains 81%. That squaring is deflating in a helpful way. ((? A paper reports a correlation of 0.5 as a strong relationship. What share of the variance is explained? ?)) Twenty-five per cent, which leaves three quarters of it unexplained. In regression the same quantity is R squared, the coefficient of determination.

Ice cream sales rise with temperature, r near 1. Drownings rise with temperature too, so ice cream and drownings correlate. Correlation shows two things move together; it never says one causes the other

Where the exam tries to catch you. The agreement trap is the commonest: a stem about two raters, two scales or two machines is asking about agreement, so the answer is kappa or Bland-Altman, not r. The second is an r of 0 offered as proof of no relationship, when the scatter plot shows a U shape. The third is forgetting to square: an r of 0.7 sounds like most of the variance and explains about half of it.

Three arms, independent participants, a normally distributed outcome measured once: one-way ANOVA is the standard opening analysis. If the GAD-7 scores had been skewed, the non-parametric equivalent, Kruskal-Wallis, would take its place.

The classic distractor is multiple t tests. Three pairwise tests at 5% each give an overall false positive risk near 14%, which is exactly why ANOVA exists.

Rule: More than two independent groups with a normal continuous outcome: one-way ANOVA first, then post hoc tests.
Trap: Running several t tests and inflating the type I error rate.
