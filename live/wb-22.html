Challenge 1 · 13.3 Prevalence, incidence, survival and mortality · 3.2.10
Cumulative proportion of clinical high-risk participants who developed a first psychotic episode, by study
A systematic review of 24 studies (2180 participants) estimated the risk of transition to a first psychotic episode in people meeting clinical high-risk criteria. The pooled transition risk was 27% over a mean follow-up of 30 months. To show the time course, the four studies that reported time-to-event data are plotted in the figure. Which statement about the transition rates shown is correct?
  1. At 400 days the proportion that had made the transition ranged from about 10% to 30% across the studies
  2. By 730 days roughly three quarters of high-risk participants had made the transition in every study
  3. Lindqvist 2011 reported the highest rate of transition
  4. No further transitions occurred after 500 days in Petrov 2015
  5. The spread between studies narrows as follow-up lengthens
Show the working
Answer: A At 400 days the proportion that had made the transition ranged from about 10% to 30% across the studies

Reading a set of cumulative incidence curves is a matter of taking vertical slices. At any chosen time, each curve's height gives that study's estimated transition risk so far, and the difference between the highest and lowest curve is the between-study range at that time. Because every curve starts at zero, differences can only grow from nothing, so the spread tends to widen over follow-up unless the curves converge later.

The range is roughly 10% to 30%. At 730 days the range has widened to 15% to 40%, and every curve is still rising, so transitions continued throughout.

The exam fills the distractors with confident over-readings: 'no further events', 'three quarters', 'narrowing'. Check each one against the figure before trusting it.

Rule: Read cumulative curves at the stated time, and remember the spread between curves usually widens as events accumulate.

Challenge 2 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.14
Which of the following statements about p values is CORRECT?
  1. If p < 0.05 the difference between the populations is definitely real
  2. A very small p value shows that the result is clinically important
  3. For two studies finding the same size of effect, the one with the larger sample will have the smaller p value
  4. A result significant at the 5% level cannot be accepted as valid
  5. A larger hypothesised difference requires a larger sample to achieve adequate power
Show the working
Answer: C For two studies finding the same size of effect, the one with the larger sample will have the smaller p value

A test statistic is roughly the observed effect divided by its standard error, and the standard error shrinks as the square root of the sample size. So the same effect becomes more 'significant' as the sample grows. This is also why p values should always be read alongside the effect size and its confidence interval.

Two studies each finding a 3-point difference in HAM-D would produce different p values if one enrolled 60 patients and the other 600; the larger study has the smaller p.

The other options rehearse classic misreadings: significance as certainty, significance as importance, and the direction of the sample size calculation.

Rule: Same effect, bigger n = smaller p.
Trap: Equating a small p with a large or important effect.

Challenge 3 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.18
Which term describes the threshold chosen in advance to reflect the acceptable risk of a type I error?
  1. Beta
  2. Confidence interval
  3. Alpha
  4. Specificity
  5. Standard deviation
Show the working
Answer: C Alpha

Before analysing, investigators fix alpha, the probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis that they are prepared to tolerate. A result is called significant when p is below alpha. Beta is the matching tolerance for missing a true effect, and 1 minus beta is power.

'Threshold based on acceptable risk of type I error' is the definition of alpha; the conventional value is 5%.

Alpha is sometimes called the significance level; p is compared against it but is not the same thing.

Rule: Alpha = acceptable type I error rate; beta = type II error rate; power = 1 minus beta.
Trap: Confusing the p value (calculated afterwards) with alpha (set beforehand).

Challenge 4 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.13
Which one of the following best defines the standard error of the mean?
  1. The mean of the squared deviations of each value from the mean
  2. The square root of the mean squared deviation from the mean
  3. The standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size
  4. The standard deviation divided by the sample size
  5. The standard deviation multiplied by the square root of the sample size
Show the working
Answer: C The standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size

If you took many samples of the same size from a population and calculated the mean of each, those means would form their own distribution, called the sampling distribution of the mean. Its standard deviation is the standard error. The central limit theorem says this distribution is approximately normal whatever the shape of the original data, provided n is reasonably large.

The standard error equals the population SD divided by the square root of n. Four times the sample halves the SE. This is why confidence intervals narrow with larger studies and why the SE, not the SD, is what you use to build a confidence interval for a mean.

When asked which is more informative on a graph, remember SD describes the patients, SE describes the precision of the estimate; error bars drawn with SE look misleadingly tight.

Rule: SE of the mean = SD / square root of n; it shrinks as the sample grows.
Trap: Picking the formula with n in the denominator rather than root n.

Challenge 5 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.18
Which of the following statements about type I and type II errors is INCORRECT?
  1. A type I error is a false positive: a difference is declared when none exists
  2. A type II error is a false negative: a real difference is missed
  3. Performing many tests without adjusting the significance threshold increases the chance of a type II error
  4. Power is the probability of avoiding a type II error
  5. Multicollinearity in a regression model can inflate standard errors and lead to type II errors
Show the working
Answer: C Performing many tests without adjusting the significance threshold increases the chance of a type II error

Each test run at alpha = 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false positive. Run 20 independent tests on null data and the chance of at least one 'significant' result is about 64%. That is why multiple testing calls for adjustment (Bonferroni and relatives) and why it is a type I, not a type II, problem.

The other options are correct definitions: type I as false positive, type II as false negative, power as 1 minus beta, and multicollinearity inflating standard errors so that real effects become non-significant.

The exam reverses the error type in one option and relies on you knowing which direction multiple testing pushes.

Rule: Multiple testing inflates type I error; low power inflates type II error.
Trap: Linking multiple testing to type II error.
At alpha 0.05 the chance of escaping a false positive across 20 independent tests is 0.95 to the power 20 = 0.36, so 64 of 100 researchers get at least one spurious significant result, a type I problem

Challenge 7 · 13.4 Measures of treatment impact · 3.2.12.2
Which statistic is defined as the control event rate minus the experimental event rate?
  1. Absolute benefit increase
  2. Relative risk reduction
  3. Absolute risk reduction
  4. Relative risk
  5. Number needed to treat
Show the working
Answer: C Absolute risk reduction

The absolute risk reduction is the foundation of the clinically useful effect measures. It is simply the proportion of patients with the adverse outcome on control minus the proportion on treatment. Unlike relative measures it reflects the baseline risk, so a 50% relative reduction of a 2% risk gives an ARR of only 0.01.

The formula is a subtraction with no division, which is the ARR. The NNT is its reciprocal and the RRR is the ARR divided by the CER.

Watch the wording: 'absolute risk reduction' is for a bad event (CER minus EER), 'absolute benefit increase' is for a good event (EBR minus CBR); the numbers are identical in magnitude.

Rule: ARR = CER minus EER, a difference in proportions.

Challenge 13 · 13.3 Prevalence, incidence, survival and mortality · 3.2.10
Two acute adult wards run by one health board in Wales have noticeably different bed occupancy, and managers believe this reflects a difference in length of admission. Some patients are still on the wards on the day the data are collected. Which method is most appropriate for comparing length of stay between the two wards?
  1. Independent samples t test of mean length of stay
  2. Chi-square test of the proportion of admissions longer than 28 days
  3. Survival analysis of time to discharge
  4. One-way analysis of variance of length of stay
  5. Mann-Whitney U test of median length of stay
Show the working
Answer: C Survival analysis of time to discharge

Survival analysis is the family of methods for time-to-event data. The event need not be death: discharge, relapse, first prescription or dropout all qualify. Its defining strength is censoring: a person who has not yet had the event when observation stops still contributes the time they were watched, instead of being discarded or wrongly treated as having had the event. It also makes no assumption that the times are normally distributed.

Length of stay is a textbook case. The distribution is strongly skewed by a few very long admissions, and on any date the data are collected some patients are still in, with an unknown final length of stay. A Kaplan-Meier curve of 'still admitted' against days, compared between wards with a log-rank test, uses every patient correctly. The t test, ANOVA and Mann-Whitney all need a complete number for every patient and cannot cope with the censored ones.

The exam flags the answer with words such as 'still in hospital', 'some had not yet been discharged', or 'followed for varying periods'. Those phrases mean censoring, and censoring means survival methods.

Rule: Time to an event with some people still waiting for it (censored) = survival analysis.
Trap: Reaching for a non-parametric test because the data are skewed, forgetting the censored patients.
Patients admitted on different days: five have been discharged (events) but three are still on the ward on the census day; survival analysis keeps the days they have already stayed instead of discarding them or pretending they were discharged.

Challenge 18 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.17
A trial of an adjunctive anti-inflammatory for treatment-resistant depression reports the relative risk reduction in non-response with a 95% confidence interval. For the result to be statistically significant at the 5% level, the interval must not include which value?
  1. 1
  2. 0
  3. 100%
  4. 0.05
  5. Infinity
Show the working
Answer: B 0

Every effect measure has a value that means no effect, called the null value. A confidence interval that includes the null value is consistent with no effect, so the result is not significant at the matching level. The null value depends on the measure: for a ratio the arms are equal when the ratio is 1; for a difference or a reduction the arms are equal when the difference is 0.

Relative risk reduction is 1 minus the relative risk. If the relative risk is 1 (no effect), the RRR is 0. So the 95% interval for an RRR must exclude 0 to be significant at 5%.

Challenge 19 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.14
A trial protocol states that the primary analysis will use the conventional significance level. What value of alpha does this imply?
  1. 0.01
  2. 0.05
  3. 0.10
  4. 0.20
  5. 0.80
Show the working
Answer: B 0.05

Alpha is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true, the false positive rate the investigator is willing to accept. Setting it in advance defines the threshold that a p value must fall below to be called statistically significant.
