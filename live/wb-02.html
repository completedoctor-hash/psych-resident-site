13.2.2Patterns of occurrence: endemic, epidemic and pandemic

Big idea. Endemic, hyperendemic and sporadic describe how steadily a disease sits in a population, while epidemic and pandemic describe an excess over what was expected, so every one of these words depends on knowing the usual level.
Picture it as a weather forecaster who knows the normal rainfall.

Syllabus: Descriptive epidemiology: patterns of disease occurrence in time and place

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 32 · Incidence, prevalence, ratios and rates →

The story in plain words. A weather forecaster can only call a month unusually wet because she knows what normal rainfall looks like. Epidemiologists are in the same position. Before anyone can say a disease is rising, someone has to know its usual level in that place at that time of year. Endemic means a disease is constantly present at its usual, expected level. That level can be low or high: what defines it is stability. Hyperendemic means persistently high, affecting all age groups. Sporadic means irregular, scattered cases with no obvious link between them. In the figure, Districts A and B are both stable; only their levels differ. Epidemic means occurrence clearly in excess of what is expected, as in District C, which climbs from about 8 cases a week to 52 in week 11. An outbreak is a localised epidemic, and a pandemic is an epidemic spread across several countries or continents. ((? A disease sits at a high but unchanging level in one region. Is it epidemic? ?)) No. Steady presence, however high, is endemic or hyperendemic; epidemic needs an excess over the expected.

The people and the evidence. John Snow's work on cholera in Soho in 1854 is the founding story. He mapped deaths around the Broad Street pump and showed that an excess of cases clustered around one water source. The method was descriptive, person, place and time laid out carefully enough that a cause suggested itself. The definitions used in the exam come from Porta's Dictionary of Epidemiology. None of the words carries a fixed threshold: a handful of cases of a disease long absent from an area can count as an epidemic, because the expected number was close to 0. The word is about excess, not size.

From everyday life to the clinic. Psychiatry meets these words more often than it seems. The COVID-19 pandemic brought waves of anxiety, bereavement and service pressure. Endemic infections such as neurocysticercosis in parts of Latin America present with seizures and neuropsychiatric symptoms, so where a patient grew up belongs in the differential. Clusters of suicide in a school or a small community are sometimes handled like an outbreak, with a response group, surveillance and care over media reporting. Here too, calling something a cluster depends on knowing how many deaths would normally be expected in that place and time.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is equating endemic with low. A stem describing a high, stable level is pointing at endemic or hyperendemic, not epidemic. Look for the words steady, constant or usual. The second trap is scale. A localised rise on one ward or in one town is an outbreak; the same pattern across continents is a pandemic. Sporadic is the odd one out: it describes irregular, unconnected cases with no excess at all.

13.2.2.3.1Incidence, prevalence and the bath

Big idea. Incidence counts people becoming ill and prevalence counts people who are ill, and duration is the bridge between them: P = I x D.
Picture it as a bath with the tap running and the plug half in.

Syllabus: Incidence and prevalence (13.2.2.3.1 and 13.2.2.3.2): measures, denominators and P = I x D

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 30 · Incidence, prevalence, ratios and rates →

The story in plain words. Picture a bath. The tap is incidence: the rate at which new cases appear among people who were well. The water in the bath is prevalence: everybody who has the condition at a given moment. The plughole is recovery, death and moving away. In steady state P = I x D, where D is the average duration. If obsessive-compulsive disorder has an incidence of 15 per 100,000 per year and a prevalence of 90 per 100,000, then D = P / I = 6 years. Mortality does not appear in the formula; it is already one of the ways water leaves the bath. Incidence comes in two forms. Cumulative incidence is new cases divided by the people at risk at the start, over a stated period. The incidence rate divides by person-time at risk, so people followed for different lengths of time each count only the years they contributed. ((? A cohort of 1,500 includes 300 who already have the condition. What is the denominator for incidence? ?)) 1,200. People who are already ill cannot become new cases.

The people and the evidence. Kessler and colleagues (2005), in the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication, found a lifetime prevalence of any DSM-IV disorder of about 46% and a projected lifetime risk to age 75 of about 51%. The gap is the point: lifetime prevalence counts survivors part-way through the age of risk, while lifetime morbid risk corrects for those not yet through it and for those who have died. Cantor-Graae and Selten (2005) pooled studies of schizophrenia in migrants and found a relative risk of about 2.7 in first-generation migrants. Studies of this kind count new cases, so they are incidence evidence. When a group's prevalence ratio is smaller than its incidence ratio, P = I x D says its duration in that place must be shorter, through recovery, death or return migration.

From everyday life to the clinic. Service planners need prevalence: how many people need a bed, a care coordinator or a clozapine clinic today. Researchers chasing causes need incidence, because only new cases show what changed before onset. A single cross-sectional survey can give the first, never the second. Prevalence comes in windows. Point prevalence is cases on one day, period prevalence is cases at any time across a period such as 12 months, and lifetime prevalence is anyone ever affected among those alive. In an outbreak, cumulative incidence over a few days is called the attack rate: 60 cases among 1,200 people who ate from a buffet is 5%.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The first trap is the incidence denominator: remove anyone who already had the condition at baseline. The second is the formula: D = P / I, and a mortality figure in the stem is a distractor. The third is the direction of change. A cure shortens duration, so prevalence falls while incidence is untouched. A treatment that keeps people alive without curing them raises prevalence. Only primary prevention lowers incidence. Rare and rapidly fatal gives low incidence and low prevalence.

13.2.1.3Mortality statistics and standardisation

Big idea. A rate removes the effect of population size but not of who lives there, so comparing areas or groups fairly means standardising for age.
Picture it as two running clubs, one full of teenagers and one full of pensioners.

Syllabus: Mortality statistics: crude, specific and age-adjusted death rates, and the standardised mortality ratio (13.2.1.3.1 to 13.2.1.3.6)

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 37 · Mortality statistics and standardisation →

The story in plain words. Compare two towns by deaths per 1,000 and the seaside town full of retirement flats will lose every time. That is not because it is unhealthy; it is because older people die more often. The crude death rate, deaths divided by the mid-year population x 1,000, is honest about the total but blind to age. In the figure, Seaview's crude rate is 21.7 per 1,000 and Newfield's is 7.2. Yet Newfield's age-specific rates are higher in every band. Half of Seaview is aged 65 or over; a tenth of Newfield is. Age is confounding the comparison. Direct standardisation applies each town's own age-specific rates to one standard population. Done that way, Seaview falls to 12.0 and Newfield rises to 13.7: the order reverses. Indirect standardisation works the other way round, applying standard rates to the study group to get expected deaths, and gives the standardised mortality ratio, observed / expected x 100. ((? Thirty-six deaths are observed and twelve expected. What is the SMR? ?)) 300: three times the mortality expected for people of that age and sex.

The people and the evidence. The SMR is the language of the mortality gap in psychiatry. Arcelus and colleagues (2011) pooled 36 studies of eating disorders and found an SMR of about 5.9 for anorexia nervosa. Cohorts like these are small, with too few deaths per age band for stable rates, which is exactly when indirect standardisation is the right tool. Hayes and colleagues (2017), using UK primary care records from 2000 to 2014, showed that the excess mortality of people with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder had not narrowed over the period and, if anything, had widened. Much of the excess comes from physical illness rather than suicide, which is why physical health monitoring is part of psychiatric care.

From everyday life to the clinic. Early-life measures have their own denominators. Perinatal mortality is stillbirths plus deaths in the first week, per 1,000 total births, because stillbirths are in the numerator. Infant mortality is deaths under one year per 1,000 live births. Cause-specific mortality counts deaths from one cause in a defined population; case fatality counts deaths among people who have the disease. Before choosing a method, look at the people. A population pyramid shows age and sex structure as back-to-back bars and tells you at a glance whether a crude comparison is safe. Deprivation shapes mortality too: the Jarman score of the 1980s weighted census items by GPs' views of workload, and England now uses the Index of Multiple Deprivation.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Rates already correct for population size, so a bigger town does not have a higher crude rate merely by being bigger. What distorts a crude comparison is structure: age above all, then sex and deprivation. Remember the direction of each method: direct uses your rates on standard people; indirect uses standard rates on your people. Choose indirect when numbers are small. An SMR of 100 means deaths as expected, and an SMR is a ratio, not a count of deaths.

13.2.1Prevention and the pathway to care

Big idea. Prevention is classified by when it acts in the life of an illness, and most specialist psychiatry happens after onset, at the tertiary level, behind a series of filters that decide who reaches care.
Picture it as a series of sieves between the street and the ward.

Syllabus: Basic epidemiology: levels of prevention and the pathway to psychiatric care

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 102 · Screening criteria and bias →

The story in plain words. Goldberg and Huxley (1980) described the route from illness in the community to a psychiatric bed as five levels separated by four filters. Each filter is a sieve: some people pass through, others are held back. In their 1992 update, of roughly 260 to 315 adults per 1,000 a year with a mental disorder in the community, about 6 reach an inpatient bed. Filter 1 is the person's decision to consult, which is illness behaviour. Filter 2 is the GP's recognition of the disorder, and it shows the biggest absolute drop: 230 per 1,000 attenders have a disorder, but only about 101.5 are recognised. Filter 3 is referral to mental health services and filter 4 is admission. Caplan (1964) gave psychiatry its three levels of prevention. Primary prevention stops a disorder starting. Secondary prevention detects and treats it early, to change its course. Tertiary prevention limits relapse, complications and disability once it is established. ((? Where on the pathway does most specialist psychiatric work fall? ?)) At levels 4 and 5, with established disorders, so most of it is tertiary prevention.

The people and the evidence. Early intervention in psychosis services are the clearest modern example of secondary prevention: rapid assessment and treatment to shorten the duration of untreated psychosis. NICE (2014) recommends that they are available to people of any age with a first episode. A second classification, from Mrazek and Haggerty (1994) for the US Institute of Medicine, sorts preventive work by target group rather than timing: universal for everybody, selective for groups at raised risk and indicated for individuals with early signs. It sits alongside Caplan's levels; it does not replace them.

From everyday life to the clinic. Restricting access to means of suicide, anti-bullying programmes and vaccinating staff against a virus that triggers post-viral depression are all primary prevention: they act before onset. Screening a high-risk group for depression is secondary. Individual Placement and Support, which helps people with schizophrenia find and keep paid work, is tertiary prevention. So are relapse prevention in recurrent depression, day centres that reduce withdrawal and physical health monitoring on clozapine. Most of a community team's caseload sits here.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is calling any treatment secondary prevention. Ongoing care in an established, chronic illness is tertiary. Caplan did count prompt treatment that shortens an early episode as secondary, which is why timing, not the word treatment, decides. Secondary is about early: catching the disorder near onset, when its course can still be changed. On Goldberg and Huxley, the first filter is the patient's decision to consult, not GP detection; GP recognition is the second. Do not mix Caplan's levels with universal, selective and indicated, which name target groups.

3.2.1Types of data

Big idea. What you are allowed to do to a number depends entirely on what the number was standing for in the first place.
Picture it as the labels on a ward drug cupboard.

Syllabus: Types of data: categorical (nominal, ordinal, dichotomous) and continuous

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 13 · Types of data and scales →

The story in plain words. Picture the drug cupboard on your ward. Some labels only name what is inside, and nothing follows from the order they happen to sit in. That is nominal data: named groups with no order, like diagnosis, blood group or sex. When a label has only 2 states, present or absent, it is dichotomous, and relapse yes or no is the example the exam reaches for. Other labels rank the shelves without telling you how far apart they are. Mild, moderate and severe sit in order, but the gap from mild to moderate need not be the gap from moderate to severe. That is ordinal data, and a single Likert item is ordinal however numerical it looks. ((? If severity is coded 1, 2 and 3, is the average severity meaningful? ?)) It is not, because the steps between the codes are not equal, so the arithmetic has nothing real underneath it. Then come the measures rather than the labels. Continuous data take any value in a range: weight, blood pressure, duration of illness. Within those, an interval scale has equal steps but an arbitrary zero, like temperature in Celsius, where 0 degrees does not mean no temperature. A ratio scale has equal steps and a true zero, like weight, age or duration, which is why doubling means something. Discrete data are counts, such as the number of admissions. They are whole numbers and not continuous, yet they are analysed as numbers because the steps between them are real and equal.

The people and the evidence. Stevens (1946) set out the four scales of measurement, nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio, and with them the argument that has governed statistics ever since: each scale permits only certain operations, and the scale comes before the arithmetic, not after it. Altman (1991) and Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) carried that logic into medical research. Their point for clinicians is practical rather than philosophical. Deciding the type of your variable is the first act of an analysis, and every later choice, summary, graph and test, follows from it automatically.

From everyday life to the clinic. Back at the cupboard, mislabelling costs you. The data type decides the summary measure: mode for nominal, median for ordinal or skewed, mean for symmetrical continuous. It decides the graph: bar or pie for categories, histogram or box plot for continuous. It decides the test: chi-square for categories, rank tests for ordinal, t tests for normal continuous. So an audit that records severity as 1, 2, 3 and then reports a mean severity of 2.4 has gone wrong at the first step, and nothing downstream can rescue it. ((? Which variable is the one the investigator sets? ?)) The independent variable, the exposure or the intervention. The dependent variable is the outcome you measure.

Name tags, a ladder, a ruler: each step up adds one thing you are allowed to do with the numbers (count, rank, then add and average)

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is coding. Writing a category as 0, 1, 2 does not make it numerical. Look at what the numbers stand for, not at how they are typed. The second is the single Likert item, which is ordinal however numerical it looks; several Likert items summed are commonly treated as continuous, and the exam will show you one item, not a scale. The third is the mirror image: discrete counts look like categories because they are whole numbers, yet they are quantitative and are analysed as numbers. Admissions per month is a count, not a group.

3.2.2Summary measures

Big idea. Every summary of a group of patients answers two questions, where the middle sits and how spread out they are, and the shape of the data decides which answer you are allowed to give.
Picture it as a queue of patients lined up by height.

Syllabus: Summary measures: proportion, mean, median, mode, range, interquartile range, standard deviation

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 25 · Mean, median and mode →Evidence-Based Medicine, page 108 · Validity of measures →

The story in plain words. Line your patients up by height and walk along the queue. The mode is the height that appears most often, the commonest person in the line. The median is whoever is standing in the middle, with half the queue each side. The mean is what you would get if everyone pooled their height and shared it out equally. In a neat, symmetrical queue all three stand in roughly the same place. Skew pulls them apart. If a few very tall people join the end, the mean walks towards them while the median barely moves, because the median counts positions rather than sizes. ((? Which average is dragged by a handful of extreme values? ?)) The mean, which is exactly why income and length of stay are reported as medians. Spread is the second question. The range is simply the tallest minus the shortest, which makes it hostage to the two most unusual people in the queue. The interquartile range is the distance from the person a quarter of the way along to the person three quarters along, so it describes the middle 50% and ignores the ends. The standard deviation is the typical distance of a person from the mean. Its square, the variance, is in squared units, which is why nobody reports variance at the bedside. In a normal distribution about 68% of people lie within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two.

In a positively skewed distribution the mode sits at the peak, the median halves the area, and the mean is dragged towards the long tail

The people and the evidence. Altman and Bland (2005) drew the line that the exam loves: the standard deviation describes how spread out the patients are, while the standard error describes how precisely you have pinned down the mean. One is about people, the other about your estimate, and the standard error shrinks as the sample grows while the standard deviation does not. Bland and Altman (1996) set out why skewed data are often transformed, usually by taking logs, before a mean is reported at all. The transformation is an admission that the mean was the wrong summary for the raw numbers.

From everyday life to the clinic. Length of stay is the classic skewed variable on any ward: most people go home within a fortnight and a few stay for months. Report a mean and you describe nobody. Report a median with an interquartile range and you describe the ordinary patient and the ordinary variation around them. A proportion is the summary for a yes or no outcome, and it is simply the count divided by the total. ((? If adding a constant to every value changes the mean, what happens to the standard deviation? ?)) Nothing. Shifting the whole queue along the corridor moves the middle but does not change how spread out they are.

One mansion on an ordinary street: the mean price more than doubles, the median barely moves. Skewed data need the median

Where the exam tries to catch you. Above one standard deviation is 16%, not 32%. The 32% figure is both tails together, so halve it when the question asks about one side only. Variance is in squared units and the standard deviation is what you report. Adding a constant moves the mean and leaves the spread alone; multiplying changes both. And the mode is the only one of the three averages available for nominal data, because you cannot rank or add a diagnosis.
