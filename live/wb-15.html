13.5.8Validity of a measure

Big idea. Validity asks whether a scale measures the thing it claims to measure, and each type of validity collects a different kind of evidence for that claim.
Picture it as a bathroom scale that must weigh you, not measure your height.

Syllabus: Validity: logical (face, content), construct (convergent, divergent), criterion (concurrent, predictive)

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 108 · Validity of measures →

The story in plain words. A bathroom scale can be beautifully consistent and still useless if it is really measuring how hard you lean. Validity is the question of whether it measures weight at all. Face validity asks whether the items look right to the people using the scale. Content validity asks whether they cover every part of the concept. Construct validity asks whether scores behave as the theory predicts: convergent when they agree with measures of the same idea, divergent when they do not agree with measures of different ideas. Criterion validity compares the scale with an outside yardstick: concurrent against a gold standard taken at the same time, predictive against an outcome later. ((? A short form drops items that look central to the concept. Which validity suffers? ?)) Face validity, even if construct validity improves.

The family of validity in the 2027 syllabus, each with the question it asks. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

The people and the evidence. Cronbach and Meehl (1955) set out construct validity for psychological tests, where no physical gold standard exists. Campbell and Fiske (1959) then showed how to test it with a matrix of traits and methods, the origin of convergent and discriminant validity. Modern scale builders use item response models such as the Rasch model to check that every item measures one underlying dimension. Items that misfit, or behave differently for men and women, are dropped.

From everyday life to the clinic. Imagine a team shortening a 16-item resilience questionnaire for students to 9 items. Removing misfitting items sharpens construct validity and makes the form quicker to complete. If some removed items were the ones students would recognise as resilience, face validity falls, and that is why the authors might keep the long form for some purposes. A summed questionnaire score is at best an interval measure: equal steps, no true zero. A student scoring 30 is not twice as resilient as one scoring 15.

Validity asks whether the instrument measures the right thing. A scale that reads how hard you lean is consistent but not valid for weight. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Keep the targets straight: a scale's purpose is defined by who it is for. A well-being scale for the general population is not a screen for mental disorder. Do not confuse validity with reliability. Stability over two weeks is test-retest reliability, not validity, and dropping items for misfit is a validity improvement, not a reliability one.

13.5.9Reliability of a measure

Big idea. Reliability is consistency: a measure that gives the same answer when nothing has changed, across time, raters, versions or halves of itself.
Picture it as a tape measure that reads the same every time, even if it was cut short at the factory.

Syllabus: Reliability: test-retest, inter-rater, parallel form, split half

Read more in your textbookEvidence-Based Medicine, page 113 · Reliability: kappa, ICC, Bland-Altman →

The story in plain words. A tape measure that was cut short at the factory gives the same wrong answer every time. It is reliable but not valid. Reliability is necessary for validity, but not sufficient. There are four ways to ask whether you would get the same answer again. Test-retest: the same people, the same test, two occasions. Inter-rater: two raters scoring the same people. Parallel form: two equivalent versions of the test. Split half: one sitting, the items divided into two halves and compared. Continuous scores are compared with the intraclass correlation coefficient, which checks agreement with the line of identity rather than mere correlation. ((? Two raters agree on a yes or no diagnosis. Which statistic? ?)) Cohen's kappa, agreement beyond chance.

Test-retest reliability: scores from two sittings two weeks apart cluster around the line of identity; one student, unwell on the second day, falls well below it. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

The people and the evidence. Jacob Cohen (1960) gave us kappa for agreement between raters on categories, correcting for the agreement expected by chance. Cronbach (1951) gave us coefficient alpha, the average of all possible split halves, now the standard measure of internal consistency. Bland and Altman (1986) warned that a high correlation can hide poor agreement, because two methods can rise together while one reads systematically higher. Their plot of differences against means is the cure.

From everyday life to the clinic. Before a well-being questionnaire is used to track students term by term, someone must show that a student whose life has not changed scores much the same two weeks later. That is test-retest reliability, and the interval must be long enough to forget the answers but short enough that real change is unlikely. Random error, such as a student unwell on the retest day, lowers reliability; it does not bias every score in one direction.

Four dartboards: a tight cluster is reliable, a cluster centred on the bull is valid. Illustrative data drawn for CDA.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The exam pairs each scenario with its type: stability over time is test-retest; two clinicians is inter-rater; two versions is parallel form; one sitting split in two is split half. Do not let a correlation coefficient stand in for agreement, and do not call a reliable measure valid without further evidence.

13.5.10Appraising a diagnostic accuracy study

Big idea. A diagnostic accuracy study is only as good as its comparison: the right patients, every one given both the new test and a credible reference standard, each read without knowledge of the other.
Picture it as a blind taste test run fairly: real customers, both plates for everyone, judges who cannot see the labels.

Syllabus: Critically appraise diagnostic studies

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 96 · Power, sample size and precision →Statistics, page 66 · Hypothesis testing, p values, type I/II errors →

The story in plain words. A fair taste test needs ordinary customers, both plates for everyone, and tasters who do not know which is which. Change any one of those and the winner is decided by the method, not the food. The design is a cross-sectional comparison: a consecutive or random sample of the people in whom the test would really be used, each given the index test and the reference standard, read blind and independently. The output is a 2 x 2 table. Each departure has a name. Spectrum bias: comparing severe cases with healthy volunteers. Partial verification bias: only positives get the reference. Incorporation bias: the index test forms part of the reference. Review bias: one result read knowing the other. ((? The most important question in appraising such a study? ?)) Were the test and the reference standard applied independently, to everyone?

The design of a fair diagnostic accuracy study, with four ways the design can mislead. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

The people and the evidence. Ransohoff and Feinstein (1978) showed how tests that shone in severe cases failed in the wider spectrum of real patients. Lijmer and colleagues (1999) measured it: case-control designs overstated accuracy roughly threefold. The response was a pair of tools: STARD 2015 for reporting such studies, and QUADAS-2 (2011) for judging their risk of bias in systematic reviews.

From everyday life to the clinic. To test whether a structural MRI rating identifies Alzheimer's disease, use brain-bank neuropathology as the reference, and read the scans without knowing the diagnosis. If the radiologist knows, the agreement will be flattering. Ask also whether the patients resemble yours. A screen validated in a specialist alcohol unit may not perform the same in a general hospital.

A fair blind taste test mirrors a fair diagnostic accuracy study. Teaching illustration, The Complete Doctor Academy.

Where the exam tries to catch you. Distractors include the journal's impact factor and the researcher's reputation. Neither tells you whether the comparison was fair. The design is cross-sectional, not a cohort or a before-and-after study, because test and reference are applied at the same point in time.

13.8.1Populations and sampling

Big idea. A sample is only as good as the way it was drawn: random selection protects against bias, size protects against chance, and the list you draw from decides who could ever be chosen.
Picture it as tasting soup with one spoon after stirring the pot.

Syllabus: Difference between sample and population, and sampling techniques in case-control, cohort and trial designs (13.8.1, 13.2.3.1.1, 13.2.3.2.2, 13.3.1.2)

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 23 · Samples, populations and sampling →

The story in plain words. A cook tastes soup with one spoon and judges the whole pot. That works only if the pot has been stirred. Sampling is the same bargain: study a few, judge the many, and stir first. The target population is everyone the results are meant to apply to; the sampling frame is the actual list you draw from; the sample is who you end up with. Study everyone and it is a census, with no sampling at all. Probability methods give every person a known chance of selection. Simple random sampling gives each an equal and independent chance. Systematic sampling takes every kth name after a random start. Stratified sampling draws at random within every subgroup, and may oversample a small one if the analysis weights it back. Cluster sampling draws whole groups and takes everyone in them; multistage sampling draws groups, then people within them. In the figure one register of 60 people is sampled four ways. The systematic sample lands on the same row in every clinic, harmless here but biased if the list had a pattern repeating every 6 names. ((? How does a stratified sample differ from a cluster sample? ?)) A stratified sample takes some people from every group; a cluster sample takes every person from some groups.

The people and the evidence. The Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey, England's main source of prevalence data, shows the method at scale (McManus and colleagues, 2016). Postcode sectors are chosen at random with stratification, then addresses within them, then one adult per household: a stratified multistage probability design that lets a few thousand interviews speak for the country. Non-probability methods have their place. Convenience sampling takes whoever is easiest to reach. Quota sampling fills preset numbers in each group, but the interviewer chooses who. Snowball sampling asks participants to recruit peers, often the only way into hidden groups such as people who use drugs and avoid services. All of them risk selection bias.

From everyday life to the clinic. In a case-control study the key sampling question is whether controls come from the same population that produced the cases. In a cohort it is whether the exposed and unexposed are drawn the same way. In a trial, eligibility criteria and recruitment sites define whom the results can describe. A trial recruiting from three London early intervention services wants to speak about all of England. The gap between who was sampled and the target population is a question of external validity, and a large convenience sample does not close it: size cuts random error, not bias.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is the list: the register you sample from is the sampling frame, not the population. The second is quota against stratified: both set numbers per group, but only stratified sampling chooses at random within the groups. The third is cluster against multistage: if everyone in a chosen group is studied it is cluster; if people are then sampled within it, multistage. And replacement means putting a name back so it can be drawn again, not swapping in someone for a non-responder.

13.5.8Validity: measuring the right thing

Big idea. Validity asks whether a score reflects the thing it claims to measure, and the type of validity depends on what you compare it with: judgement, theory or a gold standard.
Picture it as a bathroom scale that might be weighing your shoes.

Syllabus: Validity: logical (face, content), construct (convergent, divergent) and criterion (concurrent, predictive)

The story in plain words. A new rating scale for mania produces neat numbers. Validity asks whether those numbers reflect mania, or something nearby such as agitation or irritability. The syllabus groups the answers into three families, set out in the figure. Logical validity is judgement, not statistics. Face validity is whether the items look right at a glance, to patients or clinicians. Content validity is whether experts confirm that every relevant domain is covered, for example every DSM-5 criterion domain for borderline personality disorder. Criterion validity compares the measure with a gold standard. If the gold standard is measured at the same time it is concurrent validity; if the outcome comes later it is predictive validity. Construct validity is used when there is no gold standard: the measure should correlate with related measures, convergent validity, and not with unrelated ones, divergent validity. ((? A screening questionnaire and a structured clinical interview are completed on the same day. Which validity? ?)) Concurrent, because a gold standard is named and measured at the same time.

The people and the evidence. Cronbach and Meehl (1955) set out construct validity for abstract things such as anxiety that no single test can define. Campbell and Fiske (1959) then gave it a method, the multitrait-multimethod matrix: measures of the same trait should agree across methods, and measures of different traits should not. The PHQ-9 is a familiar example (Kroenke and colleagues, 2001). It was checked against diagnostic interviews by mental health professionals, which is criterion validity, and a score of 10 or more had a sensitivity and specificity of about 88% for major depression.

From everyday life to the clinic. On the ward, a negative symptom scale that correlates strongly with depression scores is a problem, not a success. Anhedonia and poverty of speech overlap with psychomotor retardation, so a good scale has to show it can tell them apart. That is divergent validity doing clinical work. A test used to select trainees should predict later performance, so predictive validity matters most. A brief screen in primary care should match a full interview done now, so concurrent validity matters most. The same correlation coefficient can carry either label: only the timing differs.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is a named gold standard. If the stem names one, the answer is criterion validity, concurrent or predictive, never convergent. Convergent validity compares with a related measure that is not a gold standard. The second trap is face against content: a casual impression is face validity, a systematic expert review of every domain is content validity. The third is homogeneity: items correlating with the total score show internal consistency, which supports, but does not prove, that one construct is being measured.

  2. Celentano DD, Szklo M (2019). Gordis Epidemiology, 6th ed. Elsevier.

13.5.9Reliability and agreement

Big idea. Reliability is consistency, and agreement between raters must be judged against how often they would agree by chance alone.
Picture it as two umpires calling the same match.

Syllabus: Reliability: test-retest, inter-rater, parallel form and split half, with kappa, weighted kappa and the intraclass correlation

The story in plain words. Two umpires watch the same match. If they make the same call every time, their decisions are reliable, though not necessarily right. Measurement has four versions of this. Test-retest gives the same test to the same stable people twice. Inter-rater has two raters score the same patients. Parallel forms uses two equivalent versions. Split-half correlates two halves of one test, a form of internal consistency. Raw agreement flatters raters, because some agreement happens by chance. In the figure two psychiatrists agree on thought disorder in 68 of 75 inpatients, so observed agreement Po is 0.907. From the marginal totals, chance alone would give Pe = 0.513. Cohen's kappa asks how much of the room above chance the raters actually used: (Po minus Pe) / (1 minus Pe) = 0.394 / 0.487 = 0.81. A kappa of 1 is perfect, 0 is chance and below 0 is worse than chance. ((? Two raters agree 90% of the time on a diagnosis present in 90% of patients. Is kappa necessarily high? ?)) No. Chance agreement is then very high too, so kappa can be modest despite impressive raw agreement.

The people and the evidence. Landis and Koch (1977) proposed the bands everyone quotes: up to 0.20 slight, 0.21 to 0.40 fair, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial and above 0.80 almost perfect. They are conventions, not laws, and kappa depends on prevalence, which Feinstein and Cicchetti (1990) described as a paradox of high agreement but low kappa. The DSM-5 field trials show why this matters (Regier and colleagues, 2013). Agreement between two clinicians interviewing the same patient gave a kappa of about 0.28 for major depressive disorder and 0.20 for generalised anxiety disorder: everyday diagnoses with only fair (depression) or slight (generalised anxiety) agreement on the Landis and Koch bands.

From everyday life to the clinic. Choose the statistic by the data. Two raters with nominal categories: Cohen's kappa. Ordered categories such as none, mild, moderate, severe: weighted kappa, which gives partial credit for near-misses. Continuous scores: the intraclass correlation coefficient or Bland-Altman limits of agreement. Internal consistency is measured with Cronbach's alpha, with about 0.7 or more usually acceptable and values over 0.9 hinting at redundant items. A trainee's self-rating against a supervisor's rating is still two raters on the same categories, so kappa applies.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is correlation offered as agreement. Pearson's r can be perfect while one rater scores every patient five points higher; an absolute-agreement ICC would catch that, r would not. The second is the kappa formula itself: agreement beyond chance over the maximum possible beyond chance, with Pe taken from the marginal totals, not from the agreement cells. The third is Cronbach's alpha confused with the significance level alpha.

13.5.10Precision, accuracy and error

Big idea. Random error scatters readings and costs precision and reliability; systematic error shifts them and costs accuracy and validity, and only the first shrinks with more measurements.
Picture it as darts thrown at a board.

Syllabus: Appraising measurement: precision and accuracy, random and systematic error, and how reliability relates to validity (13.5.8 and 13.5.9)

The story in plain words. Think of darts. A tight cluster in the bullseye is precise and accurate. A tight cluster in the top corner is precise but inaccurate. A loose spray centred on the bullseye is accurate on average but imprecise. A loose spray off to one side is neither. The figure shows the same four patterns from ward scales weighing a certified 70.0 kg weight. Scale W is tight and on target. Scale X reads about 4 kg heavy every time: systematic error, or bias. Scale Y averages exactly 70.0 kg but ranges over 6.5 kg: random error. Scale Z has both. Precision goes with reliability and is eroded by random error. Accuracy goes with validity and is eroded by systematic error. ((? Would averaging ten readings fix Scale X? ?)) No. Averaging shrinks random error, but every reading carries the same 4 kg bias, so only recalibration helps.

The people and the evidence. Bland and Altman (1986) showed why correlation cannot judge whether two methods agree. Two instruments can correlate almost perfectly while one reads consistently higher, so they proposed plotting the difference against the mean, with limits of agreement, which makes a systematic offset visible. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity. A clock that is always ten minutes fast is perfectly reliable and always wrong. A clock showing random times cannot be valid at all. Reliability therefore sets a ceiling on validity.

From everyday life to the clinic. Weight monitoring on antipsychotics shows the stakes. A single reading from Scale Y could be several kilograms out, enough to trigger or miss a change in treatment. Scale X would track change perfectly but misstate every body mass index. The same logic applies to whole studies. Internal validity asks whether the results are true for the people studied; applying an exclusion to one comparison group only, such as excluding substance misuse from home treatment but not from admission, biases the comparison. External validity asks whether the results travel to the target population.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is pairing reliability with systematic error. Random error threatens reliability; systematic error threatens validity. A narrow range means precision, not accuracy. The second is two unvalidated questionnaires correlating strongly. That shows they measure something in common, perhaps general distress, but proves neither is valid, reliable or interchangeable. Bigger samples and repeated measures cure random error, never bias.

  2. Bland JM, Altman DG (1997). Statistics notes: Cronbach's alpha. BMJ, 314: 572.
  3. Cohen J (1960). A coefficient of agreement for nominal scales. Educational and Psychological Measurement, 20: 37-46.
