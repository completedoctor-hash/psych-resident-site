3.2.13Sampling variability and the standard error

Big idea. Every sample gives a slightly different answer, and the standard error is the measure of how much that answer would wobble if you ran the study again.
Picture it as weighing the same sack on different scales.

Syllabus: Descriptive and inferential statistics, sampling variability, the standard error

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 21 · Samples, populations and sampling →

The story in plain words. Descriptive statistics summarise the patients in front of you and claim nothing beyond them. Inferential statistics use that sample to say something about the population it came from, while making an allowance for chance. That allowance is needed because every sample is slightly different. Take a hundred samples and you get a hundred means, scattered around the truth. That scatter is sampling variability, the spread of those means is the sampling distribution, and its standard deviation has its own name: the standard error. The arithmetic is short. The standard error of a mean is the standard deviation divided by the square root of n. An SD of 10 with n of 100 gives an SE of 1; the same SD with n of 25 gives 2. For a proportion it is the square root of p times 1 minus p, over n.

Quadrupling the sample halves the standard error (not quarters it): the spread of the data stays the same, only our precision about the mean improves

The people and the evidence. Altman and Bland (2005) wrote the paper that settles the confusion the exam keeps testing. The standard deviation describes how spread out the individuals are, and it does not shrink as you recruit more of them: people are as variable as they ever were. The standard error describes how precisely you have located the mean, and it does shrink. Because it falls with the square root of n rather than with n, quadrupling the sample only halves it, which is why large trials are expensive and the returns diminish.

From everyday life to the clinic. This is the difference between describing your patients and estimating a truth. If you want to say how much weight patients on a drug vary, report the standard deviation. If you want to say how confident you are in the average gain, report the standard error, or better, the confidence interval built from it. There is a limit worth holding onto. ((? Does a bigger sample reduce bias? ?)) No. More patients reduce random error only. A biased sampling method measured very precisely gives you a precisely wrong answer.

Throw darts at the true population mean: each dart is the mean of one sample. Bigger samples cluster tighter. The standard error is the spread of those darts, and it halves each time the sample quadruples

Where the exam tries to catch you. The standard trap offers an SD when the question needs an SE, or asks what happens to each as n grows. Only the standard error falls. The second is the square root. Doubling the sample does not halve the standard error; quadrupling it does. The third is the quiet assumption that precision cures bias, which it never does.

  2. Gardner MJ, Altman DG (1986). Confidence intervals rather than P values. BMJ, 292: 746-750.

3.2.14Hypothesis testing and p values

Big idea. A p value answers only one narrow question, how surprising this data would be if nothing were really happening, and almost every misuse comes from asking it something else.
Picture it as a courtroom that assumes innocence.

Syllabus: Null and alternative hypotheses, p values, one and two tailed tests, multiple testing

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 64 · Hypothesis testing, p values, type I/II errors →

The story in plain words. The court assumes innocence and asks whether the evidence is too strange to square with it. Statistics does the same. The null hypothesis says there is no difference; the alternative hypothesis says there is. The test assumes the null is true and asks how surprising the observed data would be in that world. The p value is the probability of data at least as extreme as the data you got, if the null were true. When p falls below alpha, conventionally 0.05, the null is rejected and the result is called significant. Four things p is not, and the exam tests all of them. It is not the probability that the null is true. It is not the probability the result is due to chance. It is not a measure of how large or how important the effect is, because p depends on effect size and sample size together. ((? A trial reports p = 0.4. Has it shown there is no effect? ?)) No. It may simply have been too small to detect one.

The p value is the tail area beyond the observed statistic, measured under the assumption that nothing is happening: a two-tailed test keeps both tails, so z = 2.4 gives p = 0.016

The people and the evidence. Bland and Altman (1994) set out the two-tailed and one-tailed distinction. Two-tailed is the default, because a drug might help or harm. A one-tailed test looks in one direction only, needs fewer patients, must be specified before the data are seen, and its p value is half the two-tailed one. Bland and Altman (1995) dealt with multiple testing. Run 20 tests on data where nothing is happening and you have about a 64% chance of at least one p below 0.05. The Bonferroni correction divides alpha by the number of tests, and is conservative, especially when the outcomes are correlated.

From everyday life to the clinic. Altman and Bland (1995) gave the sentence worth remembering above all others here: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A non-significant result in a small trial is a shrug, not an acquittal. The practical defence when reading a paper is to look for how many outcomes were measured and how many were reported. A single significant finding among twenty measured outcomes is what you would expect from chance alone, and a pre-registered protocol is what tells you whether that happened.

A trial in court: the null hypothesis is the presumption of innocence, the p value is how surprising the evidence would be if that presumption were true, and a verdict of not guilty is not a verdict of innocent

Where the exam tries to catch you. The classic stem defines p wrongly in one of the four ways above and asks you to pick the right definition. Anchor on the words: given that the null is true. The second trap is the one-tailed test used after the fact, which halves the p value and manufactures significance. The third is the paper with many subgroup analyses and one positive result, presented as a finding rather than as the arithmetic of multiple testing.

3.2.18Type I and II errors, power and sample size

Big idea. There are exactly two ways for a study to be wrong, and power is the deliberate decision about how often you are willing to miss something real.
Picture it as a smoke alarm, again, but this time for trials.

Syllabus: Type I and type II errors, power, sample size calculation

Read more in your textbookResearch Methods, page 97 · Power, sample size and precision →Statistics, page 67 · Hypothesis testing, p values, type I/II errors →

The story in plain words. A type I error is a false alarm: rejecting a null hypothesis that was true. Its rate is alpha, conventionally 5%, and it is chosen by the investigator before anyone is recruited. A type II error is a missed fire: failing to reject a null that was false. Its rate is beta, conventionally 20%. Power is 1 minus beta, the probability of detecting a real effect of a stated size, so the convention is 80% power, sometimes 90%. The asymmetry matters. Alpha does not depend on the sample size, because you set it. Beta does, which is why small trials miss real effects and why power is the thing a sample size calculation is really about.

Alpha is chosen by the investigator and does not depend on the sample size. Beta does, which is why a small trial misses real effects.

The people and the evidence. Four things raise power. A larger sample. A larger true effect, which is why a placebo comparator yields more power than an active one. Lower variability, from homogeneous samples, reliable measures and fewer drop-outs. And a less strict alpha, or a one-tailed test where that is genuinely justified. One thing does not: investigators cannot raise power by deciding to look for a bigger effect. The true effect is whatever it is. Changing the effect size in the calculation changes the sample you need, not the power you will have.

From everyday life to the clinic. A sample size calculation is done before recruitment and needs four inputs: alpha, the power you want, the smallest clinically important difference, and the expected standard deviation or event rate. Omit the third and the calculation is arithmetic without a question. Post hoc power calculations add nothing that the confidence interval does not already say. ((? A well-established effect fails to replicate in a small study. Most likely explanation? ?)) A type II error. An underpowered trial that finds nothing has not shown there is nothing.

The two mistakes a smoke alarm can make are the two mistakes a trial can make: ringing for nothing (type I) and sleeping through a fire (type II). Power is the chance of catching a real fire

Where the exam tries to catch you. Type I and type II get swapped in half the stems that mention them. Hold the order: type I is the false positive, alpha, the alarm that sounds for burnt toast. The second trap is the claim that increasing the sample lowers alpha. It does not; alpha is set, and the sample affects beta. The third is a negative trial presented as proof of equivalence, when it was simply too small.

3.2.17Confidence intervals

Big idea. A confidence interval says the same thing as a p value and three other things besides, which is why it has replaced the p value everywhere that matters.
Picture it as a net thrown at a fish you cannot see.

Syllabus: Confidence intervals: construction, width, and reading significance from them

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 46 · Confidence intervals →

The story in plain words. A 95% confidence interval is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. For 90% it is 1.645, for 99% it is 2.58. It can be built around a mean, a proportion, a difference between means or a difference between proportions. The careful reading is this: over repeated studies, 95% of such intervals would contain the true population value. It is not the range in which 95% of individual patients lie, which is a reference range built from the standard deviation and is a quite different and much wider thing. Width is driven by two forces. More confidence makes the net wider, so 99% is wider than 95% and 90% narrower. A larger sample makes it narrower, in proportion to the square root of n. A 90% interval sits inside the 95% interval with the same centre.

Four intervals, one null line, four different conclusions: an interval that excludes the null is significant, but only its position against the clinically important difference tells you whether the effect matters

The people and the evidence. Gardner and Altman (1986) argued in the BMJ for intervals rather than p values, and medical journals changed their instructions to authors as a result. Their case was that an interval shows the size of the effect, its precision and its significance in a single line, while p gives only the last of the three. Reading significance off an interval is mechanical. For differences, including the absolute risk reduction, it is significant if the interval excludes 0. For ratios, relative risk, odds ratio and hazard ratio, it is significant if the interval excludes 1. For the NNT the null is infinity.

From everyday life to the clinic. The clinical value is in the two awkward cases. A just-significant interval may have a lower limit so close to the null that the smallest effect still compatible with the data is trivial. A non-significant interval may be so wide that it includes an effect you would very much want. So a wide interval crossing zero means inconclusive, not negative. ((? What can a confidence interval never tell you about? ?)) Bias. Intervals quantify random error only, and a perfectly precise estimate of a biased measurement is still wrong.

An opinion poll before a referendum: the same 52% means little from 10 people and a great deal from 10,000. The confidence interval is the honest width of what you know; only the big polls separate 52% from a coin toss at 50%

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is the null value. Differences use 0, ratios use 1, and the stem will mix them in one list. The second is width: candidates reach for 99% as the better interval when the question is about precision, but a wider interval is less precise, not more. The third is reading a confidence interval as a range for individual patients.

3.2.15Parametric and non-parametric methods

Big idea. A parametric test uses the actual numbers and a non-parametric test uses their running order, which is why one is more powerful and the other is harder to break.
Picture it as measuring the runners against ranking them.

Syllabus: Parametric assumptions, non-parametric alternatives, and when to use each

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 86 · Non-parametric tests →

The story in plain words. You can describe a race two ways: by everyone's time, or by who came first, second and third. Parametric tests use the times. They assume the data, or strictly the residuals, are roughly normally distributed, that the observations are independent, and that the groups have similar variances. Non-parametric tests use the finishing order. Because they work on ranks, outliers and the shape of the distribution stop mattering, which makes them the right choice for skewed data that will not transform, for ordinal data, and for samples too small to check normality in. The cost is power. When the parametric assumptions hold, the parametric test is more likely to find a real effect, because it is using more of the information. ((? What is the alternative to going non-parametric with skewed data? ?)) Transform it, usually by taking logs, and use the parametric test on the transformed values.

Parametric tests use the values and are more powerful when their assumptions hold. Non-parametric tests use ranks, so outliers and shape stop mattering, at the cost of some power.

The people and the evidence. Five pairs cover almost everything the exam asks. The unpaired t test pairs with Mann-Whitney U. The paired t test pairs with Wilcoxon signed-rank. One-way ANOVA pairs with Kruskal-Wallis. Repeated-measures ANOVA pairs with Friedman. Pearson's r pairs with Spearman's rho. Chi-square and McNemar sit outside the pairs, because they are distribution-free by their nature: counting categories makes no assumption about a distribution at all.

From everyday life to the clinic. Three questions choose the test and they are worth asking in this order. What type is the outcome, categorical, ordinal or continuous? How many groups, and are they independent or the same people measured twice? And are the data normal enough? Answer those three and the test picks itself. Candidates who try to memorise a list of twenty tests fail; candidates who learn three questions and five pairs do not.

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is the paired design dressed up as two groups. The same patients before and after is paired, and an unpaired test on it is simply wrong. The second is assuming non-parametric means safer. It is more robust and less powerful, and with genuinely normal data it wastes patients. The third is a single Likert item, which is ordinal, however tempting the arithmetic looks.

A 95% confidence interval gives the range of population effects compatible with the data. Rule: A confidence interval that spans both important harm and important benefit means the trial is inconclusive, not negative.
Trap: Reading the interval limits as percentages of patients who benefit or are harmed.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The exam tests this by offering confident negative conclusions drawn from p values alone.

Rule: For a negative trial, look at the benefit end of the interval: if it includes an important effect, the trial is inconclusive.

The interval crosses 1 (p = 0.31) but its lower end, 0.62, is a 38% reduction in non-remission: a worthwhile effect has not been excluded.

The 95% confidence interval for a mean is the sample mean plus or minus roughly 2 standard errors, and the standard error is SD / root n. Power curves for a two-group comparison at a two-sided 5% significance level, for two true effect sizes
The figure shows power curves for a two-arm trial. Using the curves, approximately how many patients per group are needed to have 80% power to detect a moderate effect (standardised difference 0.5), and what happens to the requirement if the true effect is small (0.3)?
  1. About 40 per group; the small effect needs about 80
  2. About 65 per group; the small effect needs about 180 per group for the same power
  3. About 125 per group; the small effect needs more than 200
  4. About 200 per group; the small effect needs fewer patients
  5. About 65 per group; the small effect needs about 65 as well because alpha is unchanged
Show the working
Answer: B About 65 per group; the small effect needs about 180 per group for the same power

Power is the probability that a trial of a given size will detect a true effect of a given size at the chosen significance level. For a comparison of two means the required number per group is approximately 2 x (z alpha + z beta) squared / d squared, where d is the standardised difference. For 80% power and two-sided alpha 0.05, (1.96 + 0.84) squared x 2 is about 15.7, so n per group is about 15.7 / d squared.

With d = 0.5 that gives about 63 per group, matching the point where the upper curve crosses 0.80 in the figure. With d = 0.3 it gives about 175 per group, and the lower curve indeed reaches 0.80 only between 160 and 180 per group. Halving the effect size from 0.5 to 0.3 is a factor of 1.67, and 1.67 squared is 2.8, so the sample must grow nearly threefold.

The exam point is the shape: power is an S-shaped function of n that climbs steeply at first and then flattens, so the last few percentage points of power are the most expensive to buy.

Rule: Power rises with sample size and with effect size; a smaller effect needs roughly (0.5 / 0.3) squared, about 2.8 times, more patients.
Trap: Reading power off the wrong curve, or assuming the relationship with n is linear.
