Challenge 1 · 13.2 Diagnostic accuracy · 3.2.8
Distribution of questionnaire scores in patients with and without a depressive disorder, with four candidate cut-offs (a score at or above the cut-off is a positive screen)
The figure shows the distribution of scores on a 9-item mood questionnaire in primary care attenders with and without a depressive disorder, with four candidate cut-offs A (8), B (10), C (12) and D (14). A score at or above the cut-off counts as a positive screen. If the clinic moves its cut-off from B to D, what happens to sensitivity and specificity?
  1. Both increase
  2. Both decrease
  3. Sensitivity increases and specificity decreases
  4. Sensitivity decreases and specificity increases
  5. Sensitivity decreases and specificity is unchanged
Show the working
Answer: D Sensitivity decreases and specificity increases

Sensitivity and specificity are linked by the cut-off: any single test can be made more sensitive only by accepting more false positives, and more specific only by accepting more false negatives. The overlap of the two distributions is what makes this trade-off unavoidable; only a better test (less overlap) improves both at once.

At B (score 10) the healthy curve (mean 6, SD 3) has about 9% of its area at or above the line, so specificity is about 91%, and the depressed curve (mean 15, SD 4) has about 89% at or above it. Moving to D (score 14): the healthy tail above 14 is under 1%, so specificity rises to about 99%, while only about 60% of cases score 14 or more, so sensitivity falls to about 60%.

The exam often words this as 'to reduce false positives the threshold is raised; what happens to the false negative rate?' Same answer in different clothes: false negatives rise.

Rule: Raising the threshold trades sensitivity for specificity.
Trap: Assuming a better specificity comes free.

Challenge 2 · 13.7 Correlation and regression · 3.2.20
Which of the following statements about the correlation coefficient is FALSE?
  1. A coefficient of -1 describes a perfect negative linear relationship
  2. A strong correlation between two variables is good evidence that one causes the other
  3. A p value can be calculated to test whether a correlation differs from zero
  4. Pearson's coefficient is inappropriate when the relationship is clearly curved
  5. Correlation and simple linear regression are closely related techniques
Show the working
Answer: B A strong correlation between two variables is good evidence that one causes the other

The correlation coefficient r runs from -1 to +1 and summarises how tightly two continuous variables follow a straight line. It says nothing about which variable drives the other, and nothing about whether a third variable produces the pattern.

Each of the other statements is a textbook property: perfect negative correlation at -1, a significance test based on n and r, the requirement of linearity for Pearson's r, and the close kinship between r and the simple regression slope. The only false claim is that a strong correlation supports causation.

Exam stems like to dress this up with a plausible pair (ice cream sales and drowning, shoe size and reading age in children) where a confounder explains everything.

Rule: Correlation measures association, never causation.
Trap: Treating a large coefficient as evidence of a causal link.

Challenge 4 · 13.3 Prevalence, incidence, survival and mortality · 3.2.11
A researcher compares the crude all-cause mortality rates of two coastal towns. Which of the following is least likely to affect the interpretation of that comparison?
  1. Age distribution of the two towns
  2. Sex distribution of the two towns
  3. Size of the two populations
  4. Ethnic composition of the two towns
  5. Socioeconomic profile of the two towns
Show the working
Answer: C Size of the two populations

A crude rate is deaths divided by population, so the size of the population is handled by the division. What the division does not handle is who those people are. If one town has more elderly residents, more men, a different ethnic mix or more deprivation, its crude rate will differ for reasons unrelated to the quality of its health care or environment.

The two coastal towns could have identical age-specific death rates yet very different crude rates if one is a retirement destination. Standardisation, direct or indirect, is the fix.

The exam asks for the factor that does not matter. Anything that describes the make-up of the population matters; the raw count does not.

Rule: A rate already divides by population size; what distorts crude comparisons is the composition of the populations.
Trap: Thinking a larger population makes a rate less comparable, when it only makes it more precise.

Answer: A Cut-off 3

The horizontal axis of an ROC curve is the false positive rate, which equals 1 minus specificity. Moving to the right means more false positives and therefore less specificity. The lowest cut-off score labels the most people positive, which captures nearly every case (high sensitivity) at the cost of flagging many well people (low specificity), so it always sits at the top right end of the curve.

The classic error is to read the axis as if it were specificity itself and pick the leftmost point. Always convert: specificity = 1 minus the x value.

Trap: Reading the x axis as specificity rather than 1 minus specificity.
At cut-off 3 the x value is 0.70, so 70 of 100 non-cases are wrongly flagged and only 30 are cleared: specificity 30%, the lowest on the curve.

Challenge 6 · 13.2 Diagnostic accuracy · 3.2.7
Fagan nomogram: pre-test probability 5%, positive likelihood ratio 15
About 5% of adolescents referred to a CAMHS neurodevelopmental clinic are later diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder. A structured parent questionnaire has a positive likelihood ratio of 15. The questionnaire is positive. Using the nomogram, which is the closest post-test probability?
  1. 5%
  2. 20%
  3. 44%
  4. 75%
  5. 95%
Show the working
Answer: C 44%

The impact of a test depends on where you start. A positive LR of 15 is strong evidence, but applied to a low pre-test probability it produces post-test odds that are still below 1, so the probability stays under 50%. This is Bayes theorem at work and is the reason screening positives in low-prevalence settings are so often false positives.

Pre-test probability 0.05 gives odds 0.05/0.95 = 0.0526. Multiply by 15: post-test odds = 0.79. Convert: 0.79/1.79 = 0.44, so the post-test probability is about 44%. On the nomogram the line from 5% through 15 lands between 40% and 50%. A second independent positive test would be needed to push it higher.

The exam here is testing whether you respect the low prior. Candidates who see LR 15 and jump to 75% or 95% have multiplied probabilities or skipped the conversion back from odds.

Rule: Even a large LR leaves the post-test probability modest when the pre-test probability is low.
Trap: Treating 5% as odds and reading 0.75 as 75%.
Starting from 5 in 100 (odds 1 to 19), an LR of 15 lifts the odds to 0.79 to 1, so even after a positive questionnaire only 44 in 100 have autism.

Source: Fagan TJ (1975). Nomogram for Bayes theorem. NEJM, 293: 257.

Challenge 7 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.18
When calculating the sample size for a trial, at what level is statistical power conventionally set?
  1. 5%
  2. 20%
  3. 50%
  4. 80%
  5. 99%
Show the working
Answer: D 80%

Power is 1 minus beta, the probability that a study detects a true effect of the size specified in the design. Sample size calculations need four inputs: the expected effect size, its variability, alpha and the desired power. Raising the target power raises the required sample size steeply.

By convention trials are powered at 80%, accepting a 20% chance of missing a true effect. That is four times the 5% alpha, which reflects a judgement that a false positive is the more serious mistake. Larger or more important trials often aim for 90%.

The exam offers alpha, beta and power as neighbouring options; label each number before you commit.

Rule: Power 80% (beta 20%) is the design convention; 90% is used for important or expensive trials.
Trap: Picking 20%, which is beta, not power.

Challenge 9 · 13.4 Measures of treatment impact · 3.2.12.2
The relative risk of completed suicide associated with alcohol dependence is 6, and the prevalence of alcohol dependence in the adult population is 4%. The proportion of suicides in the population attributable to alcohol dependence is approximately
  1. 0.17
  2. 0.20
  3. 0.04
  4. 0.24
  5. 0.83
Show the working
Answer: A 0.17

The population attributable fraction is the proportion of all cases in the population that would be prevented if the exposure were removed. It rises with both the strength of the association (relative risk) and the prevalence of the exposure. Levin's formula expresses it in terms of those two quantities: Pe(RR minus 1) divided by 1 plus Pe(RR minus 1).

With RR = 6 and Pe = 0.04: Pe(RR minus 1) = 0.04 x 5 = 0.20. PAF = 0.20/1.20 = 0.167, so about 17% of suicides in the population are attributable to alcohol dependence. Although the relative risk is large, the exposure is uncommon, which keeps the population fraction modest.

Contrast this with the attributable fraction among the exposed, (RR minus 1)/RR = 5/6 = 83%: most suicides in people with alcohol dependence are attributable to it, but most suicides in the population are not.

Rule: Population attributable fraction = Pe(RR - 1) / [1 + Pe(RR - 1)], where Pe is the prevalence of exposure.
Trap: Forgetting the denominator and reporting Pe(RR minus 1) on its own.
With 4% exposed and a relative risk of 6, every 120 suicides split 96 to 24 between non-dependent and dependent people; only 4 of the 24 would have happened anyway, so the 20 excess are 20/120 = 17% of all suicides.

Source: Rothman KJ (2012). Epidemiology: An Introduction, 2nd ed. Oxford University Press.

Challenge 12 · 13.5 Inference, errors, power and confidence intervals · 3.2.13
A trial randomises 150 patients to each arm; 30 relapse on usual care and 21 relapse on the new intervention. Suppose the trial were repeated with 1500 patients per arm and the event rates in each arm stayed exactly the same. What happens to the point estimate of the relative risk reduction?
  1. It stays the same
  2. It moves closer to zero
  3. It moves further from the true population value
  4. Its confidence interval becomes wider
  5. Its upper confidence limit is likely to become negative
Show the working
Answer: A It stays the same

A point estimate such as a relative risk reduction is calculated purely from the event rates in the two arms. Sample size enters only through the standard error, which governs how wide the confidence interval is. Multiply the sample by ten and the standard error shrinks by about the square root of ten, so the interval narrows to roughly a third of its width.

Here the control event rate is 30/150 = 20% and the experimental rate is 21/150 = 14% in the small trial; in the large trial they would be 300/1500 and 210/1500, still 20% and 14%. The relative risk is 0.70 and the RRR is 30% in both. Only the interval around the 30% changes.

The exam uses this to test whether you separate the estimate (what the data say) from its precision (how sure you can be).

Rule: Sample size changes precision (the width of the interval), not the point estimate, when the event rates are unchanged.
Trap: Expecting the estimate itself to move when only the sample size changes.
Same 20% and 14% event rates, same RRR of 30%: with 150 per arm the interval spans -17% to 58%, with 1500 per arm 18% to 40%, about a third of the width.

A receiver operating characteristic curve summarises a continuous or ordinal test across every possible threshold. Show the working
Answer: C Relative risk cannot be negative

Two measures summarise how exposure changes risk. Power is the probability that a trial detects a real effect of a given size.
