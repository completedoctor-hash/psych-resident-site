3.2.3Tabular presentations

Big idea. A table is not a way of storing numbers, it is a way of making one comparison obvious, and which comparison depends entirely on which direction you read it.
Picture it as a ward whiteboard ruled into four boxes.

Syllabus: Tabular presentations: 2x2 table, frequency table, frequency distribution

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 52 · Histograms, bar charts, pie charts →Statistics, page 13 · Types of data and scales →

The story in plain words. Rule the whiteboard into four boxes. Down the side goes what happened to the patient first, the exposure or the treatment. Across the top goes what happened afterwards, the outcome. That is a 2 x 2 table, and every measure of effect in Paper B is built from those four cells. Read across a row and you get a risk, the number with the outcome divided by everyone in that row. Read within the row, with against without, and you get an odds. The same four numbers give both, which is why a question can ask for either and why so many candidates give the wrong one. ((? In a 2 x 2 table, does a / (a + b) give the risk or the odds? ?)) The risk, because the denominator is everybody in that row. A frequency table is simpler: one column of categories, one column of counts, sometimes a third of percentages. A frequency distribution does the same for a continuous variable by first chopping it into intervals, which is the step that turns a list of weights into something a histogram can draw.

Risk in the exposed is a / (a + b), reading across the row. Odds in the exposed are a / b, reading within the row. The odds ratio is ad / bc.

The people and the evidence. Kirkwood and Sterne (2003) set out the convention the exam assumes: exposure down the side, outcome across the top, cells labelled a, b, c, d from the top left. Get the orientation wrong and ad / bc returns the reciprocal of the answer you wanted. Altman (1991) is the source of the other discipline a table needs, which is that row and column totals are shown. They are what let a reader check the arithmetic, and their absence is the commonest fault in a paper's tables.

From everyday life to the clinic. The whiteboard is how a real question is answered. Of 100 patients on the drug, how many relapsed; of 100 not on it, how many relapsed. Everything else, relative risk, odds ratio, number needed to treat, is arithmetic on those four boxes. The same habit protects you when reading. Before interpreting any effect size in a paper, find the four numbers that produced it. If the paper does not let you rebuild the table, you cannot check what it claims.

A 2 x 2 table is just two crowds sorted by what happened to them: a and b are the exposed, c and d the unexposed

Where the exam tries to catch you. The commonest trap is reading the table down the column rather than along the row, which quietly turns a risk into something that is neither risk nor odds. The second is forgetting that a frequency distribution needs intervals chosen before it exists, and that the choice of interval changes the shape of the histogram you then draw. The third is that percentages in a frequency table must state their denominator. A column that sums to more than 100% is telling you that patients could appear more than once.

3.2.4Graphical presentations

Big idea. The right graph makes the shape of the data obvious at a glance, and choosing it is not a matter of taste because the type of data has already decided it for you.
Picture it as choosing the right chart at the end of the bed.

Syllabus: Graphical presentations: bar chart, histogram, box plot, scatter plot

Read more in your textbookStatistics, page 52 · Histograms, bar charts, pie charts →Statistics, page 58 · Box plots and stem-and-leaf plots →

The story in plain words. A bar chart is for categories. The bars have gaps between them because the groups are separate things, and the gap is the point. A histogram is for continuous data cut into intervals, so the bars touch: the variable runs along without interruption underneath them. A box plot summarises a continuous variable in five numbers. The box runs from the first quartile to the third, the line inside it is the median, and the whiskers reach to the furthest values within 1.5 times the interquartile range. Anything beyond is drawn as a point and called an outlier. A scatter plot is for two continuous variables measured on the same person, one on each axis. It is the only one of the four that shows a relationship rather than a distribution, and it is what you look at before claiming any correlation. ((? Two groups' lengths of stay are to be compared on one picture. Which graph? ?)) Two box plots side by side, because the variable is continuous and skewed and the comparison is of middles and spreads.

Anatomy of a box plot: the box holds the middle 50% of values, the line is the median, whiskers reach the furthest values within 1.5 IQR, circles are outliers

The people and the evidence. Tukey (1977) invented the box plot for exactly this purpose, as part of what he called exploratory data analysis: look at the shape of the data before testing anything, because the shape tells you which test is allowed. Altman (1991) adds the rule that saves most published figures, which is that a bar chart of means with error bars hides the distribution entirely. Two groups with identical means and wildly different spreads draw the same bars.

From everyday life to the clinic. At the end of the bed the choice is quick. Diagnosis on the ward, a bar chart. Length of stay, a histogram if you want the shape and a box plot if you want to compare wards. Dose against response in the same patients, a scatter plot. The discipline worth keeping is to draw the picture before running anything. A histogram showing a long right tail is telling you the mean will mislead and a rank test is likely to be the honest one.

Pick the picture by the question you are answering: named groups, one spread, several groups, or two measurements per person

Where the exam tries to catch you. The box of a box plot holds 50% of the data, not 25%. That single fact is asked more often than anything else here. The whiskers are not the minimum and maximum: they stop at 1.5 times the interquartile range, and anything further out is an outlier by definition. Bars that touch mean a histogram and continuous data; bars with gaps mean a bar chart and categories. And a truncated y axis exaggerates a difference without stating a single untrue number, which is the commonest way a real paper misleads.

A pilot case-control study asks 12 young men with first-episode psychosis and 12 age-matched controls about regular cannabis use in the preceding year. The results are shown in the table. What is the ratio of the odds of cannabis exposure among cases to the odds of exposure among controls?
  1. 0.17
  2. 2.00
  3. 2.67
  4. 6.00
  5. 9.00
Show the working
Answer: D 6.00

Odds are the number with the exposure divided by the number without it, within one group. The odds ratio compares the odds of exposure between the case group and the control group. On a 2x2 table laid out with cases in the top row and exposure in the left column, this is the cross-product (top-left x bottom-right)/(top-right x bottom-left).

Odds of exposure in cases = 8/4 = 2. Odds in controls = 3/9 = 0.33. Odds ratio = 2/0.33 = 6. The cross-product gives the same: (8 x 9)/(4 x 3) = 72/12 = 6. Cases had six times the odds of regular cannabis use.

The risk ratio of exposure (8/12 over 3/12 = 2.67) is a common distractor. In a case-control study the proportions exposed depend on how many cases and controls the investigator chose to recruit, so only the odds ratio is a valid measure of association.

Rule: Odds ratio = (odds of exposure in cases) / (odds of exposure in controls) = ad/bc.
Trap: Comparing exposure proportions (a risk ratio) rather than exposure odds.
Odds of exposure are 8 to 4 (2.0) in cases and 3 to 9 (0.33) in controls: 2.0/0.33 = 6, the odds ratio.

Challenge 7 · 13.1 Describing data · 3.2.4
Processing speed index: normal curve with mean 50 and SD 10, 68% shaded between 40 and 60, and the score of 40 marked
A processing speed index is standardised with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 and is normally distributed in the population, as shown in the figure. Approximately what percentage of the population will score below 40?
  1. 2.5%
  2. 5%
  3. 16%
  4. 32%
  5. 34%
Show the working
Answer: C 16%

The normal distribution is fully described by its mean and standard deviation, and fixed proportions of the population sit within fixed numbers of standard deviations of the mean: about 68% within one, 95% within two and 99.7% within three. Because the curve is symmetrical, whatever lies outside a band is split equally between the two tails.

A score of 40 is exactly one standard deviation below the mean of 50, because (40 minus 50) / 10 gives a z score of minus 1. The proportion outside plus or minus 1 SD is 100% minus 68% = 32%, and half of that is in the lower tail, so 16% score below 40.

The exam varies the question by asking about 2 SD (2.5% per tail) or by asking for the proportion above a cut-off rather than below. Convert the raw score to a z score first and then apply the 68, 95, 99.7 rule.

Rule: Normal curve: 68% within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD, 99.7% within 3 SD; halve the remainder for one tail.
Trap: Reporting 32%, the two-tailed figure, when the question asks about one tail only.
Of 100 people, 68 score 40 to 60 and the remaining 32 split equally into the two tails, so 16 score below 40

Challenge 9 · 13.1 Describing data · 3.2.4
Weekly alcohol consumption in a community sample: a positively skewed distribution with mode, median and mean marked
The figure shows the distribution of weekly alcohol consumption in a community sample. Which one of the following statements about this distribution is correct?
  1. The mean is a better summary of the typical value than the median
  2. The mean lies to the right of the median
  3. The standard deviation is the ideal measure of spread
  4. There is a sharp high peak at the mean
  5. Values are equally likely to fall far above and far below the average
Show the working
Answer: B The mean lies to the right of the median

Skew describes asymmetry. In a positively (right) skewed distribution most values are low and a long tail stretches to the right. The mean follows the tail because extreme values contribute their full weight, so the order of the three averages becomes mode, then median, then mean, reading left to right. In negative skew the order reverses.

The alcohol distribution in the figure has its hump on the left and a long right tail, so the mean sits to the right of the median. That is why guidance usually quotes median units per week rather than the mean, and why the standard deviation would overstate the spread for the typical drinker.

A sharp, narrow peak is kurtosis, not skew; the exam likes to offer a kurtosis description as a distractor in a skew question.

Rule: Positive skew: tail to the right, mean pulled right of the median; summarise with median and IQR.
Trap: Reversing the order of mean and median in a positive skew.

Length of stay is a classic positively skewed variable: most people leave within a few weeks, a few stay for many months. Those long stays raise the mean well above the median, which is why length of stay is usually reported as a median.
