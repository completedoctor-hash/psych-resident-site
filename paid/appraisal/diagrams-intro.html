Aim and objectives

Aim. To recognise, read and interpret every diagram that appears in MRCPsych Paper B, so that no graph on the exam paper is unfamiliar.

At the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  1. Name each of the 34 Paper B diagrams from its picture and state in one sentence what it shows.
  2. Identify the line or point of no effect on forest plots, ROC curves, L'Abbé plots, confidence interval plots and the cost-effectiveness plane.
  3. Calculate PPV, NPV, likelihood ratios and post-test probability from a 2×2 table, a frequency tree or a Fagan nomogram.
  4. Read median survival, censoring and numbers at risk from a Kaplan-Meier curve.
  5. Distinguish common-cause from special-cause variation on run charts and SPC charts.
  6. Answer the 124 diagram questions in this lecture, scoring at least 80%, by the end of the lecture.

Evidence synthesis

Evidence synthesis · Diagram 1

Forest plot

What it is. A forest plot shows the result of each study in a meta-analysis, and the pooled result, on one chart.

  1. 1Line of no effect: OR = 1. A result crossing it is not statistically significant.
  2. 2Square: one study's point estimate. Bigger square = more weight (more precise, usually bigger study).
  3. 3Horizontal line: that study's 95% confidence interval.
  4. 4Diamond: the pooled result. Centre = pooled estimate, width = its 95% CI.
  5. 5Log scale on the x-axis: ratios of 0.5 and 2 sit the same distance from 1.
  6. 6Heterogeneity statistics (I², Q test): how much the studies disagree.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which study carries the most weight, and how can you tell?
Evans 2021 (32%): it has the largest square, because it is the most precise study.
QUESTION 2
Is the pooled result significant?
Yes. The diamond (0.63 to 0.87) does not touch the line of no effect at 1.
QUESTION 3
Which single studies are not significant on their own?
Brown 2016 and Davies 2019: their confidence intervals cross 1.
QUESTION 4
Why is the x-axis on a log scale?
Ratios are symmetrical on a log scale, so OR 0.5 and OR 2 are equally far from 1.
QUESTION 5
What does I² = 18% tell you?
Low heterogeneity: the studies broadly agree, so pooling is reasonable.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 2

Funnel plot

What it is. A funnel plot checks a meta-analysis for publication bias by plotting each study's effect against its precision.

  1. 1Vertical line: the pooled effect.
  2. 2Dashed funnel: where 95% of studies should fall if there is no bias; it widens as studies get smaller.
  3. 3Empty corner: small studies showing no benefit are missing — asymmetry suggests publication bias (or small-study effects).
  4. 4Small studies scatter widely at the bottom; large studies cluster at the top.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What shape do you expect if there is no bias?
A symmetrical inverted funnel: small studies scatter widely at the bottom, large ones cluster at the top.
QUESTION 2
What does an empty lower-right corner suggest?
Small studies showing no benefit or harm are missing — possible publication bias.
QUESTION 3
Name two other causes of asymmetry.
True heterogeneity and poorer methodological quality in small studies (small-study effects).
QUESTION 4
Which statistical test checks asymmetry?
Egger's regression test.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 3

L'Abbé plot

What it is. A L'Abbé plot shows each trial's event rate in the treatment group against the control group.

  1. 1Diagonal line of equality: treatment rate = control rate (no effect).
  2. 2Circle below the line: fewer events with treatment — benefit (when events are bad outcomes).
  3. 3Circle above or on the line: no benefit or harm.
  4. 4Spread of circles across the x-axis shows different baseline (control) risks between trials, a source of heterogeneity.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
A trial sits below the diagonal. What does it mean if events are relapses?
Fewer relapses with treatment — the treatment helped.
QUESTION 2
What does the diagonal represent?
No difference: treatment rate equals control rate.
QUESTION 3
Why does circle size matter?
Bigger circles are bigger trials and carry more weight.
QUESTION 4
What does a wide spread of points along the x-axis suggest?
Different baseline risks between trials — a source of clinical heterogeneity.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 4

Network meta-analysis plot

What it is. A network plot maps which treatments have been compared directly in trials, so many treatments can be ranked together.

  1. 1Node: one treatment; bigger node = more patients.
  2. 2Line between nodes: direct head-to-head trials; thicker = more trials.
  3. 3A closed loop (triangle) lets direct and indirect evidence be checked for consistency.
  4. 4Treatments not joined by a line are compared only indirectly through common comparators (transitivity assumption).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does a thick line between two nodes mean?
Many head-to-head trials directly compare those two treatments.
QUESTION 2
How are fluoxetine and venlafaxine compared if no line joins them?
Indirectly, through common comparators such as placebo.
QUESTION 3
What assumption allows indirect comparison?
Transitivity: the trials are similar enough in patients and methods to be compared.
QUESTION 4
What does a closed loop allow you to test?
Consistency between direct and indirect evidence.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 5

CONSORT flow diagram

What it is. The CONSORT diagram shows the flow of participants through a randomised controlled trial.

  1. 1Enrolment: how many were screened and why some were excluded (affects generalisability).
  2. 2Randomisation: the point at which groups should become comparable.
  3. 3Follow-up: losses in each arm — unequal or large losses (> 20%) threaten validity (attrition bias).
  4. 4Analysis: ITT analyses everyone in the arm they were randomised to.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many were randomised and how many analysed by ITT?
200 randomised; all 200 analysed by intention to treat.
QUESTION 2
Which arm lost more patients, and why does it matter?
The drug arm (12 vs 6). Unequal losses related to side effects can bias a per-protocol analysis (attrition bias).
QUESTION 3
Where would you look to judge generalisability?
The enrolment box: how many were excluded and why.
QUESTION 4
What should an ITT analysis do with the 12 lost patients?
Keep them in the drug group they were randomised to, handling missing data appropriately.
Evidence synthesis · Diagram 6

PRISMA flow diagram

What it is. The PRISMA diagram shows how studies were found, screened and selected for a systematic review.

  1. 1Identification: databases searched — several databases plus grey literature reduce publication bias.
  2. 2Each box to the right subtracts records; numbers must add up.
  3. 3Reasons for exclusion at full text must be given.
  4. 4Included: number of studies in the review and in the meta-analysis.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many records were screened?
930 (1,240 identified minus 310 duplicates).
QUESTION 2
Why search several databases and grey literature?
To find all relevant studies and reduce publication bias.
QUESTION 3
How many studies entered the meta-analysis?
14 of the 18 included studies.
QUESTION 4
What must be reported at the full-text stage?
The reasons each study was excluded.

Survival and risk

Survival and risk · Diagram 7

Kaplan-Meier survival curve

What it is. A Kaplan-Meier curve shows the proportion of people still free of an event over time.

  1. 1Each step down = one or more events (relapses) at that time.
  2. 2Small vertical tick = a censored patient (left the study or reached the end without the event).
  3. 3Median survival: read across from 0.5 to the curve, then down — here about 3.3 months for placebo.
  4. 4Numbers at risk: how many are still being followed; few at risk means the right-hand end is unreliable.
  5. 5The curves start at 1.0 (everyone event-free). The gap between curves is summarised by the hazard ratio; compare whole curves with the log-rank test.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the median time to relapse on placebo?
About 3.3 months: read across from 0.5 to the placebo curve, then down.
QUESTION 2
What does a small vertical tick on the curve mean?
A censored patient: lost to follow-up or reached the end without relapsing.
QUESTION 3
Why is the right-hand end of the curve less reliable?
Few patients remain at risk, so each event moves the curve a lot.
QUESTION 4
Which test compares the two whole curves?
The log-rank test.
QUESTION 5
What summarises the gap between curves?
The hazard ratio (from Cox regression), assuming proportional hazards.
Survival and risk · Diagram 8

Calibration plot

What it is. A calibration plot checks whether a risk prediction model's predicted risks match the risks actually observed.

  1. 1Diagonal: perfect calibration (predicted = observed).
  2. 2Points above the line: the model under-predicts risk at higher levels.
  3. 3Calibration (agreement) is different from discrimination (ranking, measured by the C-statistic/AUC).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does perfect calibration look like?
All points on the diagonal: predicted risk equals observed risk.
QUESTION 2
Points lie above the diagonal at high risk. What does it mean?
The model under-predicts risk for high-risk patients.
QUESTION 3
How is calibration different from discrimination?
Calibration is agreement in absolute risk; discrimination is ranking cases above non-cases (C-statistic).
QUESTION 4
Why validate a model in a new population?
Performance usually falls outside the data it was built on (overfitting).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis · Diagram 9

2×2 table and likelihood ratios

What it is. The 2×2 table compares a test result with the truth from a reference standard; every diagnostic statistic comes from it.

  1. 1Columns = truth (reference standard). Sensitivity and specificity read DOWN the columns.
  2. 2Rows = test result. Predictive values read ACROSS the rows.
  3. 3PPV and NPV change with prevalence; sensitivity, specificity and LRs do not.
  4. 4LR+ above 10 or LR− below 0.1 change probability a lot.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Calculate sensitivity.
a/(a+c) = 80/100 = 0.80.
QUESTION 2
Calculate LR+.
Sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity) = 0.80 ÷ 0.10 = 8.
QUESTION 3
Which statistics change with prevalence?
PPV and NPV. Sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios do not.
QUESTION 4
Which way do you read sensitivity and specificity?
Down the columns (the truth).
Diagnosis · Diagram 10

ROC curve

What it is. A ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every possible cut-off of a test.

  1. 1Top-left corner: perfect test (sensitivity 1, false positive rate 0).
  2. 2Each point is one cut-off; the point nearest top-left (highest Youden index = sens + spec − 1) is often chosen.
  3. 3Area under the curve (AUC): probability the test ranks a random case above a random non-case. 0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect.
  4. 4Diagonal: a useless test (AUC 0.5).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does AUC = 0.87 mean?
An 87% chance that a randomly chosen case scores higher than a randomly chosen non-case.
QUESTION 2
Moving to a lower cut-off moves the point which way?
Up and to the right: more sensitive, less specific.
QUESTION 3
What AUC means a useless test?
0.5 — the diagonal.
QUESTION 4
How is the 'best' cut-off often chosen?
The point nearest the top-left corner, or highest Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1).
Diagnosis · Diagram 11

Fagan nomogram

What it is. The Fagan nomogram turns a pre-test probability and a likelihood ratio into a post-test probability with a ruler.

  1. 1Left scale: pre-test probability (how likely before the test — the prevalence in your setting). Here 20%.
  2. 2Middle scale: likelihood ratio of the test result. Here LR+ = 8.
  3. 3Right scale: post-test probability. A straight line through the first two points lands here: about 67%.
  4. 4The nomogram does Bayes' theorem for you: post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Pre-test probability 20%, LR+ 8. What is the post-test probability?
About 67%: pre-test odds 0.25 × 8 = 2, so probability = 2/3.
QUESTION 2
What does an LR of 1 do?
Nothing: post-test probability equals pre-test probability.
QUESTION 3
Where does the pre-test probability come from?
The prevalence in your clinical setting, adjusted for the patient's features.
QUESTION 4
What formula does the nomogram apply?
Post-test odds = pre-test odds × likelihood ratio.
Diagnosis · Diagram 12

Leaf plot (probability-modifying plot)

What it is. A leaf plot shows how a positive or negative test result changes probability across every possible pre-test probability.

  1. 1Upper (green) curve: probability after a positive result.
  2. 2Lower (red) curve: probability after a negative result.
  3. 3Diagonal: no change — a test with LR = 1. The wider the 'leaf', the more useful the test.
  4. 4Read up from your pre-test probability (here 30%) to each curve: positive → 72%, negative → 6%.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Pre-test 30%: what are the post-test probabilities?
About 72% after a positive result and 6% after a negative result.
QUESTION 2
What does a wide leaf mean?
The test changes probability a lot — it is useful.
QUESTION 3
Where is a test least helpful?
At very low or very high pre-test probability, where the curves hug the diagonal at the ends.
QUESTION 4
Which curve uses LR+?
The upper (green) curve.
Diagnosis · Diagram 13

Frequency tree

What it is. A frequency tree splits a round number of people by prevalence, then by test result, to show true and false results.

  1. 1Start with a round number (1,000) — natural frequencies are easier than percentages.
  2. 2First split by disease using the prevalence.
  3. 3Then split the diseased by sensitivity (true positives vs false negatives).
  4. 4Split the healthy by specificity (true negatives vs false positives).
  5. 5Predictive values come from reading across the positive (or negative) boxes: PPV = TP / all positives.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Prevalence 10%, sensitivity 80%, specificity 90%. What is the PPV?
80 ÷ (80 + 90) = 47%.
QUESTION 2
How many false positives are there?
90 (10% of the 900 without depression).
QUESTION 3
Why does a good test have a low PPV here?
Low prevalence: false positives from the large healthy group outnumber true positives.
QUESTION 4
Why use natural frequencies?
They are easier to understand and communicate than conditional percentages.
Diagnosis · Diagram 14

Venn and nested-circle diagrams

What it is. Venn and nested-circle diagrams show the same 2×2 information as overlapping groups.

  1. 1The overlap is the true positives — they have the disease and test positive.
  2. 2Disease circle outside the overlap: missed cases (false negatives).
  3. 3Test circle outside the overlap: false alarms (false positives).
  4. 4Everything outside both circles: true negatives.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the overlap?
True positives: diseased and test positive.
QUESTION 2
What is the PPV from the diagram?
Overlap ÷ whole test-positive circle = 80/170 = 47%.
QUESTION 3
Where are the false negatives?
The part of the disease circle outside the test circle (20).
Diagnosis · Diagram 15

Dot plot of test separation

What it is. A dot plot shows each person's test value in people with and without the condition, revealing how well a test separates them.

  1. 1Healthy people above the cut-off = false positives.
  2. 2Patients below the cut-off = false negatives.
  3. 3Moving the cut-off trades sensitivity against specificity.
  4. 4The more the two clouds overlap, the poorer the test — no cut-off separates them cleanly.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Where are the false positives?
Healthy people above the cut-off line.
QUESTION 2
What happens if you lower the cut-off?
Sensitivity rises and specificity falls.
QUESTION 3
What does heavy overlap between groups mean?
The test discriminates poorly; no cut-off separates them well (low AUC).

Communicating risk

Communicating risk · Diagram 16

Cates plot (icon array)

What it is. A Cates plot shows 100 people as faces to display risk with and without treatment.

  1. 1Red faces: people who have the bad outcome anyway.
  2. 2Gold faces: people saved by treatment — the absolute risk reduction.
  3. 3Grey faces: well either way. Most people do not benefit, which is why the NNT is above 1.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Calculate the ARR and NNT.
40% − 25% = 15%; NNT = 100/15 = 6.7, rounded up to 7.
QUESTION 2
What do the grey faces show?
People who stay well with or without treatment.
QUESTION 3
Why are icon arrays good for patients?
They show absolute risks in natural frequencies, which people understand better than relative risks.

Describing data

Describing data · Diagram 17

Box-and-whisker plot

What it is. A box plot shows the median, interquartile range, range and outliers of continuous data.

  1. 1Thick line in the box = median.
  2. 2Box edges = lower and upper quartiles; box height = interquartile range (middle 50%).
  3. 3Whiskers reach the furthest point within 1.5 × IQR of the box.
  4. 4Dots beyond the whiskers = outliers.
  5. 5A median off-centre in the box, or unequal whiskers, shows skew.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is the IQR for the placebo group?
19 − 12 = 7.
QUESTION 2
What do the circles above the whiskers represent?
Outliers beyond 1.5 × IQR from the box.
QUESTION 3
How does a box plot show skew?
The median sits off-centre and the whiskers are unequal.
QUESTION 4
What can a box plot not show?
The sample size and whether the data have two peaks.
Describing data · Diagram 18

Stem-and-leaf plot

What it is. A stem-and-leaf plot keeps every value while showing the shape of the data.

  1. 1Stem: the leading digit(s) — here tens of years.
  2. 2Leaves: the final digit, one per person — so every original value is kept.
  3. 3The key tells you how to read a value: always find it first.
  4. 4The longest row shows the mode class; the shape on its side is a histogram.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How many patients are there?
22 — count the leaves.
QUESTION 2
What is the mode?
34, which appears three times.
QUESTION 3
What is the median?
The average of the 11th and 12th values: 34.
QUESTION 4
What must you always read first?
The key (here 3 | 4 = 34).
Describing data · Diagram 19

Histogram, skew and averages

What it is. A histogram shows the distribution of continuous data in touching bars.

  1. 1Long tail to the right = positive (right) skew.
  2. 2Bars touch: continuous data. Area of each bar = number of people.
  3. 3In positive skew: mode < median < mean (the tail pulls the mean). Report median and IQR.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What type of skew is shown?
Positive (right) skew: a long tail to the right.
QUESTION 2
Put mean, median and mode in order.
Mode < median < mean.
QUESTION 3
Which average and spread should you report?
Median and interquartile range.
Describing data · Diagram 20

Normal distribution and z-scores

What it is. The normal distribution is a symmetrical bell-shaped curve fixed by its mean and standard deviation.

  1. 1Symmetrical bell: mean = median = mode.
  2. 2About 68% lie within 1 SD of the mean.
  3. 395% lie within 1.96 SD — this is the 95% reference range.
  4. 4Each tail beyond 1.96 SD holds 2.5%.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What proportion lies within ±1.96 SD?
95%.
QUESTION 2
An IQ of 130 (mean 100, SD 15). What is the z-score?
(130 − 100)/15 = +2: about the top 2.5%.
QUESTION 3
What proportion lies beyond 1 SD above the mean?
About 16%.
QUESTION 4
How is a 95% reference range calculated?
Mean ± 1.96 SD.
Describing data · Diagram 21

Error bars: SD, SE and CI

What it is. Error bars show either the spread of individuals (SD) or the precision of a mean (SE or confidence interval).

  1. 1SD bars: how spread out individual patients are.
  2. 2SE bars: how precisely the mean is estimated (SD ÷ √n). Always narrower than SD bars.
  3. 395% CI bars: mean ± 1.96 SE. Non-overlapping 95% CIs mean a significant difference; overlapping ones do not prove no difference.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
SD = 6 and n = 25. What is the SE?
6 ÷ √25 = 1.2.
QUESTION 2
Calculate the 95% CI.
12 ± 1.96 × 1.2 = 9.6 to 14.4.
QUESTION 3
Which bars describe individual patients?
SD bars.
QUESTION 4
Two 95% CIs overlap slightly. Is the difference definitely not significant?
No. Slight overlap can still be significant; test the difference directly.
Describing data · Diagram 22

Bar chart versus histogram

What it is. Bar charts show categories; histograms show continuous data.

  1. 1Bar chart: separate categories, gaps between bars, height = count.
  2. 2Histogram: continuous data in bins, bars touch, area = frequency (if bins are unequal, use frequency density).
  3. 3Ranked bar chart: order the bars by size so the eye compares them easily.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
How can you tell a histogram from a bar chart?
Histogram bars touch and represent continuous ranges; bar chart bars have gaps and represent categories.
QUESTION 2
In a histogram with unequal bins, what shows frequency?
The area of the bar (frequency density), not the height.
QUESTION 3
Why rank bars by size?
It makes comparison easier for the reader.
Describing data · Diagram 23

100% stacked bar chart

What it is. A 100% stacked bar compares the make-up of groups as percentages.

  1. 1Each bar is one group and always adds to 100%.
  2. 2Compare proportions, not numbers — the bars hide how many people each group had.
  3. 3Only the first segment shares a common baseline, so middle segments are harder to compare by eye.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which trust has the highest proportion waiting more than 6 weeks?
Trust C (40%).
QUESTION 2
What does the chart hide?
The number of patients in each trust.
QUESTION 3
Which segment is easiest to compare between bars?
The first segment, because it shares a common baseline.
Describing data · Diagram 24

Pareto chart

What it is. A Pareto chart shows categories in descending order with a cumulative percentage line.

  1. 1Bars sorted from largest to smallest category.
  2. 2Gold line: cumulative percentage (right axis).
  3. 3Where the line crosses 80% shows the 'vital few' causes to fix first (the 80:20 rule).

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which categories account for about 80% of incidents?
Missed doses, wrong time and omitted observations.
QUESTION 2
What is the cumulative line?
The running total as a percentage of all incidents.
QUESTION 3
How does it help quality improvement?
It shows the 'vital few' causes to tackle first.
Describing data · Diagram 25

Line chart

What it is. A line chart shows how a value changes over time.

  1. 1A dip or spike: ask what happened then (here 2020, the pandemic) before calling it a trend.
  2. 2Check the y-axis starts at zero and the units (rate per 100,000, not raw numbers).
  3. 3Compare slopes, not just levels: steeper line = faster change.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What should you ask about the 2020 dip?
What happened then (the pandemic), before calling it a trend.
QUESTION 2
Which group's admissions rose faster after 2020?
Under 25s: a steeper slope.
QUESTION 3
Why use rates per 100,000 instead of counts?
To allow for changes in population size.
Describing data · Diagram 26

Polar-area (Nightingale) chart

What it is. A polar-area chart shows values for categories such as months as wedges around a circle.

  1. 1Each wedge is one category; the angle is the same for all.
  2. 2The area of the wedge shows the value, so the radius grows with the square root — easy to over-read longer wedges.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which months have the most presentations?
March and December (red wedges).
QUESTION 2
What represents the value: radius or area?
Area. Radius grows with the square root of the value.
QUESTION 3
Why can these charts mislead?
Longer wedges look much bigger than their true values.
Describing data · Diagram 27

Scatter plot and regression

What it is. A scatter plot shows the relationship between two continuous variables; the regression line summarises it.

  1. 1Intercept (3): predicted outcome when x = 0.
  2. 2Slope (0.25): each extra week adds 0.25 months — the regression coefficient.
  3. 3r = strength and direction of a linear association; R² = proportion of variance explained (0.61 = 61%). Correlation is not causation.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Predict months to remission after 20 weeks untreated.
3 + 0.25 × 20 = 8 months.
QUESTION 2
What does R² = 0.61 mean?
61% of the variation in outcome is explained by duration of untreated psychosis.
QUESTION 3
Does r = 0.78 prove causation?
No. Confounding or reverse causation may explain it.
QUESTION 4
Why not predict beyond 50 weeks?
Extrapolation beyond the data may not hold.
Describing data · Diagram 28

Caterpillar plot

What it is. A caterpillar plot ranks units such as trusts by their estimate, each with a confidence interval.

  1. 1Red: the whole interval lies above the mean — significantly worse.
  2. 2Green: whole interval below the mean — significantly better.
  3. 3Most intervals cross the mean: their ranks are mostly chance. Ranking alone overstates differences.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
Which trusts are significantly worse than average?
Those whose whole interval lies above the national mean (red).
QUESTION 2
Why should rankings be treated with caution?
Most intervals overlap the mean, so ranks are largely due to chance.
QUESTION 3
What alternative display handles different unit sizes?
A funnel plot.
Describing data · Diagram 29

Population pyramid

What it is. A population pyramid shows the age and sex structure of a population.

  1. 1A wide base = many young people (growing population).
  2. 2More women in the oldest bands — longer female life expectancy.
  3. 3Age structure confounds crude rates, which is why rates are age-standardised.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What does a wide base mean?
Many young people: a growing population.
QUESTION 2
Why are there more women in the oldest band?
Longer life expectancy in women.
QUESTION 3
Why do pyramids matter for comparing death rates?
Different age structures confound crude rates, so rates are age-standardised.

Service quality and safety

Service quality and safety · Diagram 30

Run chart

What it is. A run chart plots a measure over time against the median to detect real change.

  1. 1A shift: 6 or more points in a row on one side of the median = a real (non-random) change.
  2. 2The centre line of a run chart is the median.
  3. 3Annotate when a change was made so you can link it to the shift. A trend = 5 or more points going up (or down) in a row.

Paper B questions on this diagram

QUESTION 1
What is a shift?
6 or more consecutive points on one side of the median.
QUESTION 2
What is a trend?
5 or more consecutive points all rising or all falling.
QUESTION 3
Does the run chart show the change worked?
Yes: after the change there is a shift of 8 points below the median.
Service quality and safety · Diagram 31

Statistical process control chart

What it is. An SPC chart plots a process over time with a mean and control limits at ±3 SD.

  1. 1A point outside a control limit = special-cause variation: investigate it.
  2. 2Control limits sit 3 SD (sigma) from the mean — not targets, but the voice of the process.
  3. 3Points bouncing inside the limits = common-cause variation: do not react to each one.

At the mean ± 3 sigma.

Tampering: it increases variation.

Health economics · Diagram 33

Cost-effectiveness plane

What it is. The cost-effectiveness plane plots the difference in cost against the difference in effect of a new treatment compared with current care.

  1. 1A point in the NE quadrant: the new treatment costs more and works better — judge its ICER.
  2. 2Dashed line: willingness-to-pay threshold. Points below it are cost-effective.
  3. 3Origin: the comparator (current care).

QUESTION 4

Dominated: it costs more and works less.

Inference · Diagram 34

Confidence intervals and the line of no effect

What it is. A confidence interval plot shows whether a result could be no effect, by checking where the interval sits relative to the line of no effect.

  1. 1CI excludes 1 (ratio): statistically significant at 5%.
  2. 2CI crosses 1: not significant — but look at the width: it may still include an important benefit (inconclusive).
  3. 3For differences (means, ARR) the line of no effect is 0, not 1.
