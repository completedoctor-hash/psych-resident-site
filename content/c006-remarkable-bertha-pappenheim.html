She is in every textbook of psychiatry, and almost always under a name that is not hers.

Anna O. The case that founded psychoanalysis. The young Viennese woman with paralyses, hallucinations, a cough that would not resolve, and periods when she could speak only English and not her native German.

Her doctor was Josef Breuer. Between 1880 and 1882 he sat with her, sometimes twice a day, and found that when she traced a symptom back to the moment it first appeared and described that moment fully, the symptom would sometimes lift.

She named the method herself. She called it the talking cure, and in lighter moods, chimney sweeping.

Freud, who never treated her, published the case with Breuer in Studies on Hysteria in 1895, and built a discipline on it.

The part the textbooks stop before

Her name was Bertha Pappenheim, and she was twenty-one when she fell ill, nursing her dying father.

Breuer's account implies a cure. It was not one. She spent years afterwards unwell, including periods in a sanatorium and a dependence on the morphine and chloral she had been given as treatment.

And then she recovered, and what she did next is why she belongs in this column rather than in a case series.

She became a writer, a translator, and one of the most significant social reformers in German-speaking Europe. She translated Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman into German. She ran an orphanage. In 1904 she founded the Jüdischer Frauenbund, the League of Jewish Women, which grew to fifty thousand members.

She travelled across Eastern Europe and the Middle East investigating the trafficking of women and girls, and published what she found, at a time when respectable society preferred it undiscussed. She founded a home for unmarried mothers and their children and ran it for decades. She is generally regarded as a founder of social work in Germany.

She never spoke publicly about her illness. She was firmly opposed to psychoanalysis being used on the children in her care.

She died in 1936, having been summoned for questioning by the Gestapo the year before. The home she built was destroyed in 1938.

In 1954 the West German government put her on a postage stamp, in a series called Helpers of Humanity.

Why she matters to a psychiatrist

Because the way her case is taught contains, in miniature, everything the profession has had to unlearn.

She is remembered by an initial, as a case rather than a person. Her recovery is credited to a treatment that did not in fact resolve her illness. Her own name for the method has survived while her name has not. And the forty years of remarkable work she did afterwards, which is the actual answer to what became of her, appears in almost no psychiatric textbook at all.

The single most important fact about Anna O is that she was Bertha Pappenheim, and that she got better and then built something.

The exam version Anna O, Bertha Pappenheim, 1859 to 1936. Treated by Josef Breuer 1880 to 1882. Published in Breuer and Freud, Studies on Hysteria, 1895. Presentation: paralyses, contractures, visual disturbance, aphasia with reversion to English, cough, and a fluctuating altered state. She coined the talking cure. The case introduced catharsis and the idea that symptoms carry meaning traceable to their origin. Breuer's abrupt ending of the treatment is the origin of the concept of countertransference. Modern retrospective diagnoses have included conversion disorder, temporal lobe epilepsy and tuberculous meningitis; none can be established.
