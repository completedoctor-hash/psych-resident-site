One The real mystery

A doctor stands in a hospital pantry in Melbourne, holding a jar of urine.

It is 1947. The pantry has been converted into a laboratory because nobody has given him a real one. There is no research grant, no assistant and no committee. There are guinea pigs, borrowed glassware, and a question he has been carrying since the war.

The question is this. Some of the men he nursed as a prisoner of war in Singapore became wildly, exhaustingly elated for weeks, and then recovered completely without anyone doing anything at all. Others simply stayed that way. If mania came and went like that, it behaved less like a character flaw and more like a fever.

And if it behaves like a fever, then something is in the blood.

He is about to test that idea in the least glamorous way imaginable, get the reasoning almost entirely wrong, and stumble onto the most effective drug psychiatry has ever had.

Two The people and the history

John Cade was an Australian psychiatrist who spent three and a half years in Changi as a prisoner of the Japanese, caring for other prisoners with almost nothing. He came home in 1945 convinced that mania was a poisoning by some normal product of the body, and melancholia its opposite.

His method was direct. He injected guinea pigs with urine from patients with mania, and with urine from ordinary people and from patients with other conditions. Urine from manic patients killed guinea pigs at lower doses. Something in it was more toxic.

He suspected urea. Urea turned out to be equally toxic in every sample, so it could not be the answer. He wondered whether uric acid was modifying the effect. Uric acid barely dissolves in water, so to get it into solution he reached for its most soluble salt, lithium urate.

Then came the part that changed everything, and it was an accident. To be sure he was measuring the right thing, he tested lithium on its own. The guinea pigs given lithium carbonate became placid. Not unconscious. Placid. Turn them on their backs and instead of the usual frantic righting they would lie there, awake, looking back at him.

His theory was wrong. Mania is not urate poisoning. But the wrong theory had put the right substance in his hand.

He took lithium himself first, to check it was safe. Then, on 29 March 1948, he gave it to a patient he described as a little wizened man of fifty-one who had been in a state of chronic manic excitement for five years. The man was the most troublesome patient in the hospital.

Within days the notes record him as more settled, tidier, less disinhibited, less distractible. Within weeks he was well enough to leave the ward. He went home. He went back to his old job.

Cade treated ten patients with mania and published the results in the Medical Journal of Australia in 1949, under the title Lithium salts in the treatment of psychotic excitement.

And then almost nothing happened, for twenty years.

Three things buried it. Cade was an unknown clinician publishing in a journal few outside Australia read. Lithium is an element that cannot be patented, so no company had any reason to promote it. And in the same year, 1949, lithium chloride was being sold in America as a salt substitute for people with heart disease, unmonitored and in large amounts. People died. Lithium acquired the reputation of a poison.

His own first patient did not have a happy ending either. He later stopped taking the treatment, became unwell again, and died in 1950.

The rescue came from Denmark. Mogens Schou, who had a brother with bipolar illness, ran the controlled trials through the 1950s and 1960s that turned an anecdote into evidence, including the demonstration that lithium prevented relapse rather than merely treating the acute episode. The United States licensed lithium for mania in 1970, twenty-one years after Cade's paper.

Three The science, made clear

Almost every drug in medicine is a molecule, built by chemists, shaped to fit a receptor like a key in a lock.

Lithium is not a molecule. It is an element. Third box on the periodic table, sitting directly above sodium. It is a bare ion, and the body cannot take it apart because there is nothing to take apart.

That single fact explains everything a doctor needs to know about it.

The liver is irrelevant. There is nothing to metabolise. All the interaction rules you learn for other psychiatric drugs, the enzymes, the inducers and inhibitors, do not apply here.

The kidney is everything. The kidney handles lithium as though it were sodium, because it looks like sodium. So anything that makes the body cling to salt makes it cling to lithium too. A water tablet. A blood pressure tablet. An anti-inflammatory. A stomach upset with vomiting. A hot week with too little to drink. Each of these can push a stable level into a dangerous one without the dose changing at all.

The window is narrow. Picture a dimmer switch where the useful range is a couple of millimetres wide. Below it, nothing. Above it, harm. That is why lithium is one of the few drugs in medicine where the blood level is checked routinely, and why the tremor matters: a fine tremor is the ordinary price of the dose, a coarse tremor is the alarm.

What does it actually do inside a cell? Honestly, we are still not certain. The leading accounts involve inhibition of inositol monophosphatase and of glycogen synthase kinase 3, which dampen the second messenger cascades that amplify signals inside neurons. The most useful way to say it to a patient is that lithium does not blunt feelings; it narrows the range through which they swing.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • Cade, Medical Journal of Australia, 1949. Ten patients with mania. First patient treated 29 March 1948.
  • Schou, from the 1950s. Controlled trials, and the establishment of prophylaxis as distinct from acute treatment.
  • United States approval, 1970.
  • Pharmacokinetics. Not protein bound. Not metabolised. Renal excretion, reabsorbed with sodium in the proximal tubule. Distributed in total body water.
  • Levels. Sampled 12 hours post dose. Roughly 0.6 to 1.0 mmol/L for maintenance, higher in acute mania, lower in older patients.
  • Raised by: thiazides, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, NSAIDs, dehydration, sodium depletion. Loop diuretics carry less risk than thiazides.
  • Common effects. Fine tremor, polyuria and thirst, weight gain, mild cognitive dulling, a metallic taste.
  • Long term. Hypothyroidism, commoner in middle-aged women. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Reduced renal concentrating ability. Hyperparathyroidism with hypercalcaemia.
  • Toxicity. Coarse tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, dysarthria, drowsiness, then seizures and coma. Withhold the drug, rehydrate, check the level urgently. Haemodialysis in severe cases.
  • Pregnancy. First trimester exposure is associated with Ebstein's anomaly. The relative risk is raised, the absolute risk remains low, and stopping abruptly carries its own substantial relapse risk.
  • Monitoring. Renal and thyroid function, and calcium, before starting and at intervals thereafter; levels more often after any dose change or intercurrent illness.
The fact most often left out Lithium is the only psychotropic with reasonably consistent evidence for a reduction in suicide, and the effect appears larger than can be explained by mood stabilisation alone. In a discussion about long term treatment in bipolar illness, that belongs in the conversation.
Fine or coarse Fine tremor is an expected dose related effect and can be helped by a beta blocker. Coarse tremor with vomiting and unsteadiness is toxicity until proved otherwise, whatever the reported level, because the clinical picture can run ahead of the blood result.

Five The compassionate truth

Bipolar illness is not a personality. It is a swing in a system that has lost its damping, and the swing is exhausting in both directions. People do not miss their mania because they are reckless. They miss it because the descent afterwards is unbearable.

Lithium is a demanding drug. It asks for blood tests, for care with fluids, for attention when other doctors prescribe. That is a genuine burden and it should be acknowledged rather than glossed over.

What it gives in return, for many people, is the thing that had been missing: a stretch of years in which their own plans hold. A psychiatrist prescribing it is not sedating anybody. They are trying to hand back the run of time in which a life can be built.

All of it traces to a man with no laboratory, no funding, a completely mistaken theory, and the discipline to notice a calm guinea pig.
