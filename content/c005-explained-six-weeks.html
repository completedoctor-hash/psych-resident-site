An antidepressant reaches its target within hours of the first tablet. Serotonin levels in the synapse rise on day one. Measurably, reliably, on day one.

And the person feels no better for a month.

That gap has puzzled psychiatry for sixty years, and it is the single strongest piece of evidence that the story we all learned about depression is incomplete.

What the old story said

Depression is low serotonin. The drug raises serotonin. The person gets better.

If that were the whole truth, antidepressants would work by Tuesday. They do not, so it is not.

A better picture

Think of a thermostat rather than a tap.

A serotonin neuron does not just release serotonin. It monitors how much it has released, using sensors on its own surface. These are autoreceptors, and their job is to keep output steady. If serotonin builds up outside the cell, the autoreceptors detect it and tell the neuron to fire less.

Now give the person an SSRI. The drug blocks reuptake, so serotonin accumulates in the synapse immediately. The autoreceptors see the surplus and do exactly what they were designed to do: they turn the neuron down.

You have raised the level and the thermostat has closed the valve. Net effect on transmission in the first days: close to nothing. This is also why some people feel worse in the first fortnight.

But the drug does not stop. Day after day the autoreceptors are exposed to a surplus they cannot correct, and eventually they do what any overworked sensor does. They stop responding. They downregulate.

The thermostat gives up. The valve opens. Transmission finally rises.

That takes about two to four weeks, which is very close to how long people actually wait.

And a third layer under that

Even receptor downregulation may not be the end of it. Sustained monoamine signalling increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which promotes the growth of new connections in the hippocampus, a region that shrinks in chronic depression and recovers with treatment.

On that account an antidepressant is not topping up a chemical at all. It is opening a window in which the brain can rewire, which is also a decent explanation for why medication and psychological therapy together beat either alone.

Why this is worth explaining to a patient

Because the single commonest reason people stop an antidepressant is that they gave it ten days.

Telling someone it takes four to six weeks sounds like an excuse. Telling them why it takes four to six weeks, that the brain has a thermostat and it has to be worn down before the change gets through, turns an unexplained wait into a process they can see themselves inside.

What to say in clinic The drug works from the first day. Your brain resists it for the first few weeks, because it is built to keep things where they are. The waiting is the brain letting go, not the drug failing.
The exam version Immediate reuptake inhibition, with somatodendritic 5-HT1A autoreceptor activation limiting net transmission. Therapeutic latency corresponds to autoreceptor desensitisation and downregulation over two to four weeks. Downstream: increased BDNF and hippocampal neurogenesis. The simple monoamine deficiency hypothesis does not account for the latency, and tryptophan depletion studies do not produce depression in never-depressed subjects.
