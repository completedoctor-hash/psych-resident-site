One The real mystery

Open on a moment. A person, a room, a year. Something happens that does not make sense.

Two or three more short paragraphs. Plain words. Nothing clinical yet.

End the section with the question the reader now cannot let go of. Why would the mind do that?

Two The people and the history

Introduce the real human beings. The pioneer, the dissenter, the patient. Give dates and give names.

Treat it as a detective case. Somebody had a theory. The theory was wrong or partly wrong. What they did next is the interesting part.

Say plainly where the popular version of the story is inaccurate, if it is. Correcting a myth is more memorable than repeating one.

Three The science, made clear

One metaphor, carried the whole way through. A messenger. A volume dial. A smoke alarm. A road network. Do not mix them.

Then say what the treatment actually does, what it cannot do, and what it costs the patient. Plain enough for a family, accurate enough for a colleague.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • Name, publication, year. What it showed.
  • The mechanism. Stated in exam language, not story language.
  • The numbers. Doses, ranges, thresholds, chromosomes.
  • The adverse effects. Common, then serious.
The differentiation that is examined The two things candidates confuse, and the single feature that separates them.
The fact most often left out Something true and useful that the textbooks bury.

Five The compassionate truth

Two or three sentences. What this condition is actually like from the inside.

Then what a psychiatrist really does here. Not control. Restoring agency, dignity and clarity to a brain that is struggling.

Close on the human being you opened with.
