One The real mystery

Half past four in the afternoon, a hot September day in Vermont, and a young foreman is packing gunpowder into a hole in the rock.

The tool in his hands is an iron bar, three feet seven inches long, an inch and a quarter thick, thirteen and a quarter pounds. He had it made to his own design. Someone speaks to him. He looks away.

The iron strikes the rock, a spark catches the powder, and the bar is fired out of the hole like a javelin. It goes in under his left cheekbone, passes behind his eye, through the front of his brain, and out through the top of his skull. It lands about eighty feet away, smeared.

He is thrown onto his back. His legs kick a few times.

Then he sits up and speaks.

Within a few minutes he is talking to the men around him. He walks, with a little help, to an ox cart. He sits upright for the three quarters of a mile back to the hotel where he is lodging. He gets out of the cart on his own and climbs a long flight of stairs.

When the doctor finally arrives, the patient greets him with a line the doctor never forgot: Doctor, here is business enough for you.

Two The people and the history

His name was Phineas Gage. He was twenty-five years old, a construction foreman on the Rutland and Burlington Railroad, and the date was 13 September 1848.

The doctor was John Martyn Harlow, a country physician who had qualified only a few years before. What he did next is why we know the case at all.

Harlow did not just save the man. He wrote him down. He described the wound, the fungus of brain that pushed out of the opening, the abscess, the weeks of delirium, the long slow recovery. And crucially, twenty years later, he came back and published a follow-up on what became of him.

Gage lost the sight in his left eye. He was left with a hole in his skull you could lay a finger in. And in the account Harlow gave in 1868, his character had changed. Where he had been shrewd, energetic and well liked, he was now, in Harlow's words, fitful and irreverent, impatient of restraint, obstinate yet capricious. His friends said he was no longer Gage.

That sentence made the case immortal. It became the founding story of the frontal lobe: the proof that a piece of brain could carry personality, and that you could remove it and leave the man walking and talking and unable to be himself.

And here is where the story has to be corrected, because the version everybody repeats is not the version the evidence supports.

The historian Malcolm Macmillan went back to the primary sources and found that almost everything after Harlow's original report was invention. Textbooks describe Gage as a drunken, aggressive, unemployable drifter. There is no contemporary evidence for any of it.

What the records actually show is a man who worked. He looked after horses. He drove a coach in Chile on a demanding route, which is skilled work requiring planning, timing and the management of passengers. One account describes him at that period as having no impairment whatever of his mental faculties.

The best modern reading is that the dramatic change was real, and was at its worst in the first few years, and that he then substantially recovered. Not a ruin. A man who reorganised himself around an injury.

He had seizures in his last years and died on 21 May 1860, aged thirty-six, of status epilepticus, near San Francisco. Harlow only learned of it years afterwards, and had the skull and the iron exhumed. Both are still at Harvard.

He is also, in Macmillan's phrase, a Rorschach inkblot. Every generation of neuroscience has projected its favourite theory of the frontal lobe onto him, and each has found what it was looking for.

Three The science, made clear

Think of the brain as a ship.

The engine room is the brainstem, and it never stops: breathing, heart rate, alertness. Nobody has to think about it.

Below decks there are specialists. One reads the charts, one handles the sails, one remembers every port the ship has visited. These are the sensory and motor areas, the language regions, the memory structures. Damage one and you lose a specific capacity. Damage Broca's area and speech becomes effortful. Damage the hippocampus and nothing new gets written down.

The frontal lobe is not a specialist. The frontal lobe is the captain.

The captain does not steer, navigate or haul rope. The captain decides which of those things happens now, and which waits, and whether the plan made this morning still makes sense at four in the afternoon. The captain holds the goal in mind while the work is being done, and stops the crew doing the first appealing thing that occurs to them.

Remove the captain and every specialist still works perfectly. The ship can sail. It just cannot decide where to go, or stick to a decision once made.

That is why the tests that catch frontal damage look so strange. Memory is fine. Intelligence is fine. Language is fine. But ask the person to keep sorting cards by colour and then quietly change the rule to shape, and they carry on sorting by colour long after the feedback tells them they are wrong. The specialist keeps working. Nobody is left to say stop.

The frontal lobe is also the last part of the brain to mature, which is not finished until the mid twenties. Every parent of a teenager has met a ship with an excellent crew and a captain still in training.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • The case. Phineas Gage, aged 25, 13 September 1848, near Cavendish, Vermont. Tamping iron, 3 ft 7 in, 1¼ in diameter, 13¼ lb. Entry under the left zygomatic arch, exit through the vertex. Predominantly left frontal damage, with orbitofrontal and probably ventromedial involvement.
  • The physician. John Martyn Harlow, initial report 1848, follow-up 1868. Survived 12 years; died 21 May 1860 of status epilepticus.
  • The historiography. Malcolm Macmillan's work shows the standard textbook account of permanent psychopathic deterioration is not supported by contemporary records. Gage held skilled employment as a coach driver in Chile.
The three frontal syndromes, which is what is actually examined Dorsolateral prefrontal: the dysexecutive syndrome. Poor planning, poor set shifting, perseveration, reduced verbal fluency, impaired working memory. Tests: Wisconsin Card Sorting, Trail Making B, verbal fluency, Tower of London. Orbitofrontal: disinhibition. Impulsivity, tactlessness, jocularity, utilisation behaviour, poor social judgement with intact formal cognition. Test: Go/No-Go, Hayling sentence completion. Anterior cingulate or medial frontal: apathy and akinetic mutism. Reduced spontaneous speech and movement, indifference, in the extreme a person who is awake but initiates nothing.
Two more that are worth having ready Frontal release signs: grasp, pout, palmomental, glabellar tap. Primitive reflexes that reappear when frontal inhibition is lost. Frontotemporal dementia is Gage's syndrome arriving slowly and from the inside. Early personality and behavioural change or progressive language failure, with memory relatively preserved at first. It is the commonest reason a person in their fifties is referred to psychiatry when the problem is degeneration.

Five The compassionate truth

Gage was not a monster and he was not a vegetable. He was a man who woke up with a hole in the part of himself that made plans, and who spent the rest of his short life working out how to live anyway. The evidence suggests he got a good way towards it.

Every psychiatrist meets people in his position. After a head injury, after a stroke, in the early years of frontotemporal dementia. Families say the same sentence Gage's friends said. He is not himself. They are describing something real and they are not being unkind.

The work is to say plainly what has happened, so the behaviour stops being read as selfishness or malice. It is to look for the treatable parts, the seizures, the pain, the depression, the drug that is making it worse. And it is to build the scaffolding, the routine, the prompts, the structure, that stands in for a captain who is no longer at the wheel.

Harlow's real contribution was not the surgery. It was that he came back twenty years later to find out what had become of his patient, and wrote it down honestly, including the parts that did not flatter anybody.

That is still the job.
