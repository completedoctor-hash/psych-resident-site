Open almost any revision text and you will find this sentence, or a version of it:

The repeated pairing of a conditioned (neutral) stimulus, for example food, with an unconditioned stimulus, for example a bell.

It is printed the wrong way round, and it is printed the wrong way round in a great many places.

What is actually the case

The dog salivates to food before anyone teaches it anything. That reflex is already installed. So:

  • Food is the unconditioned stimulus. Unconditioned means no learning required.
  • Salivation to food is the unconditioned response.
  • The bell is the neutral stimulus. Before training it produces nothing but a turn of the head.
  • Pair the bell with food often enough and the bell becomes the conditioned stimulus.
  • Salivation to the bell alone is the conditioned response.

The rule is short enough to hold. Conditioned means learned. The thing that had to be taught is the conditioned one. Food never had to be taught. The bell did.

Why the error survives

Because it is copied. A textbook prints it, a revision guide summarises the textbook, a question bank summarises the revision guide, and a candidate learns the summary of a summary of a mistake. Nobody goes back to Pavlov.

It survives particularly well in psychiatry because the terms are counter-intuitive in English. Unconditioned sounds like the weaker, vaguer, less important one. It is the opposite: unconditioned means built in.

What it costs

Every classical conditioning item in the paper. And the error propagates, because the whole vocabulary of behavioural psychology sits on top of it.

Stimulus generalisation, the fear spreading from the white rat to anything furry, cannot be described correctly if the two stimuli are the wrong way round. Nor can extinction, which is what happens when the conditioned stimulus is presented repeatedly without the unconditioned one. Nor can spontaneous recovery. Nor can the entire rationale of exposure therapy, which is deliberate extinction, deliberately performed.

The version to memorise Before learning: food, the unconditioned stimulus, produces salivation, the unconditioned response. The bell is neutral. During learning: bell, then food, repeatedly. After learning: bell, now the conditioned stimulus, produces salivation, now the conditioned response. Extinction: bell without food, repeatedly, and the response fades. Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, the faded response briefly returns.
One more that travels with it It was rarely a bell. Pavlov's laboratory used metronomes, buzzers, tones, whistles and a rotating disc. The bell is a translation artefact that has outlived every correction. It does not matter for the examination, and it is a good reminder of how durable a small error becomes once it is in print.
