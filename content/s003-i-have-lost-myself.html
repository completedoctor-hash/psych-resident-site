One The real mystery

A doctor puts a pen in a woman's hand and asks her to write her name.

She begins. Frau. Then she stops. Her hand is still on the paper, the pen is still moving, but the sentence she was inside of a moment ago has gone. She tries again. Frau. She stops again.

She looks up at him and says the thing that will still be quoted more than a century later.

"I have lost myself."

She is fifty-one years old. Not eighty-one. She knows something enormous is being taken from her, and she can feel it happening, which is the part that is hard to sit with.

Her doctor writes it down. He does not know it yet, but he is about to spend five years waiting for her to die so that he can look at her brain, and what he finds there will name a disease that will one day touch almost every family on earth.

Two The people and the history

Her name was Auguste Deter. She was a railway clerk's wife from Frankfurt, and her husband Karl had watched her change over a matter of months. She had become convinced he was unfaithful. She hid things and could not find them. She could not manage the cooking she had done for twenty years. She grew frightened of people she had known for years.

Karl could not afford private care and could no longer keep her safe. On 25 November 1901 she was admitted to the Frankfurt asylum, a building the locals called the Castle of the Insane.

There she met Alois Alzheimer, a physician in his late thirties. What he did next was not standard practice and is the reason we know her at all. He sat with her and wrote down what she said, in her own words, over and over, across weeks.

He would show her objects. She would name them and then lose them.

He asks what she is eating. She is eating cauliflower, and she says pork. He asks her surname. She gives her husband's first name. He asks her husband's name and she says, "I believe Auguste."

When he tries to examine her she says, "I will not be cut. I do not cut myself." Not confusion. Fear, and a reasonable one, expressed by a woman who can no longer follow what is being done to her.

Alzheimer left Frankfurt in 1902 for Heidelberg and then Munich, to work with Emil Kraepelin, the most powerful psychiatrist in Europe. He never forgot her. He arranged to be told when she died.

She died on 8 April 1906, aged fifty-five, of blood poisoning from bedsores, after nearly five years bedridden. Her brain and her notes were sent to Munich.

Alzheimer used a new silver staining method developed by Max Bielschowsky, which made structures visible that ordinary stains missed. Under the microscope he saw two things.

Dense clumps lying between the cells. And inside surviving neurons, tangled bundles of fibres where the ordinary internal scaffolding should have been.

On 3 November 1906 he presented the case to the 37th Assembly of Southwest German Psychiatrists in Tübingen.

The audience asked no questions. Not one. The chairman moved to the next speaker, who talked about compulsive masturbation. The local newspaper did not report it.

It was Kraepelin who understood what he had. In the eighth edition of his textbook in 1910, he gave the condition a name, and the name he chose was Alzheimer's.

There is a coda that matters. Auguste Deter's original file was lost for most of the twentieth century, and found again in Frankfurt in 1995. Her doctor's handwriting, and her own words, still legible.

Three The science, made clear

Think of a neuron as a small factory with a road network running through it.

The roads are microtubules, and along them the cell ships everything it needs from the body of the cell out to the endings, where messages are passed on. Holding those roads in shape is a protein called tau. Tau behaves like the sleepers under a railway track. It keeps the rails aligned.

In this illness tau becomes overloaded with phosphate groups. Overloaded tau lets go of the track. The rails collapse, the tau clumps together inside the cell, and what is left is the neurofibrillary tangle. The factory is still standing but nothing can be delivered anywhere, and eventually the cell dies.

Outside the cells, something different is happening. A large protein sits in the membrane like a post threaded through a wall. Enzymes trim it. Trimmed one way, the fragments are cleared harmlessly. Trimmed another way, by beta and then gamma secretase, the fragment is sticky. Sticky fragments clump into amyloid plaques, which sit between the neurons like debris on the floor of the workshop.

Two failures, then. Rubbish outside the cells, and collapsed roads inside them.

Where it begins matters as much as what it is. The damage starts in the medial temporal lobe, around the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, which is the part of the brain that writes new memories to disc. That is why the first thing to go is not the past but the present. A person can tell you in detail about 1974 and not what they said ten minutes ago. Nothing is being written down.

Separately, a small nucleus in the base of the forebrain, the nucleus basalis of Meynert, supplies acetylcholine to the whole cortex. It withers early. Acetylcholine is the chemical of attention and encoding, so as it drains away the ability to hold on to anything at all becomes harder.

This is exactly what the main drugs target, and it is worth being honest about what they do. A cholinesterase inhibitor blocks the enzyme that clears acetylcholine away, so the little that remains lingers longer in the gap. It is turning up the volume on a fading radio station. It can make a real difference to attention, engagement and daily function for a period. It does not repair the transmitter, and it does not stop the illness.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • Auguste Deter. Admitted 25 November 1901, aged 51. Died 8 April 1906, aged 55. Presented by Alzheimer at Tübingen, 3 November 1906. Named by Kraepelin in the eighth edition of his textbook, 1910.
  • Pathology. Extracellular amyloid plaques of beta-amyloid, from amyloid precursor protein cleaved by beta and gamma secretase. Intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau, which are paired helical filaments.
  • Distribution. Begins in medial temporal structures, entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, then spreads to association cortex. Medial temporal atrophy is the characteristic finding on structural MRI.
  • Neurochemistry. Early degeneration of the cholinergic nucleus basalis of Meynert, with cortical acetylcholine deficit.
  • Genetics, autosomal dominant early onset. APP on chromosome 21. PSEN1 on chromosome 14, the commonest. PSEN2 on chromosome 1.
  • Genetics, risk. APOE on chromosome 19; the e4 allele raises risk, e2 appears protective. It is a risk factor and not a diagnostic test.
  • Down syndrome. Trisomy 21 means an extra copy of APP, hence very high rates of early Alzheimer pathology.
  • Treatment. Cholinesterase inhibitors, donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine, in mild to moderate disease. Memantine, an uncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist, in moderate to severe disease or where cholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated.
The differentiations that are examined Alzheimer's: insidious amnestic onset, medial temporal atrophy. Dementia with Lewy bodies: fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder, and severe sensitivity to antipsychotics. Frontotemporal: early personality and behavioural change or language failure, with memory relatively preserved at first, and frontal atrophy. Vascular: stepwise course, focal signs, vascular risk factors. Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating over hours, impaired attention and altered consciousness, and a cause to be found.
The point about the drugs Cholinesterase inhibitors are symptomatic. They do not modify the disease. Saying so plainly to a family is more useful than allowing a hope that will be disappointed in a year, and it makes the genuine benefits easier to recognise when they appear.

Five The compassionate truth

The most important line in this whole story is not a scientific one. It is Auguste Deter saying she had lost herself, because it tells you she was still there to notice.

People with dementia are not absent. They are having to live in a present that will not stay still, and much of the distress that gets labelled as agitation is a reasonable response to being frightened, in pain, or asked to do something they can no longer follow.

A psychiatrist working in this field spends far less time prescribing than most people imagine. The work is finding the treatable thing underneath: the infection, the constipation, the deafness, the untreated pain, the drug that is making it worse. It is explaining to a family what is happening so they stop arguing with a memory that cannot be corrected. It is protecting the person's remaining choices for as long as those choices exist.

Alzheimer's contribution was not only the microscope. It was that he sat with a frightened woman for weeks and wrote down exactly what she said, in her own words, at a time when nobody thought her words were worth recording.

That is still the beginning of the work.
