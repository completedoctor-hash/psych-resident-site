One The real mystery

In 1790 a Quaker widow named Hannah Mills was taken into the York Asylum. She was grieving her husband. Her family lived too far away to visit often, so they asked local Quakers to look in on her instead.

They were turned away. Every time.

Six weeks later Hannah Mills was dead, and nobody would say how. Her friends were never allowed to see the room she died in.

Here is the strange part. The men running that asylum were not monsters. They were respectable physicians doing what the medicine of their day told them to do. Bleeding. Purging. Cold water. Chains, when a patient would not settle. They believed they were treating an illness.

So how does a whole profession end up certain it is helping, while doing something we would now call cruelty? And what does it take for one person to look at the same patient and see something entirely different?

Two The people and the history

Start with William Battie, because he is the first English doctor to say the quiet thing out loud.

In 1758 he published A Treatise on Madness. Battie ran St Luke's Hospital in London, opened in 1751 as a deliberate rival to Bethlem, and he wrote three things that were close to heresy at the time.

Madness is a disease, like gout or fever, and not a moral failing or a possession. Madness is often curable, and saying otherwise abandons patients who could get better. And, most awkwardly for his colleagues, management did more good than medicine. How you treat the person matters more than what you pour down them.

He also attacked the practice of letting the public in to look at patients. John Monro, physician at Bethlem and the second of four generations of Monros to hold that post, published a reply the same year. The argument between them is the first real public argument in English psychiatry about what a hospital is for.

Then William Tuke, a York tea merchant. When Hannah Mills died, the Quakers did not campaign or petition. They built their own place. The Retreat opened in 1796 with no chains, small numbers, gardens, work, tea with the staff, and the founding idea that a person in distress remains a person with reasons. They called it moral treatment, which in the language of the time meant psychological rather than ethical.

In Paris the same shift was happening, and the standard version of the story is not quite true. Philippe Pinel is remembered striking the chains off patients at the Bicêtre in 1793. Historians now credit much of that to Jean-Baptiste Pussin, the hospital's lay governor, who had been a patient there himself. Pinel learned it from him and wrote it down. The doctor got the painting; the former patient did the work.

Non-restraint spread. John Conolly abolished mechanical restraint entirely at the Hanwell Asylum in 1839, with more than eight hundred patients.

And then the movement ate itself. The asylums that began as sanctuaries grew. Hundreds of beds became thousands. By the middle of the twentieth century the reformers' buildings had become the thing the reformers were reacting against.

In 1959 Russell Barton named what he was seeing on those wards: institutional neurosis. Apathy, flatness, loss of initiative, a shuffling posture, no interest in the outside world. Not the illness. The building.

Two years later a sociologist called Erving Goffman published Asylums, based on a year spent inside a Washington hospital as a supposed member of staff. He described the total institution: a place where you sleep, work and play in one location, under one authority, alongside everyone else, to a timetable set by somebody else. He showed how such places quietly strip away the self through batch living and a series of small humiliations he called the mortification of self. Your clothes go. Your name shortens. Your history is rewritten in a file you cannot read.

In the same year, 1961, the Minister of Health Enoch Powell stood up and promised to demolish the asylums, in what became known as the water tower speech. The great institutions came down over the following forty years.

Three The science, made clear

What Battie and Tuke understood without the vocabulary is something we can now describe fairly precisely.

A frightened brain cannot learn. Deep in the temporal lobe sits the amygdala, which behaves like a smoke alarm. It does not think; it reacts, and it reacts before the thinking parts of the brain have caught up. Restrain someone, and the alarm screams. Screaming alarms shut down the frontal lobe, the part that plans, waits and reasons. So restraint does not calm a person down. It removes the very machinery they would need in order to calm down.

Predictability is medicine. The brain is a prediction machine. It is constantly guessing what happens next and updating when it is wrong. In a place where meals, faces and rules keep changing, every moment is an error signal, and the alarm never gets to switch off. A ward with a rhythm is not just pleasant. It reduces the load on a system already working too hard.

Doing nothing has a cost. Give a person nothing to decide, nowhere to go and no role, and within months you will see flat face, slow movement, no plans and no interest. It looks exactly like the negative symptoms of schizophrenia, which is why it fooled a generation of doctors. It was the ward.

And the reason Goffman still matters is that a total institution is not a building. It is a set of conditions. Those conditions can appear in a modern unit with soft furnishings, and can be absent from an old one. The test is whether the person still has choices.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • Battie, A Treatise on Madness, 1758. Madness as disease, often curable, management above medicine. Monro's Remarks published in reply the same year.
  • The Retreat, York, 1796. Founded by William Tuke following the death of Hannah Mills in the York Asylum in 1790. The origin of moral treatment, meaning psychological rather than ethical.
  • Pinel and Pussin, Bicêtre, 1793. The unchaining is conventionally attributed to Pinel; Jean-Baptiste Pussin, lay governor and former patient, is now regarded as central.
  • Conolly, Hanwell, 1839. Complete abolition of mechanical restraint in a large asylum.
  • Barton, Institutional Neurosis, 1959. Apathy, loss of initiative, submissiveness, flattened affect, stooped posture, loss of interest in the outside world.
  • Goffman, Asylums, 1961. The total institution. Key terms: batch living, binary management (staff and inmates as two separate worlds), mortification of self, the moral career of the patient.
  • Powell's water tower speech, 1961. Ministerial commitment to closing the asylums, opening the era of care in the community.
The differentiation that is examined Institutional neurosis is caused by the environment and improves when the environment changes: occupation, contact with the outside, choice, a role. Negative symptoms of schizophrenia are part of the illness and persist despite environmental change. They look alike at the bedside. The distinguishing question is not what the patient looks like, but what happens when the surroundings alter.
Also worth having ready Moral treatment predates any effective drug by roughly 150 years. Any argument that improvement in psychiatry began with pharmacology fails on that fact alone.

Five The compassionate truth

Hannah Mills died because the people who cared about her were kept outside the door. Everything good that came afterwards, the open wards, the gardens, the visiting, the closure of the great hospitals, began with a handful of people insisting that a distressed person is still a person, and that families are not the problem.

A psychiatrist is not there to contain someone. The job is to lower the alarm enough that the person can think again, and then hand the decisions back. Restraint is what remains when everything else has been tried and has failed, and every time it is used it should be recorded as a failure of something, not as a treatment.

The lesson from two hundred years is uncomfortably simple. Kindness was not the soft alternative to the science. Kindness turned out to be the science, arriving early.
