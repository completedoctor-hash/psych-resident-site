One The real mystery

A French naval surgeon is trying to stop his patients dying of shock on the operating table.

It is 1951. His idea is that the body's own panic response is doing much of the damage, so he has been mixing drugs to switch that response off before he cuts. One of the compounds he is given by a chemical company is a failed antihistamine.

He gives it, and something happens that has nothing to do with surgery.

The patients are awake. They can talk. They answer questions correctly. But they have stopped minding. They lie there in what he calls a state of euphoric quietude, entirely uninterested in the operation about to be performed on them.

He is a surgeon. He could have written it up as a useful anaesthetic adjunct and moved on.

Instead he went and found the psychiatrists, and would not leave them alone until they tried it.

Two The people and the history

The compound was chlorpromazine, synthesised in December 1950 by Paul Charpentier at Rhône-Poulenc in Paris, from a family of dyes that had been sitting in the chemical literature since the nineteenth century.

The surgeon was Henri Laborit, and his description of what he saw is the reason any of this happened. He did not say the drug sedated people. He said it produced a disinterest without loss of consciousness. He pushed his psychiatric colleagues to try it on agitated patients.

At the Sainte-Anne hospital in Paris, Jean Delay and Pierre Deniker did. In 1952 they published a series of patients with mania and with psychosis, and reported something nobody had seen before: not sedation, but the symptoms themselves receding.

What happened next is one of the fastest changes in the history of medicine. Within three years chlorpromazine was in use across Europe and North America. Within a decade the asylum population, which had risen without interruption for a century, began to fall and did not stop.

Three cautions belong in the same paragraph, because the triumphant version of this story is dishonest.

The first is that the drug did not empty the asylums by itself. Moral treatment, the therapeutic community, the 1959 Mental Health Act, changing social attitudes and government housing policy all pushed in the same direction. Enoch Powell's water tower speech in 1961 promised the demolition regardless.

The second is what the wards were emptied into. A great many people left long stay hospitals for accommodation that did not exist, and the phrase care in the community became, for a period, a description of neglect.

The third is the movement disorder. Within a couple of years of chlorpromazine's arrival, clinicians were describing parkinsonism, acute dystonia and akathisia. Then, in 1957, a French group described something worse: involuntary movements of the face and tongue that persisted after the drug was stopped. Tardive dyskinesia. Psychiatry had bought a genuine treatment and had not yet counted the bill.

The theory arrived afterwards, and only because of the drug. Chlorpromazine blocked dopamine receptors, and antipsychotic potency turned out to track almost exactly with how tightly a drug bound to the D2 receptor. From that came the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia, which is reasoning backwards from a medicine to a mechanism. It has been the most productive wrong-in-detail idea psychiatry has ever had.

Three The science, made clear

Dopamine is not one thing doing one job. It runs along four separate roads, and each road ends somewhere different.

The drug does not know this. Give someone an antipsychotic and it blocks dopamine on every road at once. Everything good and everything bad about these medicines follows from that single fact.

Road one, the mesolimbic pathway. This is the road that assigns importance. It is what makes a thing feel like it matters. In psychosis it is overactive, and so ordinary things arrive drenched in a significance they have not earned: the car outside means something, the newsreader is talking about you. Turn the volume down on this road and the world stops shouting. This is the treatment.

Road two, the mesocortical pathway. This one runs to the front of the brain and it is thought to be running low already, which is part of why people become flat, withdrawn and slow to think. Turn it down further and you make that worse. This is why a drug can clear the voices and leave a person emptied out.

Road three, the nigrostriatal pathway. Movement. Block about eighty per cent of the receptors here and you get the stiffness and tremor of Parkinson's disease, only produced deliberately. Block them for years and the road adapts by growing more receptors, becomes hypersensitive, and you get the writhing movements of tardive dyskinesia.

Road four, the tuberoinfundibular pathway. A short road from the hypothalamus to the pituitary whose only job is to hold prolactin down. Block it and prolactin rises, and you get milk production, missed periods and sexual difficulties in people who were never warned.

The useful range is narrow. Below about sixty five per cent occupancy of D2 receptors there is little antipsychotic effect. Above about eighty per cent the movement problems begin. The whole art of prescribing these drugs is staying in that gap, and it is why raising the dose is so rarely the answer.

The newer drugs did not solve the problem so much as move it. They block serotonin as well as dopamine, which loosens the grip on the movement road, and the price is metabolic: weight, blood sugar, cholesterol. There is no free version.

Four The doctor's notebook

  • Chlorpromazine. Synthesised by Paul Charpentier at Rhône-Poulenc, December 1950. Observed by Henri Laborit in surgical patients, 1951. First psychiatric series by Delay and Deniker at Sainte-Anne, 1952.
  • The dopamine hypothesis. Derived from the observation that clinical antipsychotic potency correlates with D2 receptor affinity. Note the direction of inference: from drug to theory, not theory to drug.
  • Occupancy. Antipsychotic effect from roughly 65 per cent D2 occupancy; extrapyramidal effects from roughly 80 per cent.
  • The four pathways. Mesolimbic, therapeutic. Mesocortical, negative and cognitive symptoms worsened. Nigrostriatal, extrapyramidal effects acutely and tardive dyskinesia chronically. Tuberoinfundibular, hyperprolactinaemia.
  • Chlorpromazine's own profile. Low potency, so relatively fewer extrapyramidal effects and more sedation, postural hypotension from alpha-1 blockade, antimuscarinic effects, photosensitivity and blue-grey skin discolouration with long term use, and a lowered seizure threshold.
The movement disorders, and when each one appears Acute dystonia: hours to days. Oculogyric crisis, torticollis, laryngeal spasm. Frightening, and rapidly reversed by an anticholinergic such as procyclidine. Akathisia: days to weeks. An inner restlessness that cannot be sat still through. Frequently misread as agitation, and then treated by raising the dose, which makes it worse. Review the dose first; propranolol or a benzodiazepine after that. Parkinsonism: weeks to months. Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor. Tardive dyskinesia: months to years. Orofacial choreoathetoid movements. May be irreversible, and may worsen transiently on withdrawal. Consider clozapine; VMAT2 inhibitors where available.
The honest sentence about deinstitutionalisation Chlorpromazine made community care possible. It did not cause it. Policy, economics, the 1959 Act and the reform movement were all pushing before the drug arrived, and the bed numbers in some countries had begun to fall already. Any answer that credits the drug alone is a story rather than a history.

Five The compassionate truth

Psychosis is not a fantasy world. It is this world, with the volume of significance turned up so far that everything seems to mean something, and the meanings will not stop arriving. It is exhausting and it is frightening, and people who have recovered describe the relief of the noise dropping away far more often than they describe a loss of anything.

But these drugs cost something, and the profession has not always been straight about that. Weight gain that changes how a person is looked at. Sexual side effects nobody asked about. Stiffness that makes someone look ill in the street. Patients who stop taking their medication are usually not lacking insight. They are making a trade, and often they have not been given accurate information about either side of it.

A psychiatrist prescribing an antipsychotic is not switching a person off. The aim is to turn down the noise enough that they can hear themselves think, at the lowest dose that does it, having said out loud what it might cost and having asked, at every review, whether the trade is still worth it.

All of it comes from a surgeon who noticed that his patients had stopped minding, and who thought that was interesting enough to tell somebody about.
