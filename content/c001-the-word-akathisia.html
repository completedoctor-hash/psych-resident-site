From the Greek. A, not. Kathizein, to sit down.

Not able to sit down. That is the whole word, and it is a remarkably exact description of what the person is experiencing.

Akathisia is not anxiety, though it is constantly mistaken for it. It is not agitation, though it looks like it from across a room. It is a physical restlessness that lives in the legs, an inability to keep them still that the person can describe precisely and cannot do anything about. People rock, pace, cross and uncross their legs, march on the spot while waiting for a kettle.

They will often say they feel like they are going to jump out of their own skin.

It is caused by antipsychotics, and by some antidepressants, and it usually appears within the first weeks of starting or of a dose increase.

Here is why the word matters. Akathisia is routinely read as the illness getting worse, and the dose goes up, and the person gets worse still. It is one of the most reliable ways to make a patient stop trusting their psychiatrist, and it is entirely avoidable by asking one question.

Not "are you feeling restless?" That gets a yes from almost anybody on a psychiatric ward.

Ask: "Is it in your legs?"

Akathisia says yes. Anxiety does not.
