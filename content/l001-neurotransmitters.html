Almost everything else in neuroscience hangs off this one topic. Lesion questions, drug side-effect questions, movement disorder questions and imaging questions all resolve, in the end, to a transmitter and a receptor. Learn this properly and a large part of the paper stops being memory and starts being reasoning.

There are only four questions to ask about any transmitter. Where is it made, where does it travel, how is it switched off, and what shape is its receptor. Answer those four for six transmitters and you have the foundation.

1. Where each one is made

Every transmitter starts as something ordinary and is converted, step by step, by named enzymes. The examination loves the enzymes, because each one is a single unambiguous fact.

The two facts to carry out of this section Tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting step for the catecholamines. Tryptophan hydroxylase is the rate-limiting step for serotonin. The hydroxylase is always the bottleneck; the decarboxylase is always the fast second step, and it is the same enzyme for dopamine and serotonin.

2. Where each one travels

Most transmitters come from one small nucleus and project almost everywhere. That is why a tiny lesion can produce a diffuse syndrome.

  • Serotonin comes from the raphe nuclei in the brainstem and projects up to cortex and down to spinal cord. Mood, sleep, appetite, and pain.
  • Noradrenaline comes from the locus coeruleus in the pons. Arousal, attention, and the stress response.
  • Dopamine comes from the substantia nigra and the ventral tegmental area in the midbrain, along four pathways worth naming: nigrostriatal for movement, mesolimbic for reward, mesocortical for cognition, tuberoinfundibular for prolactin.
  • Acetylcholine comes from the nucleus basalis of Meynert in the basal forebrain, which is the nucleus that degenerates in Alzheimer's disease.
  • Glutamate and GABA have no single nucleus. Glutamate is the transmitter of the cortical pyramidal neurons; GABA is the transmitter of interneurons everywhere. They are the background against which everything else is a modulation.
A way to hold it Four small nuclei light up the whole brain: raphe for serotonin, locus coeruleus for noradrenaline, substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area for dopamine, nucleus basalis for acetylcholine. Glutamate and GABA are not nuclei at all. They are the wiring itself.

3. How each one is switched off

A synapse is only useful if the signal stops. There are three ways to stop it, and which one applies is a common question.

  1. Taken back up. The monoamines are mostly recaptured by a transporter. SERT for serotonin, NET for noradrenaline, DAT for dopamine. This is what antidepressants and cocaine act on.
  2. Broken down by an enzyme. Acetylcholine is the clean example: acetylcholinesterase sits right in the cleft and destroys it almost immediately.
  3. Diffusion away. Real but rarely the answer.

For the monoamines, two enzymes do the destroying, and the difference between them is a compartment.

The compartment rule, which settles a recurring confusion MAO is inside the neuron, in presynaptic mitochondria. COMT is outside the neuron, in the synaptic cleft and the periphery. When a question says presynaptic, or inside, the answer is MAO. When it says synaptic cleft, or extracellular, the answer is COMT. Both are correct enzymes for noradrenaline; the stem tells you which one it wants.

The metabolites are worth knowing as a set, because they appear as answers in their own right. Dopamine leaves homovanillic acid. Noradrenaline leaves MHPG centrally and vanillylmandelic acid peripherally. Serotonin leaves 5-HIAA.

4. What shape the receptor is

Every receptor in this topic is one of two shapes, and knowing which tells you how fast it acts.

IonotropicMetabotropic
What it isA channel that the transmitter opens directlyA receptor coupled to a G protein, working through a second messenger
SpeedMillisecondsHundreds of milliseconds to seconds
ExamplesGABA-A, NMDA, AMPA, kainate, nicotinic, 5-HT3, glycineGABA-B, all dopamine receptors, all serotonin receptors except 5-HT3, muscarinic, cannabinoid CB1, opioid

Three exceptions carry most of the marks. GABA-A is ionotropic but GABA-B is metabotropic. 5-HT3 is the only ionotropic serotonin receptor, which is why it is the one that opens a channel and why blocking it stops nausea. And every G protein coupled receptor crosses the membrane seven times, which is asked as a bare number.

The shapes, since they are asked directly

  • Pentamers: GABA-A, nicotinic, 5-HT3, glycine. Five subunits round a pore.
  • Tetramers: the ionotropic glutamate receptors, meaning NMDA, AMPA and kainate.
  • Dimers: the metabotropic glutamate receptors.

The one receptor worth knowing in detail

The NMDA receptor is asked about more than any other, because it sits at the junction of learning, psychosis and anaesthesia.

  • It is a glutamate receptor and it is ionotropic, a tetramer.
  • Glutamate alone will not open it. It needs a co-agonist, either glycine or D-serine, bound at the same time. This is the single most reliably examined fact in the topic.
  • When it opens it admits calcium, as well as sodium. Calcium entry is what makes it the receptor of synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation, and therefore of memory.
  • Block it non-competitively and you get dissociation, which is ketamine.
In one line Four small nuclei supply the monoamines, two enzymes switch them off in two different compartments, and every receptor is either a channel that opens in milliseconds or a G protein that works in seconds. Everything else in this topic is detail hung on that frame.
