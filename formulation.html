Formulation: working out what is going on.

Anyone can list symptoms. A formulation explains them: what kind of problem this is, why it has happened to this person now, what it means to them, and what you will do about it. Here you learn to work it out step by step: the history, the mental state, the questions that matter in each specialty, risk, cognition and physical examination, then more than thirty real presentations worked through.

Working out what is going on, in five steps

Every formulation, from your first night on call to a complex ward round, follows the same five steps. The rest of this page teaches each one.

1
Hear the story

Let them talk, then build a timeline.

e.g. "It started when my mum went into hospital in March."
2
Describe every domain

Mood, anxiety, perception, thought, cognition, behaviour, body, self.

e.g. Low mood, plus panic, plus drinking: three domains, not one.
3
Check the body first

Infection, drugs, alcohol, medicines, thyroid, brain.

e.g. Sudden confusion at 80: a urine infection, not dementia.
4
Ask: why this person, why now?

What made them vulnerable, what set it off, what keeps it going, what protects.

e.g. Bullied at school (vulnerable), lost job (trigger), isolated (keeps it going).
5
Plan against each cause

Every changeable cause gets an action, a person and a date.

e.g. Isolated → peer group referral by Friday, care coordinator.
Chapter 1

Two doctors, one patient

Two careful doctors can see the same person and write almost identical summaries. Their formulations can still be completely different, because a summary describes and a formulation explains. Read the same patient twice.

Mr Daniel Okafor, 34. Brought by his sister after three weeks of barely sleeping, eating little and staying in his room. He lost his job as a delivery driver two months ago. He has been drinking half a bottle of vodka a night. He says there is "no point" and that people at the depot "know what I did".

34-year-old man, recently unemployed. Three weeks of low mood, insomnia, reduced appetite and social withdrawal. Drinking about 15 units a night. Expresses hopelessness. Possible ideas of reference about former colleagues. No previous psychiatric contact. Lives alone; sister supportive.

Accurate, tidy and useful for a handover. But it does not tell the next doctor what is going on, why now, or what to do first.

What kind of problem is this? Most likely a depressive episode, moderate to severe, with alcohol misuse. Two things must be ruled out before settling on that: psychotic features (his comment about colleagues) and an organic contribution from alcohol (withdrawal, intoxication, head injury).

Why him, why now? His father had depression (predisposing). Losing his job removed his structure, income and identity (precipitating). Drinking to sleep, staying in his room and shame about the dismissal are keeping it going (perpetuating). His sister, and the fact that he came, are on his side (protective).

What does it mean to him? He describes the dismissal as proof he lets everyone down, which links to being the eldest son who was expected to provide. The "they know what I did" idea may be guilt rather than a delusion. Finding out which is the key question for today.

So what do we do? Assess suicide risk properly today, given hopelessness, alcohol, recent loss and living alone. Check for alcohol withdrawal and do bloods. Clarify the belief about colleagues. Agree a plan for the drinking, treat the depression and involve his sister with his consent.

Same facts. Now the next doctor knows what you think, what you are unsure of and what happens first.

A summary is a photograph. A formulation is a map with a route on it.
Chapter 2

The three lenses

Every good formulation looks through three lenses at once. Psychiatry has argued about each of them for a century; together they still give the clearest picture of a person in trouble.

1

What kind of problem?

The level. Is this a physical illness showing itself in the mind, a major shift such as psychosis or severe mood disorder, or a reaction and a variation of ordinary human nature? Getting the level right stops you treating delirium as depression.

2

Why this person, why now?

The causes. Predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating and protective factors, each looked for in the body, the mind and the person's world. The best causes to name are the ones you can change.

3

What does it mean?

The understanding. Why this event hit this person so hard, what sense they make of it, and how their character shaped the reaction. This is what makes a patient feel understood rather than processed.

Lens 1 in detail: the three levels tap a level

Delirium, dementia, intoxication and withdrawal, epilepsy, encephalitis, thyroid and steroid effects, head injury. Anything seen in the other levels can appear here too: low mood, anxiety, hallucinations, even full psychosis. Some things only happen here: disorientation, clouding of consciousness, confabulation, seizures. If you see those, think Level I first.

The rule that follows: always rule out a Level I cause before you settle on a Level II or III explanation. "Organic until proven otherwise" is this rule in four words.

Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, severe and psychotic depression. These are clear changes from the person's usual self: passivity experiences, primary delusions, flight of ideas, pervasive melancholia. They do not arise from personality or stress alone.

Diagnose here only once a Level I cause has been reasonably excluded. Anxiety or personality difficulties may coexist, but the Level II diagnosis leads.

Common anxiety and depression, reactions to stress and trauma, personality, neurodevelopmental differences. These merge into ordinary life. That does not make them mild: they can be severe and disabling. Here the causes and the meaning do most of the work, and the diagnosis less.

At this level, the 5Ps and the person's own story usually matter more than the precise label.

Where the idea comes from. The levels go back to the German clinical tradition of Jaspers and Schneider. The cause-based grid grew out of Adolf Meyer's life-chart method and George Engel's 1977 biopsychosocial model, later sharpened at the Maudsley into the "4P" grid. A 2023 paper by Gareth Owen in Psychological Medicine argues that psychiatry should bring all three lenses back together. This course follows that approach.
Chapter 3

The history: collecting what the formulation will need

The history is not a form to complete. Each heading is there because it feeds one of the three lenses. Open each heading to see what to ask and why it matters.

Adjust the history to the person in front of you

Older adults

Screen for physical causes every time: infection, pain, constipation, dehydration, new or changed medicines. Ask about memory, getting lost, word-finding, managing money and tasks, hallucinations and stiffness. Risks widen: falls, driving, cooking and gas, wandering, financial exploitation, a carer who is close to breaking point.

Learning disability

Use the carer and the person together. Ask what is different from their usual baseline: sleep, appetite, behaviour, communication, skills lost. Pain and physical illness often show up as behaviour change. Check epilepsy, thyroid, constipation, dental pain, and changes in staff or routine.

Children and young people

Pregnancy, birth and developmental milestones. School, friendships, bullying and online life. Who lives at home and who has parental responsibility. Safeguarding is always on the list. See the young person alone for part of it.

Forensic

The offence in detail, the person's own account and the official one. Mental state at the time. Pattern of past violence, weapons, victims, substances. Attitude to victims and to treatment. Who is at risk now and how that risk could be managed.

Chapter 4

The mental state: what you can see and hear today

The history tells you the story so far. The mental state examination is a cross-section of today. It is the evidence you hand to the next doctor, so describe what you saw rather than giving a verdict.

Describe, don't label. "Laughing to himself and pausing mid-sentence as if listening" is evidence. "Responding to voices" is a conclusion. Give the evidence first and add the conclusion after it.

Chapter 7

Is the history and mental state the same as a formulation?

No. They come before it and feed it, but they are different jobs. The history and mental state collect the facts. The formulation decides which facts matter, how they connect and what to do.

The history and mental state are your evidence. The formulation is the case you make from it.
Chapter 8

Build a 5P grid

The workhorse of everyday formulation is a grid: four kinds of cause across three domains. Here it is for Mr Okafor from Chapter 1. Tap each cell to reveal it, then press Show what we do about each to see how every cause becomes an action.

Three tests for a good grid. Is each factor actually present in this person, not just common in people like them? Is it a strong factor, not a trivial one? Does the problem change when the factor changes? A grid that passes all three points straight to a plan.
Chapter 9

Eleven ways to formulate, and when to use each

The biopsychosocial grid is where you start. Other services use other frameworks, including the habit loop for anything that keeps repeating, and you will meet all of them in your training. Tap a card to see when it helps.

The habit loop. A cue (a time, place, feeling or person) triggers a routine (drinking, checking, self-harm, avoiding), which brings a reward (relief, calm, belonging). Each turn of the loop strengthens it. This is often the clearest way to explain a perpetuating factor to a patient, and the way to break it: remove or change the cue, add friction to the routine, or find a new routine that gives a similar reward.

Chapter 11

On the ward: formulation in everyday work

These are the moments in a working week when you will be asked to work out what is going on, out loud, under time pressure. Each one uses everything above.

Gathering what matters
  • A perplexed 21-year-old brought in "not eating": build the timeline and list what you still don't know.
  • A complex readmission: find the baseline and the change from baseline.
  • The collateral history that changes your diagnosis.
Reasoning in practice
  • Three plausible diagnoses: the questions that separate mood, personality and psychosis.
  • Organic until proven otherwise: when to push for investigations.
  • Two conditions at once: psychosis with substance use, depression with PTSD.
The 5P formulation
  • Presenting a new patient in three minutes on the ward round, with a formulation.
  • Formulating the effects of alcohol.
  • Formulation in personality difficulties, trauma and neurodevelopmental conditions.
Risk formulation
  • After self-harm: long-standing versus acute risk, what changed today, and the safety plan.
  • Writing a risk formulation a stranger could act on at 3 am.
Sharing it
  • Explaining your understanding to the patient, in their words.
  • Explaining it to relatives, within confidentiality.
  • Presenting it at a CPA meeting or to the team.

Now do it for real.

Take your next clerking and write three sentences under Impression: what kind of problem it is, why this person and why now, and what you will do. Show it to your supervisor. That is a formulation.
